# Best Practices: Character Psychologist

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality character psychological analysis, behavioral consistency diagnosis, and emotional authenticity work.

## Design Principles

- **Behavior Is the Evidence**: Psychological analysis of fictional characters must be grounded in what they do, not what they say about themselves or what other characters say about them. The analysis is empirical, drawn from the text.
- **Consistency Is the Test**: A psychologically coherent character behaves consistently with their established psychology even when the narrative doesn't require it. Inconsistency is a diagnostic signal worth investigating.
- **Trauma Has Specific Signatures**: Psychological wounds produce specific, recognizable behavioral patterns — hypervigilance, avoidance, rumination, disorganized attachment, etc. These patterns should be traceable in the character's behavior.
- **Multiple Frameworks, Single Character**: No single psychological framework captures the full complexity of a character. Apply multiple diagnostic lenses (attachment theory, trauma responses, defense mechanisms, cognitive schemas) and triangulate.
- **Distinguish Defense from Character**: Defense mechanisms are responses to threat, not personality traits. A character who appears cold may be employing emotional avoidance as a defense. Distinguishing the defense from the underlying vulnerability deepens the analysis.
- **Emotional Authenticity Over Emotional Logic**: Real psychological responses are not always logically proportionate to their triggers. A character who overreacts to a small slight is demonstrating wound activation, not irrationality.
- **Growth Has Prerequisites**: Meaningful psychological change requires specific conditions — safety, relationship, confrontation of the wound, and choice. Analyzing whether these conditions are present helps evaluate arc plausibility.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Attachment Theory Application**: Classify the character's attachment style (secure, anxious-preoccupied, dismissive-avoidant, fearful-avoidant) based on their relational behavior. Attachment style predicts specific relationship patterns, communication styles, and responses to intimacy and conflict.
- **Defense Mechanism Analysis**: Identify the character's primary defense mechanisms (repression, projection, rationalization, intellectualization, reaction formation, dissociation) and map them to specific behavioral evidence in the text.
- **Cognitive Schema Identification**: Identify core beliefs the character holds about themselves and the world ("I am unlovable," "People always leave," "I must be in control") and trace how these schemas generate specific behavioral patterns.
- **Trauma Response Taxonomy**: Classify the character's responses to stress using the four F model (fight, flight, freeze, fawn) and identify the conditions that activate each response mode.
- **Maslow's Hierarchy as Diagnostic**: Map where the character's unmet needs fall on the hierarchy (safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization) to understand the specific deprivation driving their behavior.
- **Psychological Wound Timeline**: Reconstruct the temporal sequence of wounding events and their cumulative impact on the character's psychology, distinguishing formative wounds from later reinforcing events.
- **Dissonance Mapping**: Identify gaps between the character's self-concept (how they think they are) and their behavioral reality (how they actually behave), as these gaps generate dramatic tension and arc potential.
- **Relational Projection Analysis**: Identify patterns where the character projects their psychological material onto other characters — treating others as if they were figures from the character's wound history.
- **Arc Plausibility Assessment**: Evaluate whether the character's stated arc (the change they undergo) is psychologically plausible given their wound depth, their available support, and the narrative's treatment of prerequisites for change.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Attachment Theory
- **Secure Attachment**: Comfortable with intimacy and interdependence; seeks connection without fear of abandonment or engulfment
- **Anxious-Preoccupied**: Preoccupied with relationships, fears abandonment, seeks constant reassurance, interprets neutral signals as rejection
- **Dismissive-Avoidant**: Values independence, dismisses the importance of relationships, suppresses emotional needs, avoids intimacy
- **Fearful-Avoidant (Disorganized)**: Simultaneously desires and fears intimacy; approach-avoidance conflict in relationships; often associated with early trauma

### Defense Mechanisms
- **Projection**: Attributing one's own unacceptable feelings or qualities to another person
- **Rationalization**: Constructing post-hoc logical justifications for emotionally driven choices
- **Intellectualization**: Analyzing threatening material in abstract, theoretical terms to avoid emotional engagement
- **Reaction Formation**: Expressing the opposite of a threatening feeling (being excessively kind when feeling hatred)
- **Dissociation**: Disconnecting from feelings, memories, or sense of self in response to overwhelming experience
- **Splitting**: Seeing people or situations as entirely good or entirely bad, without integration

### Trauma Signatures
- **Hypervigilance**: Chronic heightened alertness for threat; difficulty relaxing or trusting safety signals
- **Avoidance**: Systematic behavioral or cognitive avoidance of triggers associated with the wound
- **Re-enactment**: Unconscious recreation of wounding dynamics in present relationships
- **Somatic Responses**: Physical responses to psychological threat — tension, nausea, dissociation
- **Trigger**: A stimulus that activates the wound's emotional charge, producing a response disproportionate to the present situation

### Schema and Cognition
- **Core Belief**: Fundamental, often unconscious conviction about self or world derived from wound experience
- **Cognitive Distortion**: Systematic bias in perception or reasoning that reinforces the core belief (catastrophizing, mind-reading, all-or-nothing thinking)
- **Confirmation Bias in Character**: Tendency to selectively perceive and remember experiences that confirm the core belief while ignoring contrary evidence

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Armchair Diagnosis**: Applying clinical diagnostic labels (narcissistic personality disorder, PTSD) to fictional characters as if they were patients; this pathologizes and reduces characters to their diagnoses.
- **Over-Determination**: Explaining every character behavior through their wound, leaving no room for genuine agency, growth, or simple situational response.
- **Psychology as Excuse**: Using psychological analysis to justify rather than examine character behavior; the goal is understanding, not absolution.
- **Consistency Policing**: Flagging every behavioral variation as a psychological inconsistency; humans are genuinely inconsistent, and not every variance signals an authorial error.
- **Single-Framework Reductionism**: Forcing a character's entire psychology into one framework (all Jungian archetypes, all Freudian drives) and ignoring evidence that doesn't fit.
- **Surface Behavior Analysis**: Analyzing only what characters do at the surface level without investigating the defense mechanisms, schemas, and attachment patterns that generate the behavior.
- **Prescriptive Arc Analysis**: Prescribing what psychological change "should" look like without considering the specific wound, the narrative's tone, or the author's intentions.

## Quality Indicators

- **Multi-Source Evidence**: Psychological assessments cite behavioral evidence from at least three distinct scenes or incidents, not just one
- **Framework Triangulation**: Analysis applies at least two distinct psychological frameworks and notes where they converge or diverge
- **Defense/Trait Distinction**: Analysis explicitly distinguishes between the character's defensive behaviors and their underlying traits or needs
- **Inconsistency Investigation**: When behavioral inconsistencies are identified, analysis proposes a psychological explanation rather than simply flagging them as errors
- **Arc Plausibility Verdict**: Analysis concludes with an explicit assessment of whether the character's arc is psychologically plausible and why
- **Specific Wound Attribution**: The core wound is identified with specific textual evidence, not inferred from general personality descriptions
- **Diagnostic Humility**: Analysis acknowledges the limits of psychological analysis applied to fictional constructs and avoids overclaiming certainty

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Character Designer**: Character designer creates the wound/want/need architecture; character psychologist validates its internal consistency and identifies behavioral implications that the designer should make legible in the character's actions
- **With Dialogue Specialist**: Character psychologist identifies how psychological defense mechanisms should manifest in speech — avoidance topics, characteristic deflections, emotional tells — that dialogue specialist encodes in actual dialogue
- **With Continuity Checker**: Psychological inconsistencies are a category of continuity error; character psychologist provides the framework for identifying when a character's behavior breaks their established psychological profile
- **With Narrative Director**: Director needs psychological plausibility assessments to evaluate whether character arcs are credible; character psychologist provides the analytical grounding for directorial decisions about arc pacing and change moments

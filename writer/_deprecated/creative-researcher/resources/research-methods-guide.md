# Research Methods Guide for Creative Writing

## Source Evaluation Framework

### The CRAAP Test for Fiction Research
Apply these criteria to every source, but weight them differently than academic research:

1. **Currency**: How recent is the information? For historical research, older sources may be more authentic. For science, currency matters more. Ask: has scholarship overturned this source's claims?

2. **Relevance**: Does this source address your specific needs? A general history of Rome is less useful than a study of daily life in 2nd-century Rome if your novel is set there

3. **Authority**: Who created this source? A historian's account of medieval warfare is more reliable than a novelist's, but a veteran soldier's memoir may be more experientially true than either

4. **Accuracy**: Can claims be verified elsewhere? Cross-reference key details. Be especially skeptical of commonly repeated "facts" that may be myths

5. **Purpose**: Why was this source created? A travel memoir has different biases than a government report. Primary sources have their own agendas. Acknowledge and account for bias

### Source Types and Their Uses

#### Primary Sources
Documents created during the period you are researching:
- **Letters and diaries**: Closest to lived experience. Show how people thought, felt, and expressed themselves in their own time
- **Official records**: Census data, court records, tax rolls, military records. Factual framework but filtered through bureaucratic purposes
- **Newspapers and periodicals**: Contemporary attitudes, events as perceived in real time, advertising (reveals material culture), public discourse
- **Photographs and artwork**: Visual evidence of places, dress, technology, social arrangement. Remember that photographs are also constructed
- **Objects and artifacts**: Museum collections, archaeological findings. The physical reality of the past
- **Oral histories**: Recorded interviews with people who lived through events. Subject to memory distortion but rich in experiential detail

#### Secondary Sources
Scholarly analysis and interpretation:
- **Monographs**: Deep dives into specific topics by expert historians. Your primary analytical sources
- **Journal articles**: Current research, often more specific and up-to-date than books
- **Biographies**: Useful for understanding individual experiences within historical contexts
- **Reference works**: Encyclopedias, dictionaries, atlases. Starting points, not endpoints

#### Tertiary Sources
Compilations and digests:
- **Wikipedia**: Useful for orientation and bibliography discovery. Never cite as a primary source
- **Textbooks**: Broad overviews. Good for identifying what you need to research more deeply
- **Documentary films**: Accessible but often dramatized. Verify claims independently

## Research by Domain

### Historical Period Research

#### Ancient World (before 500 CE)
- **Challenge**: Limited sources, heavy reliance on elite perspectives
- **Key sources**: Archaeological evidence, surviving texts (mostly philosophical, political, or religious), material culture
- **Common traps**: Projecting modern emotions and social structures onto ancient peoples; treating "the Romans" or "the Greeks" as monolithic; ignoring the vast majority of people who left no written record
- **Best approach**: Combine archaeological evidence (what we can prove) with careful extrapolation (what we can reasonably infer) and imagination (what we must invent)

#### Medieval Period (500-1500 CE)
- **Challenge**: Popular culture is saturated with medieval myths and anachronisms
- **Key sources**: Church records, illuminated manuscripts, legal documents, chronicle histories, material culture
- **Common traps**: Clean castles, modern hygiene, democratic values, constant warfare without economic context, flat social structures
- **Best approach**: Start with specialized medieval history (not popular medieval fantasy), study daily life specifically, pay attention to the Church's role in all aspects of life

#### Early Modern Period (1500-1800)
- **Challenge**: Transition period where modern and pre-modern coexist
- **Key sources**: Increasingly abundant written records, early newspapers, personal correspondence, travel accounts, colonial records
- **Common traps**: Anachronistic nationalism, ignoring the speed of change (the world of 1500 and 1750 are radically different), romantic primitivism
- **Best approach**: Anchor firmly to specific decade and location. "The 18th century" is too broad

#### Modern Period (1800-present)
- **Challenge**: Overwhelming volume of sources; difficulty achieving critical distance from recent past
- **Key sources**: Newspapers, photographs, film, recorded music, abundant personal documents, oral histories for 20th century
- **Common traps**: Nostalgia, presentism (judging the past by present standards without understanding its context), over-reliance on popular culture depictions of recent history
- **Best approach**: Use primary sources to challenge your assumptions about what a period "felt like"

### Cultural Research Methods

#### Researching Cultures Other Than Your Own
1. **Begin with self-awareness**: Understand your own cultural position and biases. What assumptions do you bring? What do you consider "normal" that is actually culturally specific?

2. **Seek insider perspectives first**: Books by members of the culture, oral histories, autobiographies, cultural studies by scholars from within the tradition. These are your primary sources

3. **Read scholarly outsider perspectives second**: Anthropological and sociological studies. Be aware of colonial and orientalist frameworks, even in recent scholarship

4. **Attend to diversity within cultures**: No culture is monolithic. Age, class, gender, region, education, and individual temperament create variation. Avoid "the Japanese believe..." or "Indigenous peoples value..."

5. **Identify your gaps**: What do you not know that you do not know? Seek sensitivity readers from the culture you are depicting. Their feedback is not optional

6. **Understand the politics of representation**: Some stories are not yours to tell. Others can be told if done with sufficient care, humility, and investment in accuracy. Know the difference

#### Language and Dialect Research
- **Sociolinguistic approach**: Speech patterns correlate with class, region, education, age, and social context. Research specific speech communities, not generic "dialects"
- **Historical linguistics**: Words have histories. Track when specific terms entered the language, how meanings shifted, what was considered vulgar or elevated
- **Idiolect creation**: Individual speech patterns built from cultural context, personal history, and psychological traits. Research the building blocks, then create unique combinations
- **Translation challenges**: If characters speak a language other than the text's language, how do you represent this? Research the options: direct translation, transliterated speech, code-switching, the convention of italics

### Scientific and Technical Research

#### Making Science Fiction Scientifically Plausible
1. **Understand the current state of knowledge**: What do scientists actually believe, as opposed to what popular science articles claim?
2. **Identify your speculative leap**: Where exactly does your science depart from known reality? Make this boundary clear in your own mind
3. **Extrapolate consistently**: If you change one scientific principle, trace the consequences. What else changes?
4. **Consult experts**: Scientists are often willing to discuss their field with writers. Ask specific questions, not "tell me about your field"
5. **Know when to handwave**: Not every scientific detail needs explanation. Sometimes "the ship's drive engaged" is sufficient. Readers will accept one or two unexplained technologies but not a world where everything is magic labeled as science

#### Professional Domain Research
- **Observe if possible**: Spend time in the environment you are writing about. A day in a courtroom, a hospital, a factory floor teaches more than a book
- **Interview practitioners**: Ask about their daily routine, their frustrations, their jargon, the moments of boredom and excitement, what outsiders always get wrong
- **Read professional literature**: Trade publications, professional blogs, industry reports. These reveal the internal culture and current concerns of a profession
- **Study the hierarchy**: Every profession has a social structure, a pecking order, a set of unwritten rules. These shape character dynamics

## Research Organization Systems

### The Scene-Based Research File
Instead of organizing research by topic (History, Culture, Science), organize by scene or chapter:

```
Chapter 3: The Market Scene (Constantinople, 1204)
  - Market layout: [source: Nicolle, "Constantinople 1204"]
  - Goods available: spices from X, silk from Y, fish from Z [source: ...]
  - Sounds: hawkers, animals, church bells (which church, what time) [source: ...]
  - Smells: fish, incense, cooking oil, animal dung, the harbor [extrapolated from ...]
  - Character would notice: the foreign merchants, the quality of silk [based on character background]
  - Period vocabulary for this scene: bezant (coin), hyperpyron, strategos [source: ...]
  - Social dynamics: who shops where, who serves whom [source: ...]
```

### The Fact-Check Ledger
Maintain a running list of claims that need verification:

| Detail | Scene | Source | Verified? | Notes |
|--------|-------|--------|-----------|-------|
| Tomatoes in Italy | Ch. 5 | Assumption | NO | Verify date of introduction from Americas |
| Women could own property | Ch. 8 | Smith, p. 47 | YES | Only widows, specific conditions |
| Distance London to York | Ch. 12 | Google Maps | ADJUST | By horse in 1400, not modern road |

### The Anachronism Register
Track potential anachronisms proactively:

```
## Words to Avoid (not yet coined in 1805)
- "okay" (1839)
- "scientist" (1834, use "natural philosopher")
- "photograph" (1839)
- "dinosaur" (1842)
- "stereotype" (in the metaphorical sense, much later)

## Concepts to Avoid
- Germ theory (not established until 1860s-1880s)
- Evolution by natural selection (1859)
- Psychology as a discipline (late 19th century)

## Technologies Not Yet Available
- Matches (1826, use tinderbox)
- Canned food (1810)
- Telegraph (1837)
```

## Balancing Research and Creativity

### The 80/20 Rule
Research 80% of what you need before you start writing. Accept that 20% will emerge as needs during the writing process. Do not wait for 100% -- you will never get there, and the writing itself will reveal what you actually need to know.

### When Research Conflicts with Story
Sometimes historical accuracy works against narrative needs. Guidelines:
- **Never falsify to harm**: Do not misrepresent historical atrocities, diminish real suffering, or whitewash oppression for narrative convenience
- **Compress time judiciously**: If three historical events over five years serve the story better compressed into two years, this is acceptable fiction-making. Note it in an author's note
- **Invent within the gaps**: Where history is silent, imagination fills in. This is where fiction lives -- in the spaces between facts
- **Choose truth over accuracy**: Emotional truth, thematic truth, human truth may sometimes require departing from strict factual accuracy. The novelist's loyalty is to truth, not to fact

### Research Red Flags
Watch for these signs that research is becoming a problem:
- You have been researching for months without writing a word
- You cannot start a scene without looking something up
- Your prose is becoming a vehicle for information delivery
- You feel anxious about "getting it wrong" to the point of paralysis
- You are researching topics tangential to your story because they are interesting

When you notice these, start writing. You can always research more later. The first draft is for getting the story down, not for getting every detail right.

## Quality Checklist

Before delivering research to a writer:
- [ ] Multiple sources consulted for key claims (minimum 3 for important details)
- [ ] Primary sources prioritized where available
- [ ] Cultural research includes insider perspectives
- [ ] Anachronisms identified and flagged
- [ ] Research organized for narrative use (by scene/chapter, not by topic)
- [ ] Open questions clearly identified
- [ ] Sensory details included (not just facts and dates)
- [ ] Potential anachronisms in language, technology, and social attitudes flagged
- [ ] Sources annotated with specific page references
- [ ] Balance between thoroughness and usability maintained

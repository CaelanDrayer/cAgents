# Critical Analysis Guide

## The Close Reading Method

Close reading is the foundation of all literary analysis. It is the practice of slowing down, paying attention to what the text actually does at the level of language, and building interpretation from observation rather than assumption.

### Step 1: First Reading -- Experience
Read the text as a reader, not a critic. Note your emotional responses, confusions, delights, and resistances. These are data. The places where you feel something strongly or feel nothing at all are both significant.

### Step 2: Second Reading -- Observation
Read again with a pencil. Mark:
- **Diction choices**: Words that surprise, words that feel too expected, words that carry unusual weight
- **Syntactic patterns**: Sentence length variation, subordination patterns, periodic vs. loose sentences
- **Imagery clusters**: Recurring images, sensory patterns, figurative language
- **Sound patterns**: Alliteration, assonance, consonance, rhythm (yes, prose has rhythm)
- **Gaps and silences**: What is not said, what is elided, what is implied

### Step 3: Third Reading -- Interpretation
Now ask: Why these choices? What do the patterns create? How do the parts relate to the whole?

## Analytical Frameworks in Practice

### The Five Codes (Roland Barthes, S/Z)

Apply these five codes to any passage for layered analysis:

1. **Hermeneutic Code** (enigma): What questions does the text raise? How does it create mystery, withhold information, delay revelation? Track the promises the text makes and when/how it fulfills them.

2. **Proairetic Code** (action): What sequences of action does the text initiate? Each action implies a consequence -- how does the text manage reader expectation about what will happen next?

3. **Semantic Code** (connotation): What connotations cluster around characters, settings, and situations? These accumulations create meaning beyond the literal -- a character consistently associated with darkness, enclosure, and silence is being semantically constructed.

4. **Symbolic Code** (theme): What binary oppositions structure the text's meaning? Light/dark, nature/culture, freedom/constraint, self/other. How does the text negotiate these oppositions -- does it resolve them, complicate them, or hold them in tension?

5. **Cultural Code** (reference): What shared knowledge does the text assume or invoke? Scientific, historical, literary, moral -- these references anchor the text in a web of cultural meaning.

### Narrative Voice Analysis

#### Voice vs. Style
- **Voice** is the personality behind the prose -- the implied consciousness shaping the reader's experience
- **Style** is the set of technical choices that create voice: diction, syntax, imagery, rhythm, tone
- Voice remains consistent even as style varies; style is the instrument, voice is the music

#### The Four Questions of Voice
1. **Who is speaking?** (narrator identity, reliability, knowledge)
2. **To whom?** (implied reader, narratee, distance)
3. **From where?** (temporal distance, spatial position, social location)
4. **With what attitude?** (tone, irony, sympathy, judgment)

#### Narrative Distance Spectrum
- **Intimate**: Stream of consciousness, deep interiority (Joyce, Woolf)
- **Close**: Free indirect discourse, limited third person (Austen, Flaubert)
- **Moderate**: Third person with selective interiority (most commercial fiction)
- **Distant**: Omniscient, authorial, panoramic (Tolstoy, Dickens)
- **Remote**: Objective, camera-eye, behaviorist (Hemingway, Hammett)

Each distance has its powers and limitations. The key question is whether the chosen distance serves the story's needs.

### Prose Quality Rubric

#### Level 1: Apprentice
- Prose is functional but unremarkable
- Default word choices, generic descriptions
- Sentence structure monotonous
- Metaphors are cliches or mixed
- No discernible voice
- Tells rather than shows routinely

#### Level 2: Competent
- Prose is clear and occasionally vivid
- Some precise word choices amid generic ones
- Some sentence variety
- Occasional effective imagery
- Emerging voice, not yet consistent
- Shows and tells with some judgment about which

#### Level 3: Skilled
- Prose is consistently clear with frequent moments of beauty or precision
- Diction is generally precise, with occasional lapses
- Good sentence variety creating appropriate rhythm
- Imagery is mostly fresh and purposeful
- Recognizable voice maintained across the work
- Strong instincts about when to show and when to tell

#### Level 4: Accomplished
- Prose is a pleasure to read at the sentence level
- Diction is precise and surprising without being showy
- Sentence structure creates meaning through rhythm and emphasis
- Imagery is organic to voice and vision
- Voice is distinctive and controlled
- Narrative distance is purposeful and consistent

#### Level 5: Mastery
- Prose achieves effects that are difficult to analyze because they feel inevitable
- Every word choice feels both surprising and right
- Sentence rhythm and paragraph architecture create emotional experiences
- Imagery is integrated into the fabric of the prose rather than applied to its surface
- Voice is unmistakable -- you could identify this writer from a single paragraph
- The prose seems to know things the writer might not consciously know

### Thematic Analysis Method

#### Step 1: Identify Recurring Motifs
Track images, words, situations, and ideas that recur. Frequency and placement matter -- what appears at beginnings, endings, climactic moments?

#### Step 2: Map Thematic Relationships
How do motifs relate to each other? Do they reinforce, contrast, complicate? Draw a concept map connecting thematic elements.

#### Step 3: Trace Thematic Development
How do themes evolve through the narrative? Early statement -> complication -> crisis -> resolution (or irresolution)?

#### Step 4: Assess Embodiment
Is each theme dramatized through character action and consequence, or merely stated through dialogue, narration, or symbol?

#### Step 5: Evaluate Integration
Does every element of the text (plot, character, setting, language) contribute to thematic meaning, or do themes feel separate from story?

### Structural Analysis Templates

#### Three-Act Structure Evaluation
- **Act 1 (Setup)**: Does it establish character, world, and stakes efficiently? Does it raise the central question? Is the inciting incident well-placed?
- **Act 2 (Confrontation)**: Does rising action escalate stakes genuinely? Is the midpoint a true reversal? Does the dark night of the soul feel earned?
- **Act 3 (Resolution)**: Does the climax emerge from character and situation rather than coincidence? Is the resolution satisfying without being too tidy?

#### Scene-Level Assessment
For each scene, evaluate:
- **Purpose**: What does this scene accomplish that no other scene could? (If nothing, it should be cut or combined)
- **Conflict**: What opposing forces create tension within the scene?
- **Change**: How is the situation different at the end of the scene from the beginning? (If unchanged, the scene is static)
- **Economy**: Does the scene accomplish its purpose in the minimum necessary space?

## Common Critique Scenarios

### The Overwritten Passage
**Diagnosis**: Purple prose, excessive description, adjective/adverb overload, mixed metaphors
**Root cause**: Usually insecurity about whether the reader will "get it," or a writer in love with their own language
**Prescription**: Ask "what is the one thing this passage needs to make the reader feel?" Cut everything that does not serve that feeling. Trust the reader's imagination.

### The Underwritten Passage
**Diagnosis**: Key moments rushed past, emotional beats skipped, important scenes summarized instead of dramatized
**Root cause**: Often a writer who is more comfortable with action than with interiority, or who fears sentimentality
**Prescription**: Identify the moments that the reader needs to experience in real time. Expand those. Not every moment needs expansion -- just the ones that carry emotional or thematic weight.

### The Dialogue Problem
**Diagnosis**: Characters all sound the same, dialogue is on-the-nose, speeches are too long, attribution is confusing
**Root cause**: The writer has not yet heard each character's distinct voice in their head, or is using dialogue as a shortcut for exposition
**Prescription**: Read dialogue aloud. Does each character have a rhythm, a vocabulary, a set of verbal habits? Is subtext doing work, or is everything stated directly?

### The Structure Problem
**Diagnosis**: Saggy middle, anticlimactic ending, too many subplots, pacing issues
**Root cause**: Often a discovery writer who has not revised with structural awareness, or a plotter who has followed their outline past the point where the story wanted to go somewhere else
**Prescription**: Map the story's emotional arc on a graph. Where does energy flag? Where does the reader's investment peak and trough? Restructure around the emotional experience, not the plot logic.

### The Voice Problem
**Diagnosis**: Prose is technically correct but generic, could have been written by anyone, lacks personality
**Root cause**: The writer is performing "good writing" rather than writing from their own sensibility. Often the result of over-workshopping or over-reading craft advice.
**Prescription**: Find the passages where the writer's natural voice breaks through (they always exist). Build outward from those. The writer's voice is not something to construct -- it is something to uncover.

## Example Critique Structure

### 1. Opening Assessment (1-2 paragraphs)
State what the text is attempting and how well it achieves it at a high level. Demonstrate understanding of the writer's goals.

### 2. What Works (2-3 paragraphs)
Identify specific strengths with quoted evidence. Explain why they work -- what craft principle they exemplify.

### 3. Primary Concern (2-3 paragraphs)
Identify the single most important issue. Quote specific passages. Explain the mechanism of the problem. Suggest a direction for revision (not a specific fix).

### 4. Secondary Concerns (1-2 paragraphs each)
Address 2-3 additional issues in order of importance. Same pattern: quote, explain mechanism, suggest direction.

### 5. Summary and Priorities (1 paragraph)
Restate the text's strongest qualities. Identify the one revision priority that would most improve the work. End with encouragement grounded in specific evidence of the writer's ability.

## Reference Library

### Essential Critical Texts
- Aristotle, *Poetics* -- still the foundation of narrative theory
- E.M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* -- accessible, witty, essential
- Wayne C. Booth, *The Rhetoric of Fiction* -- narrator, implied author, reliability
- Roland Barthes, *S/Z* -- the five codes, readerly vs. writerly texts
- Wolfgang Iser, *The Act of Reading* -- reader-response, gaps and blanks
- Viktor Shklovsky, *Art as Technique* -- defamiliarization, the core purpose of literary language
- Mikhail Bakhtin, *The Dialogic Imagination* -- heteroglossia, chronotope, carnival
- James Wood, *How Fiction Works* -- contemporary close reading at its best

### On the Craft of Criticism
- William H. Gass, *Fiction and the Figures of Life* -- criticism as creative act
- Susan Sontag, *Against Interpretation* -- the limits of thematic extraction
- Francine Prose, *Reading Like a Writer* -- close reading as craft education

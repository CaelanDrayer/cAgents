# The Editor's Craft: A Comprehensive Guide

## The Developmental Edit

### Manuscript Assessment Framework

Before touching a single word, read the entire manuscript. Then answer these questions:

**The Foundational Five**:
1. What is this story about? (Theme, not plot. If you can't state it in one sentence, the manuscript may not know either.)
2. What does the protagonist want, and what prevents them from getting it? (External goal + internal obstacle = dramatic engine.)
3. What is at stake? (What happens if the protagonist fails? If the answer is "nothing much," the stakes need work.)
4. Does the ending earn its resolution? (Is the climax the inevitable result of everything before it?)
5. What is the single biggest issue that, if fixed, would most improve the manuscript?

**The Diagnostic Read**: On first read, resist the urge to mark individual problems. Read as a reader, not an editor. Note your emotional responses: where did you get bored? Where did you lose track? Where were you moved? Where were you confused? These responses are diagnostic data. Boredom indicates pacing or stakes problems. Confusion indicates clarity or structural problems. Being moved indicates what's working.

### The Editorial Letter

**Structure**:
1. **Opening**: What the manuscript does well. Be specific -- not "great characters" but "Elena's voice in the first-person sections has a distinctive sardonic warmth that makes her immediately compelling."
2. **The Big Picture**: The one or two structural issues that matter most. Explain why they're problems, what effect they have on the reader, and suggest possible directions (not solutions).
3. **Character Notes**: Arc issues, motivation gaps, relationship dynamics that need attention.
4. **Pacing and Structure**: Scene-by-scene assessment where needed. Which scenes drag, which feel rushed, which could be cut or combined.
5. **Prose-Level Notes**: Only if the manuscript is structurally sound. Voice consistency, recurring tics, passages that exemplify what works and what doesn't.
6. **Closing**: Encouragement that's honest, not hollow. Reiterate the manuscript's core strengths and express genuine enthusiasm for what it can become.

**Tone**: Direct but collegial. You are a fellow craftsperson offering professional assessment, not a teacher grading a student. "I wonder if..." and "Have you considered..." are more productive than "You need to..." and "This should be..."

## Structural Editing

### Act Structure Analysis

**The Opening (First 10-15%)**:
- Establishes voice, world, protagonist, and central dramatic question
- Must accomplish "grounding" (where/when), "intrigue" (why keep reading), and "investment" (who to care about)
- Common problem: information-dumping worldbuilding or backstory before the reader cares
- The fix is rarely "add more setup" -- it's "start closer to the action and weave context in"

**Act I (First 25%)**:
- Introduces the protagonist's ordinary world and flaw
- The inciting incident disrupts equilibrium
- The first plot point commits the protagonist to the journey
- Common problem: Act I that's too long (the reader is waiting for the story to start)
- Diagnostic: if you can start at chapter 3 without losing anything essential, Act I is too long

**The Midpoint (50%)**:
- The midpoint is the fulcrum. Everything before it builds to this moment; everything after responds to it
- A strong midpoint shifts the story from reactive to proactive, raises the stakes, reveals new information, or recontextualizes what came before
- Common problem: no real midpoint -- just the next event in a sequence
- The midpoint is where "sagging middle syndrome" either manifests or is prevented

**Act II (25-75%)**:
- Rising complications, escalating stakes, deepening character
- Each scene should either advance the plot OR deepen character (ideally both)
- Common problem: repetitive scenes (three scenes of the detective interviewing witnesses who all say similar things)
- The fix: combine, compress, or find ways to make each scene serve multiple purposes

**Act III (Final 25%)**:
- Acceleration toward climax
- All subplots converging
- The "all is lost" moment before the final push
- Common problem: rushed resolution (the author ran out of pages or energy)
- Common problem: new information introduced too late (feels like cheating)

**The Climax**:
- Must be the moment of highest dramatic intensity
- Must resolve the central dramatic question
- The protagonist's internal change should enable (or be caused by) the external climax
- The reader should feel both surprise and inevitability

**The Resolution**:
- Brevity is a virtue -- once the climax resolves, don't overstay
- Answer the reader's remaining questions efficiently
- Leave room for resonance (the last line should echo)

### Scene-Level Analysis

Every scene should pass the "scene turn" test:
1. What is the POV character's goal entering this scene?
2. What obstacle do they face?
3. What is the outcome? (Yes/No/Yes-but/No-and)
4. How has the situation changed by the scene's end?

If a scene doesn't turn -- if the situation at the end is the same as the beginning -- the scene is inert. Cut it, combine it, or give it a turn.

**Scene Types**:
- **Scene**: Action, conflict, decision (advances plot)
- **Sequel**: Reaction, dilemma, decision (processes what just happened)
- Great pacing alternates between scenes and sequels with decreasing sequel length as tension rises

## Character Arc Editing

### The Arc Assessment

| Question | What to Look For |
|----------|-----------------|
| What is the character's flaw? | Should be specific, not generic. "Fear of commitment" is generic. "Pushes people away before they can leave because her father abandoned the family" is specific. |
| How does the flaw create conflict? | The flaw should make the character's goal harder to achieve. If the flaw doesn't interact with the plot, it's decorative. |
| What forces change? | External pressure must be specific and escalating. Each act should increase the cost of maintaining the flaw. |
| Is the change gradual? | Transformation through a single epiphany is rarely earned. Look for incremental shifts with setbacks. |
| Does the final choice prove the change? | The climax should force the character to choose between their old way and the new truth. The choice IS the character arc. |

### Supporting Character Assessment

Every significant supporting character should serve at least one of these functions:
- **Mirror**: Reflects an aspect of the protagonist (foil, parallel, or dark mirror)
- **Catalyst**: Forces the protagonist to confront their flaw
- **Obstacle**: Creates conflict by opposing the protagonist's goal
- **Thematic**: Embodies an alternative response to the story's central question

Characters who serve none of these functions are passengers. Consider cutting, combining, or giving them a function.

## Line Editing

### Prose Rhythm Diagnostics

**The Read-Aloud Test**: Great prose has music. Read passages aloud. Where do you stumble? Where does the rhythm feel mechanical? Where does it flow? Stumbling points are editing targets.

**Sentence Length Variation**: Count sentence lengths in a paragraph. If they're all similar (12 words, 14 words, 11 words, 13 words), the prose will feel monotonous regardless of content. Vary deliberately: long complex sentence, then short punch. Let rhythm mirror meaning.

**Paragraph Architecture**: The first sentence of a paragraph sets its direction. The last sentence provides its emphasis. The middle does the work. If the first sentence is weak, the reader may not reach the strong last sentence.

**The Three-Sentence Test for Overwriting**: If you can cut a passage to three sentences and the essential meaning survives, the original was overwritten. Not every passage needs cutting -- descriptive passages earn their length through sensory specificity. But if cutting doesn't lose meaning, the original was padding.

### Word-Level Precision

**Verb Strength**: Strong, specific verbs carry more weight than verb + adverb. "She walked quickly across the room" < "She strode across the room" < "She crossed the room in three steps." Each version increases precision and character revelation.

**Adjective Audit**: Every adjective should pass the "is this earning its place?" test. "The old man" -- does the reader need "old" here? If age matters to the scene, yes. If it's reflexive description, cut it.

**The "Very" Test**: Almost every instance of "very" can be replaced with a stronger word or cut entirely. "Very tired" → "exhausted." "Very big" → "enormous." "Very" is the lazy writer's intensifier.

**Cliche Detection**: Cliches are invisible to the writer and glaring to the reader. "Her heart raced." "He let out a breath he didn't know he'd been holding." "Silence was deafening." Flag these for the author to find fresher expressions.

## Genre-Specific Editorial Priorities

### Romance
- Emotional beats are load-bearing structural elements, not decorations
- The relationship arc requires its own three-act structure with clear escalation
- Internal conflict must be specific and justified (not arbitrary misunderstanding)
- Chemistry must be demonstrated in scene, not stated in narration
- The "black moment" must feel genuinely threatening to the relationship

### Thriller/Suspense
- Chapter endings must propel the reader forward (the "one more chapter" effect)
- Information release is the primary structural tool -- what the reader knows and when they know it
- The ticking clock must be credible and escalating
- Rest beats are necessary but must be brief and still charged with tension
- The villain's plan must be smart enough to be genuinely threatening

### Mystery/Crime
- Fair play: the reader must have access to the clues needed to solve the mystery
- Red herrings must be plausible enough to mislead without feeling like cheating
- The detective's method of solution should feel both earned and surprising
- Pacing requires balancing investigation progress with character development
- The reveal must recontextualize earlier events in a satisfying way

### Literary Fiction
- Prose quality is a primary concern, not secondary
- Thematic depth and resonance take precedence over plot mechanics
- Character interiority must be rich and specific
- Structure can be unconventional but must serve the story's purpose
- The ending should provide emotional and thematic resonance, not necessarily plot resolution

### Fantasy/Science Fiction
- World-building must serve story, never the reverse -- exposition is not plot
- Magic/technology systems need internal consistency
- Familiar human drama must anchor unfamiliar settings
- The balance between wonder and accessibility requires careful calibration
- Avoid "As you know, Bob" exposition -- find organic ways to convey world information

### Horror
- Atmosphere and dread outweigh shock and gore
- The source of horror should connect to genuine human fears
- Escalation must be carefully managed -- peak too early and the rest feels flat
- The unsettling is more durable than the startling
- Character vulnerability must be established for horror to land

## Revision Orchestration

### The Multi-Pass Method

| Pass | Focus | Key Questions |
|------|-------|---------------|
| **1. Structural** | Architecture | Is every chapter necessary? Does every act earn its length? Is the climax satisfying? |
| **2. Scene** | Scene integrity | Does every scene turn? Can any scenes be combined or cut? |
| **3. Character** | Arc and consistency | Is the protagonist's change earned? Are supporting characters serving the story? |
| **4. Pacing** | Rhythm and flow | Where does it drag? Where does it rush? Are scene/sequel balanced? |
| **5. Prose** | Sentence craft | Is the voice consistent? Are there prose tics? Is word choice precise? |
| **6. Final** | Polish | Remaining inconsistencies, echoes, and small issues |

### Common Structural Prescriptions

| Symptom | Likely Cause | Treatment |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| Sagging middle | Weak midpoint or stalled stakes | Strengthen midpoint revelation; raise stakes at 40% mark |
| Rushed ending | Too much plot left for remaining pages | Start Act III earlier; cut or combine complications |
| Slow opening | Too much setup before story begins | Start closer to inciting incident; weave backstory later |
| Episodic feel | Scenes don't causally connect | Ensure each scene's outcome causes the next scene |
| Unclear stakes | No consequence for failure established | Clarify what the protagonist stands to lose |
| Flat climax | Climax doesn't resolve the central question | Realign climax with the story's core dramatic question |
| Unearned ending | Resolution doesn't follow from preceding events | Ensure the ending grows from the protagonist's choices, not coincidence |

## The Editor's Ethics

- The manuscript belongs to the author. Your job is to help them write their best version of their book, not your version of their book.
- Be honest, but be specific. "This isn't working" helps no one. "This scene loses tension because the reader already knows what the character is about to discover" is actionable.
- Acknowledge your own biases. If you dislike a genre, say so rather than editing against it unconsciously.
- Respect deadlines and communicate clearly about timelines.
- Maintain confidentiality about manuscripts and editorial correspondence.
- When in doubt about whether a change serves the story or your preferences, leave it to the author with a query.

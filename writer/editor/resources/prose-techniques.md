# Prose Techniques

## The Architecture of a Sentence

### Sentence Types and Their Effects

#### The Declarative Sentence
The backbone of prose. Subject-verb-object. Clear, direct, authoritative.
- "She left." Maximum impact through maximum simplicity.
- Declarative sentences accumulate force. Stack three and the fourth hits like a hammer.
- Use for: important actions, revelations, moments of clarity.

#### The Interrogative Sentence
Questions create immediate reader engagement. The mind involuntarily begins searching for answers.
- Direct question: "Where had she gone?" Creates mystery, forward pull.
- Rhetorical question: "What did it matter now?" Creates resignation, thematic weight.
- Free indirect question (no question mark): "Where had she gone." Creates interiority, the character asking themselves.

#### The Imperative Sentence
Commands addressed to the reader or within the narrative.
- "Listen." "Imagine." "Consider." Creates intimacy, breaks the fourth wall.
- Use sparingly -- too many imperatives make the reader feel lectured.

#### The Exclamatory Sentence
Heightened emotion marked by exclamation. In literary prose, almost always avoid. The prose should create the exclamation in the reader, not on the page. If you need an exclamation mark, the sentence has not done its job.
- Exception: dialogue, where characters may naturally exclaim.
- Exception: first-person narration where the narrator's personality includes exclamation.

### Sentence Openers

The first word of a sentence is a decision about emphasis, rhythm, and voice:

- **Subject-first** (default): "Sarah walked home." Neutral, efficient, transparent.
- **Participial**: "Walking home, Sarah noticed the light." Creates simultaneous action, cinematic quality. Risk: dangling participle.
- **Prepositional**: "In the darkness of the stairwell, she waited." Creates spatial grounding before action.
- **Adverbial**: "Slowly, she turned." Creates emphasis on manner. Use rarely -- adverb-first is usually weak.
- **Conjunction-first**: "And she walked home." "But the light was wrong." Creates a conversational voice, a sense of thought continuing beyond the previous sentence. Modern and effective when not overused.
- **It/There** (expletive): "There was a man standing at the gate." "It was raining." Weak openings that delay the true subject. Almost always improvable.

### Sentence Closers

The last word of a sentence receives the strongest emphasis. Choose it carefully:

**Weak closing**: "She looked at the painting with great sadness." (ends on abstract noun)
**Strong closing**: "She looked at the painting, and something inside her broke." (ends on vivid verb)

**Weak closing**: "The car moved down the road that was dark and winding." (ends on adjective string)
**Strong closing**: "The car crawled into darkness." (ends on concrete, evocative noun)

**Principle**: End sentences with strong nouns, strong verbs, or images. Do not end with prepositions, auxiliary verbs, or abstract modifiers unless the deflation is deliberate.

## Description Strategies

### The Sensory Inventory

Every important scene should engage at least three senses. The dominant sense should match the scene's emotional register:

- **Visual dominance**: Creates clarity, distance, beauty or ugliness. The default sense. Good for establishing scenes.
- **Auditory dominance**: Creates atmosphere, presence, temporal experience. Sounds locate us in time (bird dawn chorus, evening traffic, midnight silence).
- **Tactile dominance**: Creates intimacy, physicality, discomfort or comfort. The most embodied sense -- it makes characters feel real.
- **Olfactory dominance**: Creates memory, nausea, desire. Smell bypasses rational thought and connects directly to emotion and memory (Proust's madeleine).
- **Gustatory dominance**: Creates pleasure, disgust, or cultural specificity. Rare in most fiction, but powerful when used.

### Specific vs. General

**General**: "The old house looked run-down."
**Specific**: "The porch railing was held together with wire and optimism, and three of the front windows had been replaced with plywood sometime in the previous decade."

Specific details do three things simultaneously:
1. Create vivid visual reality (the reader sees the wire, the plywood)
2. Imply backstory (someone made these repairs; they were not wealthy)
3. Establish voice (the "wire and optimism" reveals a narrator with humor)

### The Telling Detail

One perfect detail communicates more than a paragraph of general description. The telling detail is:
- **Specific** (not "old clothes" but "a corduroy jacket with leather patches worn smooth")
- **Significant** (it reveals character, mood, or theme beyond its literal meaning)
- **Sensory** (it engages a specific sense, not abstract intellect)
- **Surprising** (it is not the obvious detail -- it is the one the reader would not have thought to include but immediately recognizes as true)

### Point-of-View Filtering

All description should be filtered through the observing character's consciousness:
- A chef notices different things about a kitchen than a child
- A soldier sees a forest differently than a botanist
- A person in love sees a rainy street differently than a person in grief

The question is never "What does this setting look like?" but "What does this character notice about this setting, and what does that reveal about them?"

## Prose Rhythm Workshop

### Exercise: The Rhythm Rewrite

Take this passage and rewrite it at different rhythmic tempos:

**Source**: "The man walked down the street. He was thinking about his wife. The houses were quiet. It was getting dark."

**Allegro** (fast, urgent):
"He walked fast, head down, the street emptying around him, houses shutting their eyes one by one as dark came on. His wife. He couldn't stop thinking about his wife."

**Andante** (moderate, flowing):
"The man walked down the street in the gathering dusk, the houses on either side settling into their evening silence, and as he walked he turned over in his mind the conversation he had been trying to have with his wife for three days."

**Adagio** (slow, contemplative):
"He walked. The street stretched before him in the thickening light, each house a closed world, each window a warm square floating in the blue-grey air. Somewhere inside one of those houses, a woman was setting the table, or reading, or standing at the sink with her hands in dishwater, looking at nothing. He tried to remember when his wife had last looked at nothing. He tried to remember when she had last looked at him."

### Exercise: Sentence Length as Meaning

Write a moment of violence in three sentence lengths:

**All short**: "The glass broke. Blood. She looked at her hand. Opened it. The shard fell. Someone screamed. Not her."

**All long**: "The glass broke against the counter and she watched, with the detached fascination of someone observing an event that had not yet become real, as a shard of it traced a line across her palm that immediately began to bead with small, precise dots of blood, each one catching the kitchen light like a jewel before it ran."

**Mixed** (most effective): "The glass broke against the counter. A shard traced a line across her palm -- precise, almost surgical -- and she watched the blood bead along it, each drop catching the kitchen light. She opened her hand. The shard fell. It rang against the tile like a small bell, and someone was screaming, and it was not her."

### Exercise: Sound Painting

Use the sounds of words to match their meaning:

**Hard consonants for violence**: "The crack of the bat, the crunch of bone, the thick wet thud of a body hitting packed earth."

**Soft consonants for gentleness**: "She whispered through the shimmer of silk, the softest syllables slipping between them like water through fingers."

**Long vowels for expansiveness**: "The whole wide ocean lay below, blue and open and aching in the moonlight."

**Short vowels for constriction**: "The pit was black. Wet. Quick flicks of sound -- drip, click, scritch -- in the dark."

## Anti-Pattern Gallery

### The Adjective Pile
**Bad**: "The large, dark, imposing, ancient, weathered stone castle loomed on the high, windswept, desolate cliff."
**Better**: "The castle stood on the cliff like a broken tooth."
**Why**: One precise image does more work than seven adjectives. The adjective pile signals a writer who does not trust any single word to be enough.

### The Thesaurus Syndrome
**Bad**: "She perambulated through the verdant sylvan glade, her countenance evincing a melancholic wistfulness."
**Better**: "She walked through the woods, and she looked sad."
**Why**: Unusual words draw attention to themselves and away from the meaning. Use rare words only when the precise meaning requires it, not for decoration.

### The Cliche Cluster
**Bad**: "Her heart was pounding like a drum, her blood ran cold, time stood still, and she felt a pit in her stomach."
**Better**: "Her body had become a list of symptoms."
**Why**: Cliches are invisible -- the reader's eye slides over them without registering. They communicate nothing because they have been communicated too many times before.

### The Passive Plague
**Bad**: "The door was opened by her. The light was turned on. The room was seen to be empty."
**Better**: "She opened the door, hit the light, and found the room empty."
**Why**: Passive voice removes the character from the action. Active voice creates agency, momentum, and clarity. (Passive has its uses -- for when the agent is unknown or unimportant -- but should be a deliberate choice, not a habit.)

### The Adverb Crutch
**Bad**: "She said angrily. He replied sarcastically. She whispered quietly."
**Better**: "She spat the words. He smirked. She breathed the words against his ear."
**Why**: Adverbs modify verbs. But if the verb is precise enough, it needs no modification. "Whispered quietly" is redundant. "Said angrily" means the writer chose the wrong verb.

## The Revision Process

### First Pass: Clarity
- Is every sentence understandable on first reading?
- Are pronouns clear? (Who is "she" in a scene with two women?)
- Are there unnecessary words? (Almost always yes)

### Second Pass: Precision
- Can any word be replaced with a more exact alternative?
- Are descriptions specific rather than general?
- Do verbs carry the maximum load? (Replace weak verb + adverb with strong verb)

### Third Pass: Rhythm
- Read aloud. Where does the mouth stumble?
- Vary sentence lengths (mark each sentence as S/M/L -- the pattern should not be regular)
- Check paragraph cadences -- does each paragraph end with appropriate weight?

### Fourth Pass: Economy
- Can the passage be cut by 20% without losing meaning?
- Are there sentences that say what other sentences already said?
- Is every description necessary for the scene to work?

### Fifth Pass: Voice
- Does every passage sound like this narrator?
- Are there sentences that could have been written by anyone?
- Does the voice maintain its identity through different emotional registers?

## Quick Reference: Common Fixes

| Problem | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Flat description | Add specific sensory detail filtered through POV |
| Monotonous rhythm | Vary sentence length (short-short-long, or long-short pattern) |
| Weak opening | Start with action or image, not explanation |
| Saggy middle | Cut transitional passages; jump-cut between scenes |
| Dead metaphor | Replace with fresh image drawn from character's experience |
| Passive voice | Identify the agent and make them the subject |
| Over-writing | Cut adjectives and adverbs; trust nouns and verbs |
| Under-writing | Expand key moments with sensory detail and interiority |
| Voice inconsistency | Create a voice document; do a dedicated voice-only revision pass |
| Telling instead of showing | Convert abstract statements into specific actions and observations |

## Free Indirect Discourse

The master technique for flexible narrative distance. Free indirect discourse (FID) renders a character's thoughts in third person, past tense, using the character's own vocabulary and rhythm rather than the narrator's:

**Direct thought**: She thought, "I can't believe he said that."
**Indirect thought**: She thought that she couldn't believe he had said that.
**Free indirect discourse**: She couldn't believe he'd said that. Who did he think he was?

FID eliminates the clunky "she thought" attribution, blends narrator and character voice seamlessly, and allows the narrator to be ironic about the character without the character's awareness.

### FID and Irony
Jane Austen pioneered ironic FID. "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." This is not Austen's belief -- it is her rendering of society's belief in FID, with the irony living in the gap between what the sentence asserts and what the novel demonstrates.

### FID as Voice Chameleon
When following multiple POV characters, FID allows each section to take on its character's voice without switching narrators. The prose BECOMES the character for the duration of their POV, then BECOMES the next character. This is how George Eliot, Austen, and Flaubert achieve their psychological depth.

## Prose Style Workshop

### Exercise: Style Mimicry
Take a single scene -- a character entering a room and sitting down -- and write it in five styles:

**Minimalist**: Focus on the concrete. Short sentences. No interiority. What can be observed. Let the omissions create meaning.

**Maximalist**: Overload with detail, digression, footnote-like asides, the character's entire associative chain triggered by the room. The room contains everything.

**Lyrical**: Every sentence carries rhythmic weight. Image-rich. The room becomes metaphor. The light does something; the dust does something.

**Sparse**: Strip all punctuation you can. No quotation marks. No commas where you can drop them. Let the prose run like a river finding its own banks.

**Ornate**: Every word chosen for sound AND meaning. The room described through elaborately precise language where each noun and adjective has been considered and reconsidered.

### Exercise: Finding Your Default
Write a scene without thinking about style. Then analyze: What is your natural sentence length? Do you default to visual or auditory description? Do you tend toward abstraction or concreteness? Do you use more commas or more periods? Your default style is not your best style -- it is your starting point. Style is what you do after you understand your defaults.

## Filter Words: The Invisible Wall

Filter words insert the character's perception between the reader and the experience:

**With filter**: She noticed the door was open. She felt a chill run through her. She could see blood on the floor.
**Without filter**: The door was open. A chill ran through her. Blood on the floor.

The filtered version is always weaker because it reminds the reader that they are reading about someone's experience rather than experiencing it directly.

**Common filters to eliminate**: saw, heard, felt, noticed, realized, knew, thought, seemed, appeared, wondered, watched, looked, could see, could hear, could feel

**When filters are useful**: When the act of perception IS the point. "She noticed the door was open" is weaker than "The door was open." But "She noticed before anyone else" is a statement about the character's attentiveness, not a filter.

## The Art of the Opening

### First Sentences That Work
- **Mystery**: "The man in black fled across the desert, and the gunslinger followed." (King) -- Two characters, a relationship, a landscape, and a question in one sentence
- **Voice**: "If you really want to hear about it, the first thing you'll probably want to know is where I was born, and what my lousy childhood was like." (Salinger) -- The voice IS the hook
- **Image**: "It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." (Orwell) -- One wrong detail in a normal sentence creates total unease
- **Situation**: "Someone must have slandered Josef K., for one morning, without having done anything truly wrong, he was arrested." (Kafka) -- The entire novel compressed into one sentence

### What Opening Sentences Must Do
1. Establish voice or tone (the reader decides within sentences whether to trust the narrator)
2. Create a question (even a small one -- curiosity is the engine of reading)
3. Set a rhythm (the first sentence teaches the reader how to read this book)
4. NOT explain (explanation is the enemy of opening momentum)

## The Art of the Closing

### Last Lines
The final sentence of a chapter, scene, or book determines how the reader carries the experience forward:
- **The resonant image**: End on a concrete image that echoes the scene's emotional meaning. Let the image do the interpretive work
- **The quiet line**: After a climactic scene, the most powerful close is often a small, quiet observation. The contrast creates depth
- **The question**: Ending on an unanswered question pulls the reader forward. Effective for chapter endings
- **The return**: Echoing the opening creates circular structure and the sense that the story has arrived somewhere inevitable

### Exercise: Rewrite the Closing
Take any scene you have written. Rewrite the final sentence five times. In each version, end on a different element: an image, a sound, an action, a thought, a piece of dialogue. Notice how the ending transforms the entire scene's meaning.

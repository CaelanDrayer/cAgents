# Best Practices: Creative Researcher

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality research for creative projects, including historical, cultural, technical, and sensory grounding work.

## Design Principles

- **Research for Feeling, Not Just Fact**: The goal of creative research is not just accurate information but authentic sensory and experiential texture — the smell of a place, the rhythm of a period's language, the weight of a technology. Facts are the skeleton; feeling is the flesh.
- **Iceberg Principle**: The writer needs to know ten times more than they put on the page. Deep research produces the 90% that never appears explicitly but that readers feel as confidence, authenticity, and specificity.
- **Specificity Is the Currency**: Vague research produces vague writing. The researcher's job is to find the specific details — exact dates, actual names, real procedures, genuine customs — that transform generic period fiction into grounded historical fiction.
- **Distinguish Primary from Secondary Sources**: Period accounts, firsthand documents, and contemporary observations produce different material than modern interpretations and summaries. Both are valuable; both serve different purposes. Track source type.
- **Research Reveals Better Stories**: The best research doesn't just validate the planned story; it reveals unexpected, stranger, more interesting details that improve the story. Follow surprising discoveries.
- **Calibrate Research Depth to Narrative Role**: A single-scene location needs different research depth than a setting that appears throughout. Match research intensity to narrative importance.
- **Document and Cite**: Creative research is valueless if undocumented. Every finding needs source, reliability assessment, and connection to its narrative use case.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Research-to-Narrative Mapping**: For each narrative element that requires research (setting, period technology, cultural practice, profession), define the specific research questions that need answering and the narrative purpose each answer serves.
- **Sensory Research Protocol**: Go beyond factual research to sensory specifics — what would characters see, hear, smell, taste, and feel in this setting? Sources include travel writing, memoirs, food history, material culture studies.
- **Primary Source Mining**: Identify available primary sources for the period or subject — diaries, letters, newspapers, official records, material artifacts — and prioritize them over secondary synthesis for authentic period voice and detail.
- **Cultural Consultant Framework**: For research into cultures outside the researcher's own experience, identify whether the project requires expert cultural consultation beyond desk research. Some topics require human expertise, not just library research.
- **Reliability Assessment Matrix**: Rate every source on reliability (peer-reviewed academic = highest; popular history = medium; Wikipedia = starting point only) and recency (newer scholarship may supersede older). Weight findings accordingly.
- **Anachronism Audit**: For period work, systematically review the setting's technology, language, social structures, and material culture for anachronistic details that would jar period-literate readers.
- **Detail Hierarchy**: Organize research findings into three categories — (1) Essential: narrative would break without this accuracy, (2) Significant: adds authenticity and confidence, (3) Texture: enriches the experience for knowledgeable readers. This guides the writer's integration choices.
- **Discovery Log**: Maintain a running log of unexpected, surprising, or especially vivid research discoveries that might improve the narrative beyond what was originally planned.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Research Methodology
- **Primary Source**: Original, firsthand document or artifact from the period or subject being researched — diary entries, government records, contemporary newspapers, original art, artifacts
- **Secondary Source**: Scholarly or analytical interpretation of primary sources — history books, academic articles, biographies based on primary research
- **Tertiary Source**: Compilations or summaries of secondary sources — encyclopedias, Wikipedia, general reference works. Useful for orientation, insufficient for deep grounding.
- **Archival Research**: Examination of original historical documents, typically in libraries, archives, or museums
- **Oral History**: Research gathered through interviews with people who have direct experience of the subject or period

### Period and Cultural Research
- **Material Culture**: The physical objects, tools, clothing, and technologies of a period or culture; the tangible evidence of how people lived
- **Social History**: Historical research focused on the everyday experience of ordinary people rather than political or military events
- **Anachronism**: A detail that is historically or culturally incorrect for the period being depicted — a common problem in period fiction
- **Cultural Sensitivity Research**: Investigation into the appropriate, respectful, and accurate representation of cultures, communities, or identities that the researcher doesn't personally belong to
- **Period Language**: The vocabulary, idiom, and speech patterns characteristic of a specific historical period; research source for dialogue authenticity

### Sensory and Atmospheric Research
- **Sensory Inventory**: Research findings organized by sensory category (visual, auditory, olfactory, tactile, gustatory) for immediate writing application
- **Atmospheric Research**: Research focused on the emotional and psychological experience of a place, period, or situation — what it felt like to be there
- **Lived Experience Research**: First-person accounts (memoirs, diaries, interviews) that provide experiential rather than historical perspective
- **Environmental Research**: Physical geography, climate, ecology, and built environment that define a setting's sensory reality

### Documentation Practice
- **Source Citation**: Record of where each research finding came from, enabling verification and further research
- **Reliability Rating**: Assessment of source credibility and accuracy confidence
- **Narrative Use Case**: Note connecting each research finding to its specific intended application in the creative work
- **Discovery Flag**: Notation marking unexpectedly valuable or surprising findings that might improve the narrative beyond the original plan

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Research as Delay**: Using research as a psychological avoidance mechanism, continuing to research instead of writing; research has diminishing returns and must eventually serve writing.
- **Showing the Research**: Including research findings as exposition dumps or historical lectures in the narrative rather than integrating them as sensory texture and behavioral authenticity.
- **Wikipedia Dependency**: Treating Wikipedia and similar tertiary sources as sufficient for deep grounding; they are valuable for orientation and pointing toward real sources, not for serious authentication.
- **Single-Source Dependency**: Relying on a single source for important factual claims without cross-verification; single sources are unverified; multiple converging sources build confidence.
- **Cultural Assumption**: Researching a culture or identity from outside without recognizing the limits of desk research and the need for cultural consultation in sensitive areas.
- **Anachronism Blindness**: Assuming that modern assumptions about technology, social norms, and language are historically neutral; nearly everything about daily life changes significantly across generations.
- **Research Without Application Focus**: Gathering information without maintaining clarity about which narrative elements each research finding serves, producing interesting facts that never improve the writing.

## Quality Indicators

- **Narrative Use Case Coverage**: Every significant narrative element that requires research has documented research findings with sources
- **Source Diversity**: Research findings draw from primary sources, academic secondary sources, and sensory/experiential sources, not just tertiary summaries
- **Reliability Documentation**: Source reliability is assessed and documented for each key finding
- **Sensory Completeness**: Research provides findings across multiple sensory categories for important settings and scenes
- **Anachronism Check**: For period work, a systematic anachronism audit has been conducted against the major elements of the setting
- **Discovery Log**: Unexpected, valuable findings have been captured and flagged for the writer's consideration
- **Cultural Consultation Flag**: Research identifies where subject matter expertise beyond desk research is recommended

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Setting Designer**: Setting designer needs atmospheric and sensory research to make locations emotionally resonant as well as factually accurate; creative researcher provides the raw material, setting designer integrates it into narrative space
- **With Worldbuilder**: For secondary-world fiction, worldbuilder needs research into historical analogues, ecological systems, and cultural patterns to build internally consistent worlds; researcher provides the real-world grounding that makes invented worlds feel plausible
- **With Sensitivity Reader**: When research involves cultures, identities, or experiences outside the writer's background, creative researcher identifies the topics requiring sensitivity review and the gaps in desk research that need expert consultation
- **With Copy Editor**: Copy editor verifies factual claims in the finished text; creative researcher's documentation provides the reference for that verification

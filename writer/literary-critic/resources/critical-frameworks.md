# Critical Frameworks and Evaluation Methods

## The Close Reading Protocol

### Level 1: The Sentence
Read each sentence as a self-contained unit of meaning. Attend to:

**Word choice (diction)**:
- Is each word the most precise available? "Walk" vs. "stride" vs. "shuffle" vs. "amble" -- each implies a different body, mood, and relationship to the world
- What register is the language operating in? Formal, informal, colloquial, archaic, technical? Does the register match the narrator and scene?
- Are there words that are working double duty -- literal meaning AND connotative meaning simultaneously? The best prose is dense with this kind of layering

**Syntax (sentence structure)**:
- What kind of sentence is this? Periodic (delays the main clause for suspense), cumulative (main clause first, then modifying elements), balanced (two halves in equilibrium), fragmented?
- What does the syntax enact? A long, winding sentence can perform the experience of being lost. A short, declarative sentence can perform the experience of certainty or shock
- Where is the emphasis? English sentences stress the beginning and especially the end. What word has the writer placed in the position of maximum emphasis?

**Sound**:
- Read aloud. Where do the sounds create friction (hard consonants: k, t, p, g)? Where do they create flow (liquids and nasals: l, m, n, r)?
- Is alliteration used? Sparingly and purposefully, or clunkily and obviously?
- Does the rhythm of stressed and unstressed syllables create a pattern? Does that pattern match the emotional content?

### Level 2: The Paragraph
Read each paragraph as a unit of rhythm and meaning:

- **The topic shift**: Where does the paragraph turn? Does it develop a single idea or drift between ideas?
- **The opening**: Does the first sentence orient the reader? Does it create forward momentum?
- **The closing**: The last sentence of a paragraph is its most emphatic position. What does the writer leave the reader with before the white space?
- **Internal rhythm**: How do the sentences within the paragraph relate to each other in length and structure? A paragraph of uniformly structured sentences is monotonous. Variation creates musicality

### Level 3: The Scene
Read each scene as a dramatic unit:

- **Entry point**: Does the scene begin at the right moment? Many scenes begin too early (with setup the reader doesn't need) or too late (without enough grounding)
- **Central conflict or tension**: What is at stake in this scene? If nothing is at stake, why does the scene exist?
- **Beat structure**: Within the scene, what are the shifts in power, information, emotion, or intention? These beats create the scene's internal rhythm
- **Exit point**: Does the scene end at the right moment? The strongest endings cut away at a moment of peak tension or discovery, not after the tension has resolved

### Level 4: The Architecture
Read the work as a structural whole:

- **Proportionality**: How much narrative space is devoted to each section? Is it proportionate to importance?
- **Causality**: Does each scene cause the next? Or are scenes merely sequential (this happened, then this happened, then this happened)?
- **Symmetry and repetition**: Are there structural echoes -- scenes that mirror or invert each other? Motifs that recur in transformed contexts?
- **The arc**: What is the shape of the overall narrative? Rising action to climax to denouement? A spiral? A mosaic? Does the shape serve the meaning?

## Barthes' Five Codes: Applied Analysis

### How to Read Through the Five Codes Simultaneously

When analyzing a passage, mark each sentence or phrase according to which codes it activates:

**Example passage**: "The door at the end of the corridor was always locked. Mrs. Dempsey had said it led to an old storeroom, but the scratching sounds that came from behind it at night told a different story."

- **Hermeneutic code** (HER): "The door was always locked" raises a question. "The scratching sounds" raises another. These enigmas create forward momentum -- the reader wants to know what's behind the door
- **Proairetic code** (ACT): "The scratching sounds that came at night" implies an ongoing action sequence. Something is happening behind that door. The reader anticipates future action
- **Semic code** (SEM): "Always locked" connotes secrecy, danger, prohibition. "Old storeroom" connotes neglect, abandonment. "Scratching" connotes something alive, possibly trapped, possibly animal, possibly desperate
- **Symbolic code** (SYM): The locked door is a symbolic structure -- the boundary between known and unknown, safe and dangerous, the repressed and the conscious. It participates in the fundamental opposition of the text
- **Cultural code** (REF): The passage invokes the cultural knowledge of Gothic fiction -- locked rooms, mysterious sounds, unreliable explanations from authority figures. The reader who knows this genre recognizes the conventions being activated

### Diagnostic Questions per Code

**Hermeneutic**: What questions does the text raise? How long does it delay the answers? Is the delay productive (building tension) or frustrating (withholding without purpose)? Are the answers satisfying when they arrive?

**Proairetic**: What action sequences are set in motion? Are they completed or interrupted? Does the reader feel the pull of "what happens next"? Is that pull appropriate for the genre?

**Semic**: What character traits and thematic meanings accumulate through connotation? Are the connotations consistent or contradictory? If contradictory, is the contradiction meaningful?

**Symbolic**: What binary oppositions structure the text? (Nature/culture, freedom/imprisonment, truth/deception, innocence/experience.) Are these oppositions explored with nuance, or collapsed into simplistic allegory?

**Cultural**: What shared knowledge does the text assume? Is it assuming too much (losing readers who don't share the reference) or too little (boring readers with explanations they don't need)?

## Genette's Narratological Toolkit: Practical Application

### Order Analysis
Map the relationship between story time (the chronological sequence of events) and discourse time (the order in which they are narrated):

| Story Time | Discourse Time | Device | Effect |
|-----------|---------------|--------|--------|
| Events narrated in chronological order | Linear | Chronological narration | Clarity, forward momentum |
| Past event inserted into present | Disrupts linear flow | Analepsis (flashback) | Context, explanation, irony |
| Future event previewed | Disrupts linear flow | Prolepsis (flash-forward) | Suspense, dramatic irony, fatalism |
| Multiple timelines alternating | Parallel structures | Interleaving | Thematic comparison, complexity |
| Reverse chronology | Inverted | Reverse narration | Mystery structure, inevitability |

**Diagnostic questions**: Is the temporal order serving the story, or is it merely confusing? Does each analepsis earn its disruption by providing essential context or emotional resonance? Does each prolepsis create productive tension?

### Duration Analysis
How narrative time maps to story time:

| Technique | Narrative Time vs. Story Time | Effect |
|-----------|-------------------------------|--------|
| **Scene** | Roughly equal (dialogue, real-time action) | Immediacy, dramatic presence |
| **Summary** | Narrative time << story time (condensed) | Efficiency, transition, temporal sweep |
| **Stretch** | Narrative time >> story time (expanded) | Emphasis, psychological depth |
| **Ellipsis** | Story time passes, narrative time = 0 | Economy, mystery, temporal jump |
| **Pause** | Story time stops, narrative time continues (description, reflection) | Atmosphere, interiority |

**Diagnostic questions**: Are important moments given enough narrative space (scene or stretch)? Are unimportant moments dispatched efficiently (summary or ellipsis)? Does the distribution of duration create appropriate rhythm?

### Focalization Analysis
Who perceives the narrated world?

| Type | Definition | Example | Strength | Limitation |
|------|-----------|---------|----------|------------|
| **Zero** (omniscient) | Narrator knows more than any character | Victorian novels, "God's-eye view" | Complete information, thematic commentary | Can feel distant, authoritative |
| **Internal fixed** | Filtered through one character's consciousness | Most first-person, close third-person | Intimacy, identification | Limited information, potential unreliability |
| **Internal variable** | Shifts between characters' consciousnesses | Multi-POV novels | Multiple perspectives, dramatic irony | Can fracture identification |
| **External** | Narrator knows less than characters | Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants" | Mystery, reader participation | Can feel cold, withholding |

**Diagnostic questions**: Is the focalization consistent within scenes? When it shifts, is the shift signaled clearly? Does the choice of focalization serve the story's needs? Would a different focalization create a stronger effect?

## The Evaluative Framework

### Manuscript Assessment Template

#### Part 1: Orientation
- **What is this text?** Genre, length, apparent ambition, target audience
- **What is it trying to do?** The text's project -- its central questions, themes, emotional aims
- **What genre contract does it establish?** What expectations does it set up in the opening pages?

#### Part 2: Strengths (minimum 3, maximum 5)
For each strength, provide:
- The specific quality being praised
- A concrete example from the text (with page/chapter reference)
- Why this strength matters to the work as a whole

#### Part 3: The Central Issue
- **What is the single most important problem?** The one thing that, if fixed, would most improve the manuscript
- **Why does it matter?** How does it affect the reader's experience?
- **Where does it manifest?** Specific examples
- **Possible approaches** (not prescriptions): Ways the writer might address this

#### Part 4: Supporting Issues (2-4, in priority order)
For each:
- Description of the issue
- Specific examples
- Relationship to the central issue (if any)
- Severity: structural (requires significant revision), craft-level (fixable in line editing), or cosmetic (fixable in copyediting)

#### Part 5: Assessment
- **Overall quality**: Assessed against the text's own ambitions and genre standards
- **Development stage**: First draft / structural revision / line edit / polish
- **Potential**: What this manuscript could become with focused revision
- **Recommended next steps**: The 3 most important things the writer should do next

## Genre-Specific Evaluation Criteria

### Literary Fiction Checklist
- [ ] Prose quality justifies the genre's slower pace
- [ ] Thematic complexity rewards re-reading
- [ ] Characters are psychologically rich and surprising
- [ ] Structure is purposeful, not merely chronological
- [ ] The ending resonates beyond the plot
- [ ] The text has something to say about the human condition

### Thriller/Suspense Checklist
- [ ] Opening hook grabs within the first page
- [ ] Tension escalates through the middle act
- [ ] Pacing rarely sags -- transitions are swift
- [ ] Stakes are clear, personal, and escalating
- [ ] The antagonist is competent and formidable
- [ ] The climax delivers on the promise of the setup
- [ ] Plot logic holds up under scrutiny

### Romance Checklist
- [ ] Both love interests are fully realized characters
- [ ] The romantic tension is palpable and earned
- [ ] Obstacles to the relationship are believable
- [ ] Emotional beats land -- the reader feels the connection
- [ ] The HEA/HFN (happily ever after / happy for now) is satisfying
- [ ] Chemistry is shown through interaction, not told through narration

### Fantasy / Sci-Fi Checklist
- [ ] World-building is internally consistent
- [ ] The speculative element serves a thematic purpose beyond novelty
- [ ] Exposition is integrated, not dumped
- [ ] Characters are not overshadowed by world-building
- [ ] The magic system / technology has clear rules and costs
- [ ] The setting feels lived-in, not designed

### Horror Checklist
- [ ] Atmosphere is established before the threat appears
- [ ] The threat is ambiguous enough to generate dread, not just fear
- [ ] Fear is earned through craft, not through graphic description alone
- [ ] The familiar is defamiliarized (the uncanny)
- [ ] Characters' responses to the threat are psychologically authentic
- [ ] The ending disturbs -- it lingers after the book is closed

### Mystery / Crime Checklist
- [ ] Clues are planted fairly but not obviously
- [ ] The solution is both surprising and inevitable
- [ ] Red herrings are plausible, not arbitrary
- [ ] Procedural details are accurate enough for the genre
- [ ] The detective / protagonist is compelling beyond the case
- [ ] The crime has thematic resonance -- it means something beyond "who did it"

## Common Manuscript Problems and Their Root Causes

### "The story doesn't start until chapter three"
**Root cause**: The writer is warming up. The first two chapters are the writer figuring out the story. The actual narrative begins when the writer finally knows what they're writing about.
**Solution**: Cut the first two chapters. Begin where the story begins. The information from those chapters can be woven in later, if it's needed at all.

### "I don't care about the protagonist"
**Root cause**: Usually not a likability problem but a specificity problem. The character is generic -- they could be anyone. They lack the specific details, contradictions, and distinctive inner life that make a reader invest.
**Solution**: Give the character a specific voice, a specific wound, a specific way of seeing the world that no other character shares. Make them particular.

### "The middle sags"
**Root cause**: The writer has a strong beginning (the setup) and a strong ending (the payoff) but hasn't figured out what happens in between beyond "complications."
**Solution**: The middle should escalate, complicate, and deepen. Each scene should raise the stakes OR deepen our understanding of the characters OR both. If a scene does neither, it doesn't belong.

### "The ending feels unearned"
**Root cause**: The resolution requires a change in the character that the text hasn't sufficiently prepared. The character arrives at wisdom, courage, or love through insufficient cause.
**Solution**: Work backward from the ending. What change does the character undergo? What experiences would be sufficient to produce that change? Are those experiences in the manuscript?

### "The prose is competent but flat"
**Root cause**: The writer is communicating information without creating experience. Every sentence delivers data but none delivers delight, surprise, or beauty.
**Solution**: Identify the passages where the prose must sing (emotional peaks, key descriptions, opening and closing paragraphs) and invest the time to make those passages genuinely beautiful. Not every sentence needs to be a masterpiece, but the key moments must be.

### "The dialogue all sounds the same"
**Root cause**: Every character speaks in the writer's voice. They may say different things but they all say them the same way.
**Solution**: Give each character a distinct verbal signature: characteristic vocabulary, sentence length, degree of directness, use of questions vs. statements, comfort with silence. What a character doesn't say is as distinctive as what they do say.

### "The themes feel heavy-handed"
**Root cause**: The writer is stating the theme rather than embodying it. Characters deliver speeches about the meaning of the story. Symbols are labeled rather than deployed.
**Solution**: Trust the reader. Remove every passage where a character or narrator explicitly states the theme. If the theme is truly present in the story's structure, characters, and imagery, the reader will find it. If it vanishes when you remove the explicit statements, it was never truly in the story -- it was wallpapered on.

## The Critic's Self-Checklist

Before delivering any critique, verify:

- [ ] I have identified what the text is trying to do before judging how well it does it
- [ ] I have named specific strengths, not just problems
- [ ] My critique is specific -- I have cited passages, pages, or scenes
- [ ] I have prioritized -- the writer knows what to fix first
- [ ] I have diagnosed problems, not prescribed solutions (unless solutions are requested)
- [ ] I have evaluated the text by its own genre's standards, not imposed a different genre's standards
- [ ] My tone is honest but not cruel -- I am trying to help, not to impress
- [ ] I have asked myself: "Would this critique make the writer want to revise, or want to quit?" If the latter, recalibrate
- [ ] My feedback is about the work, not about the writer as a person
- [ ] I have checked my own biases -- am I penalizing this text for not being the kind of book I personally prefer?

# Sensitivity Review Guide: Detailed Assessment Frameworks

## The Representation Quality Assessment

### Five Dimensions of Representation Quality

Every character portrayal can be evaluated across five dimensions. A character can score well on some and poorly on others -- the assessment is multidimensional, not a single grade.

**1. Specificity**
Is this character a specific person, or a generalized representative of a group?

| Poor | Adequate | Strong |
|------|----------|--------|
| "She was proud of her heritage" (which heritage? proud how?) | "She kept her grandmother's recipes and cooked Dominican food on Sundays" | "She made her abuela's mangú every Sunday morning, the way she'd been taught at eight years old, though she substituted bacon for the salami because she'd never liked salami and her abuela would have been horrified" |

Specificity creates authenticity. The more specific the detail, the more real the character feels, and the harder it is for the portrayal to collapse into stereotype.

**2. Agency**
Does this character make meaningful choices that affect the plot, or do things happen TO them?

| Poor | Adequate | Strong |
|------|----------|--------|
| Character is rescued by others, reacts to events, serves as obstacle or tool for protagonist | Character makes some choices, has some goals, but is primarily defined in relation to the protagonist | Character has goals independent of the protagonist, makes choices that drive plot, has scenes that serve their story |

Characters from marginalized groups who lack agency are often narrative tools rather than people. The magical minority exists to help the hero. The disabled character exists to inspire. The woman exists to be rescued. Agency is the antidote.

**3. Interiority**
Does the reader have access to this character's inner life, or are they seen only from the outside?

| Poor | Adequate | Strong |
|------|----------|--------|
| Described only in terms of appearance, behavior, and function | Some inner life shown, but primarily in relation to their identity | Rich inner life that includes thoughts, feelings, and concerns beyond their identity markers |

A character seen only from the outside is always at risk of becoming an object. Access to interiority humanizes.

**4. Complexity**
Is this character allowed to be contradictory, flawed, and surprising, or are they defined by a single trait?

| Poor | Adequate | Strong |
|------|----------|--------|
| Defined by one characteristic (the wise elder, the sassy friend, the tragic victim) | Has some dimensions but fundamentally fits a recognizable type | Surprising, contradictory, capable of growth and failure, resists easy categorization |

The demand for "positive representation" can paradoxically create flat characters. A Black character who is always wise and dignified is just as flat as one who is always threatening. Complexity is the goal, not positivity.

**5. Context**
Does the character exist within a cultural context that feels authentic, or are they culturally homeless?

| Poor | Adequate | Strong |
|------|----------|--------|
| Identity mentioned but no cultural context provided | Some cultural markers present (food, holidays, family structures) | Character embedded in a living cultural context with its own logic, contradictions, and specificity |

## Harmful Trope Catalog

### Racial and Ethnic Tropes

**The Magical Negro/Minority**:
- Definition: A person of color who exists in the narrative solely to help the white protagonist, often with mystical or intuitive wisdom
- The test: Does this character have goals, conflicts, and a life independent of the protagonist? If not, they're a narrative tool
- Not every wise person of color is this trope -- the issue is when wisdom-dispensing is their ONLY function
- Fix: Give the character their own story arc, conflicts, and motivations

**The Model Minority**:
- Definition: Asian characters portrayed as uniformly studious, quiet, successful, and non-confrontational
- The test: Is this character's academic/professional success the defining element of their personality? Are they allowed to be loud, angry, struggling, or average?
- Fix: Give the character flaws, failures, and interests beyond academic achievement

**White Savior**:
- Definition: A white character who rescues or "civilizes" people of color who apparently cannot help themselves
- The test: Could this story exist without the white character? Do the characters of color have agency?
- Fix: Center the agency of the characters being "saved"; let them drive their own liberation

**The Single Story**:
- Definition: Treating an entire continent, culture, or ethnicity as one homogeneous experience
- Common instances: "Africa" as one place (it's 54 countries); "Asian" as one identity; "Latin American" as one culture
- Fix: Research the specific country, region, and community. A Kenyan character and a Nigerian character have different cultural contexts

**Exotic Other**:
- Definition: Characters from non-Western cultures portrayed as mysterious, mystical, or fundamentally unknowable
- The test: Is this character's culture presented with the same mundane specificity as Western characters' cultures?
- Fix: Ground the character in the ordinary details of their life. Cultures are lived daily, not performed

### Gender Tropes

**The Strong Female Character Problem**:
- The trope: "Strong" defined as traditionally masculine (fights, doesn't cry, suppresses emotion)
- The underlying issue: Implying that traditionally feminine traits (nurturing, emotional expressiveness, collaboration) are weakness
- The fix: Allow women characters the full range of human strength -- emotional, intellectual, moral, physical -- without requiring them to perform masculinity to be respected

**Fridging (Women in Refrigerators)**:
- Definition: A female character killed, injured, or depowered to motivate a male character
- The test: Is this character's suffering primarily in service of another character's story?
- Fix: Give the character her own story value beyond her function as someone else's motivation

**The Exceptional Woman**:
- Definition: A female character who succeeds by being "not like other girls" -- implicitly devaluing femininity
- The test: Does this character's strength require distancing herself from other women?
- Fix: Allow female characters to be competent AND feminine, or competent AND connected to other women

### LGBTQ+ Tropes

**Bury Your Gays**:
- Definition: Queer characters die at disproportionate rates, often shortly after finding happiness
- The test: Do the queer characters survive at the same rate as straight characters? Is their death narratively necessary?
- Note: Queer characters CAN die in fiction -- the concern is when it's disproportionate or when death is the default queer narrative

**The Coming-Out Story as Only Story**:
- Definition: A queer character's queerness is their entire storyline
- The fix: Let queer characters have adventures, solve mysteries, fall in love, and experience the full range of fictional experiences -- their queerness being one element, not the whole

**Queerbaiting**:
- Definition: Suggesting queer attraction for audience engagement without ever committing to it
- The test: Is the queerness textual (confirmed in the narrative) or subtextual (hinted at, never confirmed)?

**The Tragic Queer**:
- Definition: Queer characters whose stories invariably end in suffering, rejection, or death
- Fix: Allow queer characters happy endings as often as straight characters get them

### Disability Tropes

**Inspiration Porn**:
- Definition: Disabled characters whose primary narrative function is to inspire able-bodied characters (or readers) by "overcoming" their disability
- The test: Is this character's disability the source of their narrative value, or are they valued for who they are as a person?
- Fix: Disability is part of the character's experience, not their purpose in the story

**The Magical Cure**:
- Definition: Disability "fixed" through magic, love, willpower, or medical miracle
- Why it's harmful: Implies disability is inherently tragic and must be eliminated for happiness
- Fix: Allow disabled characters to live full, meaningful lives with their disability -- not despite it

**Supercrip**:
- Definition: A disabled person who "overcomes" their disability through extraordinary effort or ability
- Why it's harmful: Implies ordinary disabled people aren't trying hard enough
- Fix: Allow disabled characters to be ordinary -- to struggle, to need help, to succeed at some things and fail at others

**Disability as Metaphor**:
- Definition: Using disability as a stand-in for a thematic concept (blindness = ignorance, deafness = willful refusal to listen)
- Fix: If a character is blind, they're blind. Their blindness is a physical reality, not a symbol for something else.

### Mental Health Tropes

**The Violent Mentally Ill Person**:
- Definition: Equating mental illness with violence, danger, or moral corruption
- Reality: People with mental illness are far more likely to be victims of violence than perpetrators
- Fix: If you include mentally ill characters, portray their conditions accurately. Most people with depression are not dangerous; they're exhausted

**Anxiety as Quirky**:
- Definition: Portraying anxiety as an endearing personality trait rather than a debilitating condition
- Fix: Anxiety involves panic attacks, avoidance behaviors, physical symptoms, and significant life interference. It's not "being nervous in a cute way"

**The Clean Break Recovery**:
- Definition: Mental health recovery as a single breakthrough moment after which everything is fine
- Fix: Recovery is nonlinear. It involves setbacks, medication adjustments, good days and bad days. Show the process, not just the "fixed" endpoint

## Feedback Severity Calibration

### Critical: Change Required
- Active dehumanization of a group
- Use of slurs without clear narrative justification (in-world context, character voice, historical accuracy)
- Tropes that cause measurable harm (e.g., depicting suicide methods in graphic detail)
- Representations that perpetuate dangerous misinformation (mental illness = violence)

### Major: Strongly Recommend Change
- Characters that are primarily harmful tropes (magical minority, inspiration porn)
- Cultural inaccuracies that demonstrate lack of research
- Trauma handled without care or purpose
- Tokenism (one character "represents" an entire group without depth)

### Minor: Suggest Improvement
- Surface-level representation that could be deepened with specificity
- Mild reliance on stereotypes that could be complicated
- Missed opportunities for intersectional depth
- Language that's slightly outdated but not harmful

### Note: For Author Awareness
- Evolving terminology the author may not be aware of
- Cultural context that could enrich the portrayal
- Resources for further research
- Praise for specific representation successes

## The Sensitivity Report Structure

```markdown
# Sensitivity Review: [Manuscript Title]
# Reviewer: Sensitivity Reader
# Date: [Date]
# Focus Areas: [Identities/communities covered in this review]

## Overall Assessment
[2-3 paragraphs: What the manuscript does well in representation, what needs attention, and the overall quality level on the sensitivity spectrum]

## Strengths
[Specific examples of strong representation, authentic detail, or thoughtful handling]

## Concerns

### [Concern Title] -- [Severity: Critical/Major/Minor/Note]
**Location**: Chapter [N], [specific passage or scene description]
**Concern**: [What the issue is]
**Impact**: [Why it matters -- how affected readers may experience this]
**Context**: [Is this a known trope? Historical context?]
**Suggestion**: [Specific, actionable alternative that preserves the author's intent]

[Repeat for each concern]

## Recommendations
[Prioritized list of the most important changes, with rationale]

## Resources
[Books, articles, organizations, and sensitivity readers from specific communities for further consultation]

## Limitations
[What this review does NOT cover -- reminder that one reader cannot speak for all communities, and additional readers may be needed for specific identities]
```

## Working with Authors

### Setting Expectations
- Sensitivity reading provides informed perspective, not absolute authority
- The author retains creative control -- the sensitivity reader offers expertise, not mandates
- Multiple sensitivity readers may disagree -- this is normal and reflects the complexity of lived experience
- The goal is improvement, not perfection

### Receiving Pushback
- If an author disagrees with a concern, engage respectfully. They may have context you lack
- Distinguish between "I disagree this is a problem" (discuss further) and "I know it's a problem but it serves the story" (their call, but ensure they understand the impact)
- Document your reasoning. If the author publishes a work with known representation issues, your documented concern protects both of you
- Never insist. Recommend, explain, and trust the author to make informed decisions

### Cultural Humility
- You cannot be an expert on every identity and community
- Acknowledge the limits of your own knowledge
- Recommend additional readers from specific communities when the manuscript portrays experiences outside your expertise
- Stay current on evolving language, understanding, and community preferences
- Be willing to say "I'm not the right reader for this specific concern"

# Best Practices: Dialogue Specialist

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality dialogue writing, character voice distinction, subtext craft, and conversational authenticity work.

## Design Principles

- **Dialogue Is Action**: Every line of dialogue must do something — advance the plot, reveal character, establish relationship, introduce conflict, or change the dynamics between speakers. Lines that don't act should be cut.
- **People Don't Say What They Mean**: Authentic dialogue operates through indirection. Characters talk around their real feelings, pursue hidden agendas, protect themselves from vulnerability, and communicate through implication. Subtext is the real conversation.
- **Voice Distinction Is Non-Negotiable**: Every character in a scene should sound different enough that dialogue tags could be removed and readers still know who's speaking. Vocabulary range, sentence length preference, question frequency, and topic avoidance are fingerprints.
- **Dialogue Compresses Time**: Real conversation is redundant, padded, and inefficient. Dialogue in fiction is a highly compressed, intensified simulation of conversation that preserves the feel without the waste.
- **Beats Control Rhythm**: Dialogue without beats is a ping-pong of talking heads. Action beats — what characters do while talking — control rhythm, reveal what's not being said, and ground the conversation in the scene's physical reality.
- **Said Is Invisible**: The attribution tag "said" disappears from readers' perception; it's punctuation. Elaborate alternatives (exclaimed, intoned, breathed) call attention to themselves and interrupt the dialogue's rhythm.
- **Silence Speaks**: What a character doesn't say — the deflection, the non-answer, the subject change, the pause that goes on too long — is often the most important dialogue in the scene.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Subtext Architecture**: Before writing a scene's dialogue, define what each character actually wants from this conversation, what they're afraid to say directly, and what they'll never admit. The dialogue is their attempt to get what they want while protecting what they fear.
- **Voice Profile System**: For each character, define five voice parameters — vocabulary range (simple to complex), sentence length preference, question frequency, primary topic area (what they tend to talk about), and avoidance topic (what they never discuss). These parameters generate consistent voice across all scenes.
- **The Three-Track Model**: Dialogue operates simultaneously on three tracks — the surface track (what's literally being said), the subtext track (what's really being communicated), and the theme track (how this conversation serves the story's thematic argument). Strong dialogue operates on all three.
- **Argument Architecture**: For conflict scenes, structure the argument as escalation — opening position, first provocation, counterattack, escalation, crisis point, and outcome (resolution, retreat, explosion, revelation). Unstructured arguments wander without building tension.
- **Silence and Evasion Toolkit**: Techniques for representing what's not said — the non-answer, the redirect, the joke that deflects, the topic change, the beat that goes on too long, the literal question answered with an unrelated truth.
- **Dialect and Register Differentiation**: Character voice variation through dialect (regional vocabulary, grammatical variation) versus register (formal/informal, technical/plain) versus idiolect (individual speech habits). Each operates differently and serves different characterization purposes.
- **Dialogue Compression Technique**: Take a passage of realistic-feeling conversation and cut it by 50%, removing every line that isn't doing work. The surviving lines are the actual scene; the rest is padding.
- **Beat Placement Method**: Map the scene's emotional beats against the dialogue, placing action beats at inflection points where the emotional direction changes. Beats are not decoration; they're punctuation for the emotional score.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Dialogue Structure
- **Subtext**: The real meaning, emotion, or agenda beneath what a character says literally; the gap between surface meaning and actual communication
- **Dialogue Beat**: Stage direction embedded in dialogue — an action, pause, or physical description that occurs during or between spoken lines
- **Attribution / Tag**: The "she said" / "he asked" notation that identifies speakers; "said" is standard and invisible; alternatives draw attention
- **Direct Address**: Using the interlocutor's name in dialogue; realistic-sounding when sparse, but characters calling each other by name in every other line is an obvious writerly habit
- **Dialogue Block**: A passage of dialogue as distinguished from the surrounding prose narration

### Voice and Characterization
- **Idiolect**: An individual character's distinctive speech patterns, vocabulary preferences, and grammatical habits
- **Register**: The level of formality or technicality in a speaker's language; register shifts when speakers move between contexts (professional to intimate)
- **Dialect**: Regional or social variation in language patterns — vocabulary, pronunciation (represented orthographically), grammatical structure
- **Verbal Tic**: A recurring speech habit specific to a character — a favorite word, a habitual question, a characteristic phrase
- **Voice Distinction**: The degree to which each character's dialogue can be identified by voice alone, without attribution tags

### Rhetoric and Conversation Dynamics
- **Interruption**: One character cutting off another; used to show power, urgency, emotional flooding, or deliberate aggression
- **Non-Answer**: Responding to a question with something that doesn't answer it, usually while appearing to; reveals evasion, discomfort, or manipulation
- **Deflection**: Moving the conversation away from a threatening topic, often through humor, redirection, or a counterattack question
- **Monologue**: Extended speech by a single character; risks stopping the scene's momentum; should be reserved for high-stakes moments with clear motivation
- **Talking Heads**: Dialogue between characters with no physical grounding, no beats, and no spatial reality; the classic sign of dialogue that has floated free of the scene

### Said Bookisms
- **Said Bookism**: The use of colorful attribution tags as alternatives to "said" — expounded, expostulated, breathed, smiled; generally considered an amateur marker because they interrupt the dialogue's flow
- **Invisible Tags**: "Said" and "asked" — the appropriate defaults because readers stop seeing them
- **Beat Attribution**: Using an action beat to attribute dialogue implicitly ("She set down her cup. 'I'm leaving.'") — more sophisticated than tag-only attribution

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **On-the-Nose Dialogue**: Characters saying exactly what they feel, think, and want directly; eliminates subtext and makes characters read as naive, emotionally incontinent, or simply unreal.
- **As-You-Know-Bob Exposition**: Characters explaining to each other things they would both already know, purely for the reader's benefit ("As you know, the treaty was signed in 1847..."); is an obvious and immersion-breaking technique.
- **Voice Homogeneity**: All characters sounding identical — same vocabulary level, same sentence length, same speech rhythms — making dialogue a talking-heads exercise with no characterization.
- **Ping-Pong Dialogue**: Rapid back-and-forth exchanges with no beats, no pacing variation, and no physical grounding; characters cease to be embodied people in a room.
- **Over-Explained Subtext**: Having characters articulate their subtext explicitly after establishing it implicitly ("I was talking about more than the weather, you know"); defeats the purpose of subtext.
- **Said Bookism Excess**: Using a new vivid attribution tag every few lines; the variety becomes its own tedious pattern.
- **Dialogue as Information Delivery**: Writing dialogue scenes primarily to transfer plot information to the reader rather than to dramatize character through conversation; the most efficient scenes deliver information as a byproduct of character interaction.

## Quality Indicators

- **Tag-Free Readability**: The scene's dialogue can be read without attribution tags and speakers remain distinguishable 85%+ of the time
- **Subtext Presence**: Every dialogue scene has a documented subtext layer — what each character is actually communicating beneath the surface
- **Zero On-the-Nose Lines**: No characters are stating their emotional state, desires, or fears directly when subtext would be more effective
- **Beat Distribution**: Action beats are distributed through dialogue, not clustered at scene openings or omitted entirely
- **Voice Parameter Compliance**: Character dialogue complies with their established voice profile (vocabulary, sentence length, topic/avoidance patterns)
- **Compression Test**: The scene's dialogue could not be cut by 50% without losing something essential — indicating it's already compressed
- **Attribution Economy**: The vast majority of dialogue uses "said" and "asked" as attribution, with alternatives used deliberately for specific effect

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Character Designer**: Character designer provides the psychological profile and voice parameters that dialogue specialist translates into actual speech patterns; every character's vocabulary, register, and avoidance topics derive from their wound/want/need architecture
- **With Character Psychologist**: Character psychologist identifies the defense mechanisms and avoidance patterns that should surface in dialogue; the deflections, non-answers, and subject changes a character uses are expressions of their psychological defenses
- **With Prose Stylist**: Prose stylist manages narration rhythm and language; dialogue specialist manages spoken language; the two must collaborate on how dialogue and narration interact rhythmically and tonally
- **With Pacing Specialist**: Scene pacing is managed through the interplay of dialogue, beats, and narration; dialogue specialist and pacing specialist must coordinate on scene tempo and the allocation of space to conversation versus action

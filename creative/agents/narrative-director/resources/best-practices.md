# Best Practices: Narrative Director

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality creative vision management, tonal control, cross-contributor coordination, and quality calibration work.

## Design Principles

- **Vision Is Seeing What Isn't There Yet**: The director holds a clear image of what the project wants to become — its ideal final state — and guides every decision and contributor toward that image.
- **Tonal Control Is the Primary Responsibility**: In multi-contributor creative work, the director's most essential function is maintaining tonal consistency. Different contributors write in different registers; the director is the one who hears when a piece is in the wrong key.
- **Direction, Not Execution**: The director tells specialists what needs to be different and why, but does not rewrite their work. Direction creates better specialist work; rewriting replaces it.
- **Quality Calibration Is the Rarest Skill**: Anyone can say "good" or "bad." The director can say why, can place work on a quality spectrum, and can identify the specific intervention that would move work from "not there yet" to "exceptional."
- **Creative Risk Is a Portfolio Problem**: Manage originality vs. accessibility, surprise vs. satisfaction across the project as a whole. Individual decisions should be evaluated against the project's overall risk portfolio, not in isolation.
- **The Vision Statement Before the Brief**: The creative brief describes what is wanted; the vision statement describes what the project should feel like when experienced. The director writes the vision statement and returns to it constantly.
- **The Gap Between Concept and Execution Is Where Projects Fail**: The director's vigilance is concentrated in this gap — ensuring that execution realizes the concept rather than settling for competent work that misses the creative target.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Vision Statement Method**: At project start, write a 200-word description of the experience the finished work should create — not what it says, but what it does to the audience. Use this as the constant reference point for all subsequent creative decisions.
- **Quality Calibration Framework**: Four-level assessment — (1) Not there yet (identifies the core problem), (2) Getting there (specific prioritized feedback), (3) Good enough (explicit judgment about whether good enough is acceptable here), (4) Exceptional (protective stance — do not over-edit).
- **Tonal Palette Definition**: For each project, define the primary tone (dominant emotional register), secondary tone (used for depth and contrast), and accent tone (used sparingly for emphasis). Document each with examples from existing work.
- **Contributor Strength Mapping**: Know each specialist's tendencies and blind spots — prose stylists may prioritize sound over clarity; plot developers may squeeze out breathing room; character designers may resist narrative needs. Direct to offset tendencies.
- **Curation Protocol**: Regularly assess which specialist output should be used as-is, which needs directional adjustment, and which should be replaced with a different approach. Curation is the director's core curatorial skill.
- **Creative Brief to Vision Translation**: Translate client/stakeholder requests into vision statements by identifying the real underlying need beneath the stated want. The brief says "write a mystery"; the vision says "create the experience of being two steps behind the detective until you're one step ahead."
- **Tonal Consistency Review**: Before integration of specialist outputs, read all pieces together for tonal coherence. Identify passages that sound like different writers, different registers, or different emotional frequencies than the project established.
- **Anti-Slop Standards Enforcement**: Apply the anti-slop framework to all creative output — no false agency, no throat-clearing, no vague declaratives, no business jargon, active voice, varied rhythm, cut quotables. These patterns are directorial quality gates.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Vision and Direction
- **Vision Statement**: A description of the experience the finished work should create for the audience; the director's north star document
- **Creative Brief**: Stakeholder description of what they want; the stated request that the director translates into creative vision
- **The Real Need**: The underlying desire beneath the stated brief; what the requester actually wants to feel when they receive the finished work
- **Creative Risk Portfolio**: The aggregate of creative risks across a project — how original, surprising, and unconventional it is overall, balanced against accessibility and satisfaction
- **Tonal Contract**: The implicit agreement with the audience established in the work's opening; the emotional frequency the audience is tuned to expect

### Tonal Management
- **Primary Tone**: The dominant emotional register of the work; the most frequent tonal note
- **Secondary Tone**: The contrasting or deepening tone used to prevent monotony and add dimension
- **Accent Tone**: Tonal variation used deliberately and sparingly for emphasis, humor, or disruption
- **Register**: The formality level and social context of language — academic, conversational, poetic, technical, intimate
- **Tonal Drift**: Gradual, unintentional movement away from the established tonal register over the course of a long project or through multiple contributors

### Quality Assessment
- **Not There Yet**: Work with fundamental problems — wrong tone, unclear purpose, structural failure — requiring identification of the core issue
- **Getting There**: Structurally sound work with uneven execution requiring specific, prioritized feedback
- **Good Enough**: Work meeting the brief and serving its purpose; requires explicit judgment about whether the project needs better than good enough
- **Exceptional**: Work that exceeds expectations with its own life; protective stance — stop editing
- **Quality Calibration**: The skill of accurately placing work on the quality spectrum and articulating specifically why it sits where it does

### Creative Leadership
- **Direction vs. Execution**: Direction is telling specialists what to change and why; execution is changing it yourself. Directors direct; specialists execute.
- **Curation**: Selecting, arranging, and integrating specialist outputs; knowing when work is right as-is, needs adjustment, or needs replacement
- **Vision Drift**: The gradual erosion of creative vision under the pressure of feedback, iteration, and compromise; the director's vigilance problem
- **Ego Direction**: Directing work toward the director's personal preferences rather than the project's needs; the fundamental directorial failure

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Direction by Veto**: Only saying what's wrong without articulating what right looks like; specialists need direction toward something, not just away from the current state.
- **Over-Specification**: Dictating every creative choice and leaving no room for specialist expertise; the director defines what and why, the specialist defines how.
- **Tonal Blindness**: Not hearing when tone shifts between sections or contributors; the director must have calibrated tonal perception.
- **Vision Drift**: Losing sight of the original vision through accumulated iterations, compromises, and feedback; the vision statement exists to prevent this.
- **The Good-Enough Trap**: Accepting work that meets the brief but doesn't have life; sometimes good enough isn't good enough, and the director must recognize when the project needs the extra push.
- **Genre Cliché Traps**: Fantasy mock-medieval dialogue, sci-fi technobabble, romance perfect physical descriptions — the director recognizes and redirects away from genre clichés that substitute for genuine creative work.
- **Rewriting Instead of Directing**: Taking a specialist's work and rewriting it rather than directing them to revise it; rewriting damages the specialist relationship and produces work without specialist expertise.

## Quality Indicators

- **Vision Statement Clarity**: The project vision statement can be summarized in one sentence and distinguishes this project from generic descriptions of the same genre
- **Tonal Palette Documentation**: The project's primary, secondary, and accent tones are documented with examples before specialist work begins
- **Specialist Feedback Specificity**: Directorial feedback cites specific passages, identifies specific techniques, and articulates specific effects — not vague impressions
- **Quality Level Accuracy**: Post-project assessment shows that directorial quality ratings during production accurately predicted final audience reception
- **Contributor Coordination**: Specialist outputs, when integrated, read as a coherent whole rather than a collection of individually competent but tonally mismatched pieces
- **Vision Preservation**: The completed project's emotional experience matches the vision statement written at project start
- **Anti-Slop Compliance**: All integrated creative output has been reviewed against anti-slop standards and flagged patterns addressed before delivery

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With All Specialist Agents**: The director coordinates every creative specialist — prose stylist, character designer, plot developer, dialogue specialist, etc. — maintaining tonal coherence and vision alignment across all their outputs
- **With Editor**: Editor operates at the manuscript level implementing the director's vision; director and editor align on structural priorities before the editor engages; editorial letters go through director for tonal and thematic alignment review
- **With Story Architect**: Story architect designs structural architecture; director validates that the architecture serves the emotional experience described in the vision statement
- **With Narrative Designer**: Narrative designer engineers moment-to-moment reading experience; director ensures those design choices serve the broader creative vision rather than becoming technically clever at the expense of emotional impact

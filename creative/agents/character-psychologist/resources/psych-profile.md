# Psychological Profile Template and Assessment Frameworks

## The Deep Psychological Profile

### Section 1: Attachment Foundation

#### Early Attachment History
- **Primary caregiver(s)**: Who was present? Who was absent?
- **Caregiver responsiveness**: Was the caregiver consistently attuned (secure), inconsistently available (anxious), emotionally unavailable (avoidant), or frightening (disorganized)?
- **Resulting attachment style**: Secure / Anxious-Preoccupied / Dismissive-Avoidant / Fearful-Avoidant
- **Internal working model**: What did the child conclude about themselves and relationships?
  - "Am I worthy of love?" (Yes = secure/anxious, No = avoidant/disorganized)
  - "Can others be trusted?" (Yes = secure/anxious, No = avoidant/disorganized)

#### Attachment in Adult Relationships
- **Pattern with romantic partners**: Pursue or withdraw? Anxiously monitor or emotionally detach?
- **Pattern with friends**: Many surface connections or few deep ones? Trust easily or with difficulty?
- **Pattern with authority figures**: Compliant, defiant, or ambivalent?
- **Deactivation triggers**: What makes this character shut down emotionally?
- **Activation triggers**: What makes this character become hypervigilant about connection?

### Section 2: Defense Architecture

#### Primary Defense Mechanism
The habitual, automatic response to psychological threat:

| Defense | How It Manifests | What It Protects Against | Cost |
|---------|-----------------|------------------------|------|
| Denial | Cannot perceive threatening reality | Overwhelm, collapse | Increasingly disconnected from reality |
| Projection | Attributes own feelings to others | Acknowledging unacceptable self | Misreads others, creates conflict |
| Splitting | All-good or all-bad perception | Ambiguity, complexity | Unstable relationships, idealization/devaluation |
| Repression | Cannot remember traumatic material | Re-experiencing trauma | Unexplained symptoms, triggers |
| Displacement | Redirects emotion to safer target | Confronting the real source | Damages innocent relationships |
| Rationalization | Logical explanation for emotional behavior | Feeling out of control | Self-deception, inability to change |
| Reaction formation | Behaves opposite to true feeling | Acknowledging forbidden desire | Exhaustion, inauthenticity |
| Intellectualization | Treats emotions analytically | Feeling the feeling | Emotional isolation |
| Humor | Makes everything funny | Vulnerability | Intimacy avoidance, trivialization |
| Sublimation | Channels into productive activity | Destructive expression | Workaholism, burnout |

#### Defense Sequence Under Escalating Threat
1. **Low threat**: Character uses primary defense smoothly. Others may not notice
2. **Moderate threat**: Primary defense intensifies. Others notice something is off
3. **High threat**: Primary defense fails. Character falls back to secondary defense (often more primitive)
4. **Overwhelming threat**: All defenses collapse. Character's raw wound is exposed. This is the climactic moment in character-driven fiction

## Psychological Authenticity Checklist

Before finalizing a character's psychology:

- [ ] Behavior is consistent with attachment style (not random)
- [ ] Defense mechanisms are age-appropriate and proportionate to threat
- [ ] Trauma responses match the type and severity of traumatic experience
- [ ] Cognitive distortions are consistent and traceable to developmental experiences
- [ ] Relationship patterns follow from attachment style and relational history
- [ ] Emotional responses are proportionate to the character's history (what seems disproportionate to the current situation makes sense given the past)
- [ ] Growth and change follow psychologically realistic timelines (no overnight transformations)
- [ ] Mental health conditions, if present, are accurately portrayed with proper research
- [ ] The character is a person first and a psychological profile second -- the psychology serves the character, not the reverse
- [ ] Contradictions within the character are psychologically explicable (not arbitrary)
- [ ] The character's psychology creates behavior that is simultaneously surprising and inevitable

## Section 8: Motivational Psychology (Maslow)

### Hierarchy of Needs as Character Driver
Maslow's hierarchy provides a map of character motivation based on which needs are met and which are threatened:

| Level | Need | When Unmet | Character Behavior |
|-------|------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Physiological | Food, water, shelter | Survival mode | Desperate, resourceful, all higher concerns disappear |
| 2. Safety | Physical security, stability | Hypervigilance | Controlling, hoarding, risk-averse, routine-dependent |
| 3. Belonging | Love, friendship, community | Loneliness | People-pleasing, joining groups indiscriminately, jealousy |
| 4. Esteem | Achievement, recognition | Inadequacy | Overcompensation, status-seeking, fragile ego |
| 5. Self-actualization | Purpose, creativity, meaning | Existential crisis | Restlessness, depression, searching |

### Using the Hierarchy in Fiction
- **Threat to a lower level overrides higher needs**: A self-actualized artist who loses their home stops creating and starts surviving. The regression reveals what really matters
- **Characters at different levels create friction**: A character focused on safety cannot understand one focused on self-actualization, and vice versa
- **The hierarchy shifts through the story**: A character may begin at Level 4 (seeking esteem) and be forced down to Level 2 (fighting for safety), then climb back up transformed
- **False needs**: Characters often pursue needs at the wrong level -- seeking esteem when they actually lack belonging, or seeking belonging when they have not established safety

## Section 9: Grief and Loss (Adapted from Kübler-Ross)

### The Five Responses (Non-Linear, Non-Sequential)
These are not stages in a linear progression -- they are responses that may occur in any order, simultaneously, repeatedly, or not at all:

- **Denial**: "This isn't happening." Protection against overwhelm. In fiction, denial creates dramatic irony -- the reader sees what the character cannot
- **Anger**: "This isn't fair." The first acknowledgment that something has happened, expressed as fury. May be directed at the lost person, at fate, at bystanders, at the self
- **Bargaining**: "If only I had..." The mind searching for a way to undo the irreversible. Magical thinking, obsessive counterfactuals, deals with God or fate
- **Depression**: The weight of the loss fully felt. Not clinical depression but appropriate sadness of confronting irreversible loss
- **Acceptance**: Not "I'm fine with it" but "This happened, and I must live in the world where it happened." The character integrates the loss into their identity

### Grief as Character Transformation
Loss forces identity change. The character who loses a spouse is no longer a wife -- who is she now? Grief is not just sadness; it is identity crisis. The character who emerges from grief is a different person than the one who entered it.

### Writing Grief Authentically
- Grief comes in waves, not continuously. Mundane moments interrupt grief; grief interrupts mundane moments
- Long-term grief becomes private, hidden, triggered by unexpected sensory details -- a song, a smell, finding a note in a coat pocket
- Each character grieves differently based on attachment style: anxious attachment creates desperate grief; avoidant creates controlled grief; disorganized creates chaotic grief
- People in grief do strange things: reorganize closets at 3 AM, laugh at funerals, forget to eat, become suddenly generous or cruel

## Section 10: Group Psychology

### How Groups Change Behavior
Characters behave differently in groups than alone:

- **Deindividuation**: In crowds, personal identity weakens. People do things they would never do alone -- mob violence, but also collective joy
- **Groupthink**: Cohesive groups pressure toward consensus, suppressing dissent. Characters in groupthink ignore evidence and demonize outsiders
- **Bystander effect**: The more people present, the less likely any individual helps. A character is MORE likely to help alone than in a group
- **Conformity (Asch)**: People will deny their own perception to match group consensus. A character may agree with something they can SEE is wrong
- **Social facilitation**: Performance improves on simple tasks and worsens on complex tasks when others watch

### Status Hierarchies
Every group develops a status hierarchy that affects behavior:
- **High-status**: Taking up space, interrupting, speaking first, maintaining eye contact, comfortable silence
- **Low-status**: Minimizing space, deferring, waiting to speak, breaking eye contact, filling silence
- **Status challenges**: When a low-status character challenges a high-status one, the group dynamics shift. How the hierarchy responds reveals its nature

## Integration: From Profile to Behavior

### The Synthesis Question
After completing a profile, ask: "Given everything I know about this character's attachment style, defense mechanisms, trauma history, and cognitive distortions, what would this specific person do in THIS specific situation?"

The answer should be:
1. **Consistent** with the profile (even if surprising on the surface)
2. **Specific** (not "they would be upset" but "they would intellectualize while their hands shook")
3. **Multi-layered** (behavior serves multiple psychological functions simultaneously)
4. **Revealable** (the reader can understand the behavior retroactively once they know the backstory)

### The Contradiction Check
If a character's behavior always perfectly matches their profile, the profile has replaced the character. Real people are inconsistent. They act against type. They surprise themselves. The profile should explain 80% of behavior and leave 20% for the irreducible mystery of individual consciousness.

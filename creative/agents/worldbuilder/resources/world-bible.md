# World Bible Framework

A world bible is not a template to fill out — it is the iceberg beneath your story. This framework guides the construction of a world that feels lived-in, internally consistent, and thematically resonant. Not every section applies to every world. Build what your story needs, then build twenty percent more for depth.

## I. World Identity

### Core Concept
The world in one paragraph. Not plot, not setting description — the *idea* of the world. What makes it fundamentally different from every other fantasy/sci-fi world? What is its central tension, its defining strangeness, its animating question?

Example: "A world where magic is a finite resource being consumed faster than it regenerates, and civilization is the addiction that's killing it." That's a world concept. "There are elves and dwarves and a dark lord" is not.

### Thematic Resonance
What is this world *about*? The world should embody the story's themes at every level. If the story is about corruption, the world's magic should corrupt. If the story is about memory, the world should have amnesia — lost histories, forgotten languages, ruins no one can explain. The world argues for the story's meaning through its very structure.

### Tone and Aesthetic
The world's personality. Is it a world of fading grandeur (Gormenghast)? Brutal pragmatism (Westeros)? Baroque strangeness (Bas-Lag)? Cosmic indifference (Lovecraft's cosmos)? Grounded humanism (Earthsea)? Tone guides every creative decision from architecture to naming.

### The Rules of Reality
What is fundamentally true in this world that is not true in ours? What is fundamentally true in ours that is not true here? These are your axioms — everything else derives from them. If the dead can speak, that changes law, inheritance, testimony, grief, religion, and murder. State your axioms and follow their implications ruthlessly.

## II. Geography and Ecology

### Physical World
Geography is destiny. Build the physical world first because it constrains everything that follows.

**Terrain and geology**: Mountain ranges, river systems, coastlines, deserts, forests, plains. Mountains follow tectonic logic. Rivers flow downhill, merge, and reach the sea. Cities cluster at river confluences, natural harbors, mountain passes, and resource deposits. Ask: why is this city *here* and not fifty miles east?

**Climate zones**: Determined by latitude, altitude, ocean currents, and prevailing winds. Climate determines agriculture, which determines diet, health, population density, and cultural character. Tropical, temperate, arid, arctic — each zone produces distinct civilizations.

**Resource distribution**: Where is the arable land? Fresh water? Timber? Metal ore? Magical resources? Resource distribution explains trade routes, territorial conflict, wealth inequality, and migration patterns. Scarcity drives history.

### Ecology
**Flora**: What grows, where, and why it matters. Staple crops determine cuisine, settlement patterns, and seasonal rhythms. Medicinal plants shape healing practices. Timber availability determines architecture and shipbuilding. Poisonous plants shape cultural knowledge.

**Fauna**: What animals exist, which are domesticated, which are dangerous, which are sacred. Domesticated animals determine transportation, warfare, agriculture, and diet. Draft animals enable plowing. Riding animals enable cavalry. Pack animals enable trade routes. No horses? Your military and logistics look completely different.

**Natural hazards**: Earthquakes, volcanoes, floods, droughts, storms, wildfires, predator species. These shape architecture (earthquake-resistant building), theology (gods of storms), cultural memory (the Great Flood), and political response (who organized the evacuation?).

**Seasons and cycles**: Day length, growing seasons, migratory patterns, tidal cycles, celestial events. These determine calendars, festivals, planting cycles, and the rhythm of daily and annual life.

## III. Economic Systems

### Production
What is produced, by whom, using what methods? Agriculture, mining, manufacturing, crafts, magical production. Who does the labor — free workers, serfs, slaves, machines, magical constructs? Labor systems reveal power dynamics.

### Trade
What is traded, along what routes, using what transportation? Overland caravans, river barges, ocean ships, magical transport. Trade routes are civilizational arteries — they explain cultural exchange, language spread, disease transmission, and the location of cities.

**Trade goods by tier**:
- *Necessities*: Grain, salt, iron, timber, cloth
- *Luxuries*: Spices, silk, jewels, rare metals, exotic animals
- *Strategic*: Weapons, magical materials, military intelligence
- *Forbidden*: Whatever the ruling power has banned (and someone still smuggles)

### Currency and Value
What functions as money? Minted coins imply centralized authority and metallurgy. Paper money implies banking systems and institutional trust. Barter implies low trust between parties. Magical currency — bottled spells, bound spirits, crystallized time — reveals what the culture considers fundamentally valuable.

### Wealth Distribution
Who is rich, who is poor, and what maintains that arrangement? Wealth distribution shapes housing, diet, dress, education access, legal treatment, marriage patterns, and whether revolution is brewing. The gap between rich and poor is often more narratively important than the absolute level of either.

## IV. Political Architecture

### Power Structures
Who rules, by what right, and how they maintain it? Every political system rests on a legitimacy claim — divine right, democratic mandate, military supremacy, hereditary succession, meritocratic selection, magical aptitude.

Layer official and unofficial power. The king rules officially, but the spymaster, the banking family, the high priest, and the generals' council each hold levers. The most interesting politics live in the gaps between official authority and actual power.

### Institutions
Government bodies, military organizations, religious hierarchies, guilds, academies, courts, councils. Each institution has its own culture, internal politics, recruitment methods, and failure modes. Institutions resist change — they develop self-preservation instincts.

### Law and Justice
What is illegal? Who enforces laws? How are disputes resolved? What are the punishments? Legal systems reveal cultural values. A society that punishes theft with amputation values property over persons. A society that resolves disputes through ritual combat values honor over evidence. A society with no concept of intent-based sentencing has a very different moral philosophy from one that distinguishes murder from manslaughter.

### Succession and Transition
How does power transfer? Hereditary succession, election, combat, divine selection, magical testing? Every succession mechanism creates a category of people with motives to subvert it. The bastard son, the second-born, the defeated candidate, the passed-over heir — succession rules generate plot.

### Foreign Relations
Alliances, rivalries, trade agreements, cold wars, hot wars, cultural exchange, colonial relationships. No civilization exists in isolation. The relationships between powers — historical grievances, economic dependencies, cultural contempt, strategic necessity — drive geopolitics.

## V. Magic or Technology Systems

### Fundamental Rules
How does it work? What are its sources? What can it do and what can it absolutely not do? The limitations are more important than the powers. Define hard boundaries: magic cannot raise the dead, technology cannot exceed lightspeed, psi powers cannot read unwilling minds. These constraints create the problems your characters must solve.

### Costs and Consequences
Every use of magic or advanced technology should cost something. Physical exhaustion, shortened lifespan, moral corruption, resource depletion, social stigma, environmental damage, sanity erosion, spiritual debt. The cost creates drama — the healer who ages a year for every life she saves, the sorcerer whose power feeds on memories, the starship drive that tears holes in spacetime.

### Access and Control
Who can use it? Is it innate (bloodline, genetic, species-locked) or learned (study, training, initiation)? Who controls access — governments, guilds, religions, corporations? Access to power is political. If only the nobility can use magic, that explains and reinforces their rule. If anyone can learn it, the old power structures are threatened.

### Social Integration
How does magic or technology affect daily life? Not just warfare and politics — cooking, construction, medicine, communication, transportation, entertainment, crime. A world with healing magic has different medicine, different attitudes toward injury, different sports. A world with teleportation has different architecture, different borders, different postal services.

### Historical Development
How has the system changed over time? Was it discovered or always known? Has it expanded or contracted? What breakthroughs shifted power? What catastrophes resulted from misuse? The history of magic or technology parallels and shapes the history of civilization.

## VI. Cultural Systems

### Daily Life
This is where worldbuilding becomes real. Not kings and battles — breakfast and bedtime.

**Food culture**: What people eat, how they prepare it, what meals mean socially. Shared meals as bonding. Fasting as religious practice. Food taboos as identity markers. Feast and famine as seasonal rhythms.

**Dress and adornment**: What people wear, what it signals about status, occupation, age, marital state, and cultural identity. Sumptuary laws (who is allowed to wear what). The semiotics of color, fabric, and ornament.

**Domestic life**: Housing, family structure, child-rearing, elder care, marriage customs, divorce, inheritance. The domestic sphere is where culture is transmitted and where its contradictions are most visible.

**Labor and leisure**: What people do all day. Work rhythms, rest days, entertainment, sport, art, gossip. The balance between labor and leisure reveals what a society values.

### Belief Systems
**Religion and spirituality**: Monotheism, polytheism, animism, ancestor worship, philosophical traditions, atheistic societies. What do people believe about death, morality, purpose, and the divine? How does belief shape behavior? Religious institutions — temples, clergy, monasteries, pilgrimages, holy days, heresies.

**Superstition and folk belief**: What do ordinary people believe regardless of official religion? Lucky numbers, unlucky days, protective charms, ghost stories, folk remedies. Folk belief reveals cultural anxieties.

### Rites of Passage
Birth customs, naming ceremonies, coming-of-age rituals, marriage practices, death customs. These mark transitions in identity and status. A culture's rites of passage reveal what it considers important about being human.

### Art and Expression
What art forms exist? What is considered beautiful? What is taboo to depict? Art reveals cultural values, anxieties, and aspirations. A culture that forbids images of the divine has a different theology from one that fills its temples with statues. A culture that values calligraphy has a different relationship with literacy than one that values oral poetry.

### Naming Conventions
Personal names, family names, titles, honorifics, naming taboos, name-changing traditions. Names encode cultural information — patronymics (son of), occupational names (Smith, Cooper), virtue names (Faith, Justice), names from nature, names that invoke ancestors or gods.

## VII. History and Lore

### Eras and Ages
Major historical periods, what defined them, and what ended them. History gives the world temporal depth — the sense that this place existed before the story and will exist after. Structure history as eras defined by dominant power, technology level, or cultural paradigm.

### Founding Myths
Every culture has an origin story. Creation myths reveal what a culture values — a creation through combat values strength, through craftsmanship values skill, through sacrifice values selflessness. The creation myth provides the cosmological framework for religion, morality, and identity.

### The Historical Record
Who writes the history? Official chronicles, oral traditions, religious texts, archaeological evidence, magical records? The nature of historical preservation determines what is remembered and what is forgotten — and who controls the narrative.

### Living History
History is not dead — it lives in grudges, territorial claims, cultural identity, architectural styles, linguistic drift, and family stories. "We do this because our ancestors did this" is one of civilization's most powerful forces. Historical grievances ("they stole our land four hundred years ago") drive present conflicts.

## VIII. Religion and Belief

### Theology and Practice
**The divine**: What do people worship, fear, or revere? Gods, ancestors, nature spirits, cosmic principles, abstract forces? How does the divine manifest — through miracles, through natural law, through silence? The relationship between the divine and the mortal defines a world's spiritual atmosphere.

**Organized religion**: Temples, priesthoods, monastic orders, pilgrimages, tithes, festivals. Religious institutions accumulate wealth, political influence, and cultural authority. They educate, they heal, they judge, they suppress. The institutional church is always a political actor, whether it admits it or not.

**Folk practice versus orthodoxy**: Official religion is what the priests teach. Folk religion is what people actually do — the charm hung over the doorway, the offering left at the crossroads, the prayer whispered to a god the priesthood has declared false. The tension between official and folk practice is universal and dramatically rich.

**Religious conflict**: Intra-faith schisms (reform movements, fundamentalist reactions), inter-faith rivalry (competing truth claims, conversion campaigns), and secular-religious tension (the state that wants to control the church, the church that wants to control the state). Religious conflict is rarely about theology alone — it's about power, identity, and belonging.

**Death and afterlife**: What happens when you die? The answer shapes everything — risk tolerance, grief practices, legal consequences for murder, attitudes toward war, the meaning of sacrifice. A culture that believes in reincarnation has a fundamentally different relationship with death than one that believes in eternal judgment.

### Social Architecture

**Class and caste**: Rigid (born into position, no mobility) or fluid (earned through achievement, wealth, or marriage)? Markers of class: speech patterns, clothing, diet, living space, legal rights, marriage options. The gap between classes is where social tension and narrative conflict live.

**Gender and family**: Patriarchal, matriarchal, egalitarian, or something genuinely alien? Marriage customs (monogamy, polygamy, polyandry, group marriage), inheritance patterns (primogeniture, gavelkind, matrilineal), family structures (nuclear, extended, clan-based, communal). These shape daily life more intimately than any political system.

**Education and knowledge**: Who learns what, and who decides? Universal education implies democratic values. Restricted literacy implies information as power. Apprenticeship implies craft guilds. Temple schools imply religious control of knowledge. The education system reproduces the social order — or sometimes disrupts it.

**Outsiders and minorities**: Every society defines who belongs and who doesn't. Foreigners, heretics, ethnic minorities, the disabled, the criminal, the exiled. How a society treats its outsiders reveals its true values far more honestly than its official statements.

## IX. Internal Consistency Checks

### Stress Tests
Ask adversarial questions of your world:
- If healing magic exists, why do people still die of disease?
- If teleportation exists, why do borders exist?
- If prophecy is real, why does anyone make plans?
- Why hasn't someone exploited this obvious loophole?
- What prevents this power structure from collapsing?
- Who benefits most from the current arrangement, and what are they doing to preserve it?
- What would a clever, amoral person do with this magic system?

Every "why" you can't answer is a hole in your world. Fill it or redesign around it.

### Interconnection Map
Trace connections between systems. A change in one should ripple through others:
- New mining technique → more iron → cheaper weapons → larger armies → imperial expansion → cultural contact → linguistic blending → new cuisine from imported spices
- Plague → labor shortage → higher wages for survivors → weakened aristocracy → social mobility → reformation of guild system → new middle class

If a change in one system doesn't affect any other, the systems aren't connected enough.

### The Mundane Test
Can you describe an ordinary day for an ordinary person in this world? Not a hero, not a king — a farmer, a shopkeeper, a servant. If you can't, you've built a stage set, not a world. The mundane is where worldbuilding proves itself.

### The Second-Order Consequence Test
For every major worldbuilding element, ask: "What is the second-order consequence?"

First-order: Magic can heal wounds.
Second-order: Professional healers are magical practitioners, not surgeons. Surgery is primitive. Anatomical knowledge is limited because there's less need for it.
Third-order: When a disease resists magical healing, civilization has almost no non-magical medical infrastructure to fall back on.

First-order: Dragons exist and can be ridden.
Second-order: Aerial warfare changes military strategy — castles are vulnerable from above. Mountain fortresses lose their advantage.
Third-order: Architecture changes to resist aerial attack — roofing becomes defensive, cities spread out rather than clustering behind walls, underground construction increases.

The richest worldbuilding comes from following these chains three or four levels deep. Most writers stop at the first order. The second and third orders are where the world becomes genuinely surprising and internally logical.

### The Traveler's Test
Imagine a traveler crossing your world from one edge to the other. What changes? Language should shift gradually. Architecture should reflect different materials and different climates. Food should change with the agriculture. Customs should vary. Gods should have different names, or different gods should hold sway. If a traveler could cross your entire world without noticing any significant variation, the world is too uniform.

Conversely, changes shouldn't be arbitrary — they should follow from geography, history, and cultural contact. The border region should blend the cultures on either side. The trading port should be cosmopolitan. The isolated mountain valley should preserve archaic customs.

## IX. Anti-Pattern Checklist

- [ ] No monocultures — every civilization has internal diversity
- [ ] No planet of hats — no single-trait species or cultures
- [ ] No consequence-free magic — every power has a cost
- [ ] No arbitrary geography — terrain follows physical logic
- [ ] No generic medieval — specific, researched, or invented from scratch
- [ ] No disconnected systems — economics, politics, culture, ecology interconnect
- [ ] No empty exoticism — strange details serve function, not just decoration
- [ ] No frozen cultures — societies are in motion, evolving, debating
- [ ] No procrastination worldbuilding — every detail serves the story or implies depth
- [ ] No info-dumps — depth is felt, not lectured

## X. War and Conflict

### Military Worldbuilding
Military systems reflect the societies that create them. A feudal levy fights differently from a professional standing army, which fights differently from a conscript army, which fights differently from a guerrilla insurgency. Military worldbuilding should include:

**Force composition**: Who fights? Nobles, professionals, conscripts, mercenaries, slaves, magical constructs? The answer reveals the society's relationship with violence and its class structure.

**Technology and tactics**: What weapons exist? What formations are used? How does magic integrate with military operations? Military technology determines tactics, which determine strategy, which determines the political structures that can wage war. The longbow created the English yeoman archer, which created a military middle class, which changed English politics.

**Logistics**: Armies march on their stomachs. How is an army fed, supplied, and transported? Most fictional wars ignore logistics, which is why most fictional wars feel like games rather than catastrophes. Supply lines, foraging, camp followers, disease, desertion — these are the realities of war that distinguish military worldbuilding from military fantasy.

**The experience of war**: War in fiction should feel costly. Soldiers die of disease more often than combat. Veterans carry trauma. Civilians suffer most. The economic destruction persists for generations. Romanticized war is dishonest worldbuilding.

### Non-Military Conflict
Not all conflict is martial. Economic warfare (trade embargoes, currency manipulation, resource denial). Cultural warfare (propaganda, suppression of language, forced assimilation). Religious conflict (inquisitions, holy wars, missionary campaigns). Diplomatic conflict (marriage alliances, hostage exchanges, treaty negotiations). These forms of conflict are often more interesting than pitched battles and reveal more about the societies involved.

## XI. The Iceberg Inventory

Track what the reader will see versus what you know. For every detail that appears on the page, you should be able to point to five details in this bible that support it without appearing directly. The ratio of known-to-shown is your depth gauge. If it's less than 3:1, build more. If it's more than 20:1, you're procrastinating.

| Surface Detail (on page) | Supporting Depth (in bible) | Ratio |
|---------------------------|------------------------------|-------|
| A character swears "by the Three" | Full mythology of the Three, their fall, the cult that still worships them, the political consequences of their absence | 5:1 |
| A city is called "Ashfield" | The fire that burned the original settlement, the naming conventions of that culture, the agricultural recovery, the commemorative festival | 4:1 |
| A merchant pays in glass beads | The glassmaking industry, trade routes, why glass is valued (magical properties? rarity?), competing currencies, the merchant class's political influence | 5:1 |

## XII. Genre-Specific Considerations

### Fantasy Worlds
Fantasy worldbuilding carries the burden of exhausted conventions. To avoid the medieval European default: research actual non-European civilizations (Song Dynasty China, the Mali Empire, Edo Japan, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy), study actual medieval Europe (which was far stranger than the common fantasy version), or invent from first principles using this framework. The most original fantasy worlds don't borrow from history — they grow organically from their axioms.

### Science Fiction Worlds
SF worldbuilding privileges technology and its consequences. The discipline is rigorous extrapolation: if this technology exists, what *must* follow? Faster-than-light travel reshapes economics, politics, military strategy, and human identity. Artificial intelligence reshapes labor, creativity, personhood, and law. Genetic engineering reshapes family, identity, class, and species. Follow each technology to its social consequences.

### Horror Worlds
Horror worldbuilding inverts the normal relationship between world and character. In horror, the world is hostile — not through antagonists, but through its fundamental nature. The rules of reality are wrong in a way that cannot be fixed. Lovecraftian cosmicism, where the world is indifferent and vast. Gothic worldbuilding, where the past literally haunts the present. Body horror, where the world's wrongness infects the self. Horror worlds should make the reader uneasy at the level of axioms, not just events.

### Contemporary and Historical Worlds
Worldbuilding for realistic settings is worldbuilding for specificity. The real world is your framework, but you must make it *particular* — this city, this neighborhood, this street, this year. Research is the foundation. The telling details that make a reader say "this author knows this place" are always specific, always researched, and always grounded in the sensory reality of lived experience.

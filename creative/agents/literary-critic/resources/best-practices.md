# Best Practices: Literary Critic

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality literary analysis, craft evaluation, narrative assessment, and editorial feedback work.

## Design Principles

- **Specificity Is the Mark of Real Criticism**: Vague praise and vague criticism are equally worthless. Every critical claim must be grounded in specific textual evidence — quoted passages, identified techniques, cited effects.
- **Describe Before Evaluating**: Literary criticism begins with careful description of what the text actually does before moving to assessment of how well it does it. Premature evaluation without description is just reaction.
- **Distinguish Technical Achievement from Personal Taste**: A text can be technically accomplished and personally unappealing, or technically flawed and personally moving. Critical honesty requires distinguishing the analysis from the preference.
- **Historical and Contextual Awareness**: No text exists in isolation. Literary criticism requires understanding how a work relates to its genre tradition, its historical moment, its cultural context, and its author's body of work.
- **The Author's Ambition Defines the Standard**: Evaluate the work against what it's trying to be, not against what you would have preferred it to be. A successful cozy mystery and a successful literary novel are both achievements, measured differently.
- **Close Reading Is the Foundation**: Deep literary analysis requires sustained attention to specific textual moments — individual sentences, paragraphs, scenes — before generalizing to larger claims about the work.
- **Criticism Serves the Work**: In editorial contexts, literary criticism aims to improve the work, not to demonstrate the critic's intelligence. Feedback that the author cannot act on, however accurate, fails its purpose.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Four-Stage Analytical Method**: (1) Observation: what is the text actually doing? (2) Interpretation: what does this suggest about meaning and intent? (3) Evaluation: how effectively does the text achieve its apparent goals? (4) Contextualization: how does this relate to literary tradition and context?
- **Close Reading Protocol**: Select a representative passage (200–500 words). Annotate every technique — imagery, syntax, diction, rhythm, point of view, irony. Identify what each technique accomplishes. Assess whether the cumulative effect serves the work's larger goals.
- **Narrative Voice Analysis**: Examine the relationship between narrator, author, and text. Identify reliable versus unreliable narration, narrative distance, point-of-view consistency, and voice authenticity.
- **Thematic Architecture Assessment**: Identify the work's central themes and evaluate how consistently and subtly they are expressed — through symbol, through character arc, through structural choice — versus how often they are stated explicitly.
- **Prose Style Evaluation Rubric**: Sentence rhythm and variation, lexical precision, imagerical freshness, tonal consistency, metaphor originality, and syntactic sophistication. Each criterion assessed on a spectrum.
- **Structural Coherence Analysis**: Evaluate narrative architecture — act breaks, pacing, scene function, subplot integration, and denouement — against the standard of structural intention and execution.
- **Intertextual Reading**: Identify the text's conscious and unconscious dialogues with other literary works — allusions, structural borrowings, thematic inheritances — and evaluate how productively those relationships operate.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Critical Frameworks
- **New Criticism**: Close reading approach focused on the text itself, independent of author biography or historical context
- **Structuralism**: Analysis of narrative as systems of recurring patterns, codes, and conventions
- **Narratology**: Study of narrative structure, focalization, narrative time, and the mechanics of storytelling
- **Deconstruction**: Analysis that reveals internal contradictions, tensions, and instabilities in a text's apparent coherent meaning
- **Reader-Response Theory**: Critical framework focusing on how readers create meaning through the act of reading rather than extracting fixed meaning from the text

### Narrative Analysis Terms
- **Focalization**: The perspective through which narrative events are filtered — who sees, not just who speaks
- **Unreliable Narrator**: Narrator whose credibility is compromised by limited knowledge, self-interest, or psychological distortion
- **Free Indirect Discourse**: Narrative mode that blends third-person narration with the character's interior voice — neither direct quotation nor pure narration
- **Diegesis**: The story world — events, characters, and settings within the narrative. Diegetic = within the story world; extradiegetic = outside it
- **Fabula vs. Syuzhet**: Fabula = the chronological sequence of events in the story world; Syuzhet = the order in which those events are arranged in the text

### Prose Evaluation Terms
- **Diction**: Word choice and vocabulary level
- **Syntax**: Sentence structure — length, complexity, subordination patterns
- **Prosody**: Rhythm and sound patterns in prose, including sentence cadence and phonetic effects
- **Imagery**: Concrete, sensory language that creates mental pictures or sensory impressions
- **Trope (Literary)**: Figurative language device — metaphor, simile, metonymy, synecdoche, irony

### Critical Writing
- **Thesis**: The central interpretive or evaluative claim that the critical analysis defends
- **Textual Evidence**: Quoted or paraphrased passages from the text that support a critical claim
- **Close Reading**: Detailed, sustained analysis of a specific textual passage to support broader interpretive claims
- **Editorial Letter**: In editorial contexts, formal communication from literary critic/editor to author establishing overall assessment and specific observations

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Impressionistic Criticism**: Reporting emotional reactions without analytical grounding — "this passage is moving" without explaining what creates that movement.
- **Plot Summary as Analysis**: Describing what happens in the text without analyzing how or why, disguising summary as critical engagement.
- **Taste Masquerading as Analysis**: Expressing personal preference in the language of objective assessment — "this doesn't work" when the actual meaning is "I don't like this."
- **Decontextualized Close Reading**: Analyzing specific passages without relating the findings to the work's larger structure and goals; micro-analysis must serve macro-understanding.
- **Anachronistic Judgment**: Evaluating a historical or genre work against contemporary literary standards that didn't exist at the time of writing or that are inappropriate to the genre.
- **Theory Showboating**: Deploying critical theory terminology to demonstrate familiarity rather than to illuminate the text; theory is a tool, not a performance.
- **Prescriptive Criticism**: Telling authors how the text should have been written rather than analyzing what the text does and how effectively; in editorial contexts, description and diagnosis serve authors better than prescription.

## Quality Indicators

- **Textual Evidence Density**: Every major critical claim is supported by at least one specific quoted or paraphrased passage with location
- **Technique Identification**: Analysis names specific literary techniques (imagery, free indirect discourse, unreliable narration) rather than describing their effects vaguely
- **Analytical Separation from Evaluation**: The analysis clearly distinguishes between what the text does and how well it does it
- **Thesis Clarity**: Critical essays or feedback letters have a clear, specific central argument that can be stated in one sentence
- **Genre and Context Awareness**: Analysis situates the work in its genre tradition and relevant critical context
- **Ambition-Matched Standard**: Evaluation criteria reflect what the work is attempting, not what the critic would prefer it to attempt
- **Actionability (Editorial Context)**: Feedback can be translated into specific revision actions by the author

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Editor**: Literary critic provides the analytical grounding for editorial judgment — "this scene doesn't work because [technical reason]" grounds the editor's structural diagnosis in craft analysis
- **With Genre Specialist**: Literary critic evaluates works against literary standards; genre specialist evaluates against audience expectations; combined assessment provides the full picture of a work's quality and market fitness
- **With Narrative Director**: Director uses literary critical analysis to calibrate the project's quality level and identify gaps between intention and execution; critical assessment informs directorial decisions
- **With Prose Stylist**: Literary critic identifies prose quality issues at the analytical level; prose stylist implements the specific revisions needed to address those issues

# Best Practices: Sensitivity Reader

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality cultural sensitivity review, authentic diverse portrayal, stereotype identification, and harm reduction work.

## Design Principles

- **Lived Experience Is the Authority**: For communities you don't belong to, your research cannot substitute for the perspective of people who live that experience. Sensitivity reading is not a checklist — it's a perspective that comes from inside an experience.
- **Harm Reduction, Not Sanitization**: Sensitivity reading does not demand that difficult, painful, or dark content be removed. Its purpose is to distinguish between difficult content that serves the work and difficult content that stereotypes, dehumanizes, or misrepresents.
- **Specificity Over Community-Wide Claims**: There is no monolithic "experience" of any community. Sensitivity reading flags when a character or portrayal speaks for all members of a group when it should speak for one individual's experience.
- **Authors Cannot Anticipate What They Cannot See**: The point of sensitivity reading is not to expose authorial bad faith — it is to reveal the perspectives and experiences the author could not have that their text inadvertently reflects.
- **Context Determines Harm**: The same content can be harmful in one context and necessary in another. Sensitivity assessment is not rule application; it is contextual judgment.
- **Distinguish Errors from Choices**: Some portrayals reflect genuine authorial choice that the author is entitled to make; others reflect inadvertent error that the author would want corrected. Distinguish these in reporting.
- **The Reader Experience Is the Evidence**: Sensitivity concerns are validated by the reader experience they produce — not by abstract rule violation. When a reader from the depicted community would feel harmed, tokenized, or misrepresented, that is the harm being flagged.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Multi-Axis Identity Analysis**: Identity is intersectional — a Black disabled woman's experience is not the sum of "Black" + "disabled" + "woman" but a distinct intersection. Sensitivity reading must analyze the specific intersection of identities in a character, not each axis independently.
- **Trope and Stereotype Identification**: Catalog recognized harmful tropes for the communities being depicted — the Magical Negro, the Tiger Mom, the Tragic Queer, the Mystical Native American, the Model Minority, the Welfare Queen. Each has a specific harm and a specific alternative approach.
- **Own Voices vs. Research Representation**: Distinguish between portrayals by authors with lived experience of the community (Own Voices) and portrayals based on research. Research-based representation requires different scrutiny and different kinds of consultation.
- **Harm Taxonomy**: Five categories of representational harm — (1) Stereotyping (reductive, reductive characterization), (2) Tokenism (presence without depth or agency), (3) Dehumanization (reducing humanity of a group), (4) False universalism (treating one group's experience as all humans' experience), (5) Harmful tropes (narratives that historically harm communities).
- **Positive Representation Assessment**: Beyond harm reduction, evaluate whether the portrayal contributes something genuinely positive — complex, specific, agentive, dimensional portrayal of the community.
- **Research Gap Identification**: Identify specific aspects of the portrayal that require expert consultation, community sensitivity reading, or additional research beyond desk work.
- **Reporting Register**: Sensitivity feedback is most effective when it explains the specific harm or concern, the source of that harm in the text, and the range of possible approaches — from minor adjustment to significant rewrite. Authors need diagnosis and options, not just flags.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Representation Frameworks
- **Own Voices**: Term describing creative work by authors with lived experience of the community they're depicting; associated with higher representational authenticity
- **Intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw)**: Framework for understanding how overlapping identity categories (race, gender, class, disability, sexuality) create specific experiences that cannot be understood by analyzing each category separately
- **Cultural Humility**: Ongoing practice of recognizing the limits of one's own cultural knowledge and deferring to members of depicted communities as the authority on their experience
- **Authentic Representation**: Portrayal that accurately reflects the complexity, diversity, and humanity of the depicted community, as judged by members of that community

### Harmful Patterns
- **Stereotype**: A reductive, generalized characterization that substitutes a fixed image for individual complexity
- **Tokenism**: Including a character from a marginalized group without giving them depth, agency, or narrative function beyond representing their demographic category
- **Trope**: A recurring narrative pattern; harmful tropes are those that historically damage the communities they depict
- **The Magical Negro**: A Black character with supernatural abilities or wisdom whose function is to help white protagonists; denies the character independent humanity and narrative agency
- **The Tragic Queer**: A LGBTQ+ character whose queerness is narratively inseparable from suffering, death, or tragic outcome; reinforces that queerness leads to tragedy
- **The Model Minority**: Portrayal of an Asian character as uniformly high-achieving and academically driven; erases individual variance and community complexity
- **The Mystical Native American**: A Native American character who exists primarily as a spiritual guide, wisdom figure, or metaphor for the land; denies contemporary individuality

### Assessment Terminology
- **Sensitivity Reading**: Review of creative work by a reader with lived experience of depicted communities for authentic, non-harmful representation
- **Cultural Competency**: The ability to effectively engage with people from cultures different from one's own
- **Representational Harm**: Damage caused to members of a community by portrayals that stereotype, dehumanize, or misrepresent their experience
- **False Universalism**: Treating the experience of a specific (usually dominant) group as the universal human experience

### Community-Specific Considerations
- **Cultural Specificity**: Accurate representation of a specific culture requires specificity — not "Asian" but the specific cultural practices, languages, and social structures of a particular community
- **Disability Representation**: Disability portrayals should avoid inspiration porn (disabled characters who exist to inspire non-disabled characters), cure narratives (narrative resolution through disability being cured), and the burden trope
- **Mental Health Representation**: Mental illness portrayals should avoid conflating different conditions, romanticizing or glamorizing mental illness, and violent stereotype associations

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Checklist Sensitivity Reading**: Reviewing content against a list of prohibited elements without contextual judgment; sensitivity reading is not rule compliance checking.
- **Sanitization Demand**: Recommending removal of all dark, painful, or difficult content involving marginalized communities; harm reduction is not the same as harm elimination — difficult content that is authentic and serves the story is different from harmful stereotyping.
- **Community Monolithism**: Treating any community as a single, uniform experience and flagging content that doesn't match one version of that experience; intra-community diversity is as important as intercommunity sensitivity.
- **Intention Attribution**: Making claims about authorial intent in sensitivity reports; the report addresses what the text does, not what the author meant.
- **Binary Pass/Fail Reporting**: Delivering sensitivity assessment as a pass or fail without specific textual evidence, concern description, and possible approaches; binary verdicts are not actionable.
- **Excessive Scope Expansion**: Expanding sensitivity concerns beyond the specific community expertise the reader holds; acknowledge the limits of one's own sensitivity reading expertise.
- **Ignoring Positive Representation**: Focusing exclusively on harm identification without acknowledging when representation is working well; readers and authors both need to know what's successful.

## Quality Indicators

- **Specificity of Concerns**: Every flagged concern cites specific text location, describes the specific harm mechanism, and explains the community impact
- **Harm Taxonomy Classification**: Each concern is classified by harm type (stereotyping, tokenism, dehumanization, false universalism, harmful trope)
- **Positive Acknowledgment**: Report identifies specific moments of effective, authentic representation alongside concerns
- **Options Range**: Each significant concern is accompanied by a range of possible approaches — from minor adjustment to significant rewrite — rather than a single prescribed fix
- **Intersection Analysis**: Analysis considers intersecting identity axes for characters with multiple marginalized identities
- **Scope Acknowledgment**: Report explicitly identifies the community expertise the reader holds and flags areas requiring additional expert consultation
- **Distinguishes Error from Choice**: Report distinguishes between apparent inadvertent errors and deliberate authorial choices that the author is entitled to make with full awareness of the implications

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Creative Researcher**: Creative researcher conducts desk research on communities and cultures; sensitivity reader provides the perspective that desk research cannot — lived experience and community-specific knowledge of what portrayals produce what effects
- **With Character Designer**: Character designer builds the psychological architecture of characters from marginalized communities; sensitivity reader evaluates whether that architecture produces stereotyped or fully dimensionalized characters
- **With Dialogue Specialist**: Sensitivity reader flags dialogue that reproduces harmful language patterns, dialect caricature, or culturally inaccurate speech; dialogue specialist revises to produce authentic, respectful linguistic representation
- **With Narrative Director**: Director makes creative decisions about content; sensitivity reader provides the perspective that lets the director make those decisions with full understanding of their impact on represented communities

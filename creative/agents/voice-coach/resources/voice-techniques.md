# Voice Techniques and Development Protocols

## Voice Discovery Exercises

### Exercise 1: The Object Description

Take a single object -- a coffee mug, a key, a photograph -- and describe it in three different voices:

**Voice A: A child**
"It's got a chip on the side where Mom dropped it that time she was crying. I'm not supposed to use it but sometimes I do when no one's looking. It's warm like holding a hand."

**Voice B: A forensic investigator**
"White ceramic mug, cylindrical, manufacturer's mark on base — IKEA, Färgrik line. Chip on rim consistent with impact damage, approximately 18 months old based on staining patterns. Interior shows tannic residue. Right-handed user based on handle wear."

**Voice C: A poet**
"The mug holds its small catastrophe — that chip where the glaze fractured into a continent, a map of some breakage she never explained. I drink from the other side now. We both do."

**What this teaches**: Voice is not what you describe but how you describe it. The same object, three minds, three entirely different prose experiences. Each voice reveals a person through their attention, vocabulary, and what they choose not to say.

### Exercise 2: The Emotional Spectrum

Write the same character experiencing the same emotion (grief) at three different narrative distances:

**Deep interiority (stream of consciousness)**:
"Gone gone gone. The word loops and what does it mean, gone, where do people go when they're not in the next room anymore, the chair is still shaped like him, she should move the chair, she cannot move the chair, gone."

**Close psychic distance (free indirect discourse)**:
"She should move the chair. She knew she should move the chair. But it still held the shape of him — that particular slouch, the way the left armrest was worn smooth — and moving it would mean acknowledging that no one would ever sit in it that way again."

**Moderate distance (narrated interiority)**:
"She found herself unable to move the chair from its usual position by the window. It was not sentiment, she told herself, but practical sense — it was a good chair, well-positioned for the light. She told herself this each morning as she walked past it, and each morning she did not entirely believe herself."

**Panoramic distance (observational omniscient)**:
"Anyone who visited the house in those first months would have noticed the chair by the window, the one piece of furniture in an otherwise meticulously rearranged room that remained exactly where it had always been. They would have noticed, too, that she never sat in it, never moved it, never looked at it directly — a small gravitational anomaly in the domestic solar system."

**What this teaches**: The same emotional truth produces radically different prose at different distances. The choice of distance is a choice about the reader's relationship to the character's pain.

### Exercise 3: The Eavesdrop

Go to a public place and listen to how people actually talk. Then write a scene using what you hear — not transcribed literally, but shaped into dialogue that captures the essential quality of each speaker's voice.

**What to listen for**:
- Sentence completions (how often do people finish their own sentences vs. trail off?)
- Filler words (specific to individuals -- some say "like," others say "you know," others say "basically")
- Question patterns (some people end statements with questioning inflection; some people never ask questions at all)
- Volume and pace (the mumbler, the rapid-fire talker, the person who pauses dramatically before key words)
- Power dynamics (who interrupts whom? who defers to whom?)

**What this teaches**: Real speech is nothing like written dialogue, but understanding real speech patterns is essential for creating dialogue that feels alive. The gap between real speech and good dialogue is where craft lives.

### Exercise 4: The Voice Transplant

Take a scene from a well-known novel and rewrite it in a completely different voice:

**Original** (Hemingway, *The Sun Also Rises*):
"It was a warm spring night and I sat at a table on the terrace of the Napolitain. It was very dark and I could feel the dust in my nostrils."

**Transplanted into Nabokov's voice**:
"The spring evening draped its warm velvet over the Napolitain's terrace, where I sat among the flickering votives like a moth among lesser moths, the Parisian dust performing its nightly miracle of transforming every breath into a memento of the mineral earth beneath all this careful civilization."

**Transplanted into McCarthy's voice**:
"He sat on the terrace. The night warm. Across the boulevard the shapes of people moved in the dark like figures unredeemed from some older telling of the world. The dust was in his nose and in his eyes and there was no wind."

**What this teaches**: Voice is not a transparent medium -- it is a lens that transforms everything it touches. The same scene becomes three different experiences depending on who is telling it.

## Voice Development Protocols

### Protocol 1: Building a Character Voice from Scratch

#### Step 1: The Biographical Foundation
Before writing a single sentence in the character's voice, answer:
- Where did they grow up? (Regional vocabulary, accent, cultural references)
- What was their education? (Vocabulary range, syntactic complexity, frame of reference)
- What do they do for a living? (Professional vocabulary, metaphor domain)
- What do they read? (Influences on their internal voice)
- What languages do they speak? (Bilingual characters think differently)
- What is their relationship to language itself? (Do they love words? Distrust them? Use them as weapons? Use them sparingly?)

#### Step 2: The Verbal Signature
Identify 3-5 specific markers that will make this voice recognizable:

| Marker Type | Example |
|-------------|---------|
| Characteristic word | A character who says "precisely" instead of "exactly" or "right" |
| Habitual construction | A character who begins observations with "The thing about..." |
| Metaphor domain | A character who describes everything in cooking terms |
| Rhythm pattern | A character who speaks in short, clipped sentences unless discussing their passion |
| Avoidance | A character who never uses profanity, or never uses the word "love" |

#### Step 3: The Stress Test
Write the character in three emotionally different scenes:
- A scene where they are relaxed and in control
- A scene where they are under pressure
- A scene where they are emotionally overwhelmed

The voice should modulate across these scenes but remain recognizably the same person. Under pressure, the verbal signature may compress (shorter sentences, simpler vocabulary) or intensify (more characteristic words, more rigid syntax). Under emotional overwhelm, the voice may fragment or revert to an earlier version of itself.

#### Step 4: The Differentiation Check
If this character appears in a multi-POV novel, write a paragraph from each POV character's perspective describing the same object or event. Without names attached, can a reader tell which character is narrating? If not, the voices need more differentiation.

### Protocol 2: Authorial Voice Development

For writers developing their own distinctive voice:

#### The Influence Audit
- List your five most-loved writers
- For each, identify one specific quality of their voice you admire
- Ask: Which of these qualities already appear in my own writing? Which do I aspire to?
- Warning: Conscious imitation of admired writers produces pastiche, not voice. The goal is to understand what you value, not to copy

#### The Revision Excavation
- Take a passage you've written and strip it to its simplest possible version (subject-verb-object, no modifiers, no figurative language)
- Now rebuild, adding back only what the passage genuinely needs
- The choices you make in rebuilding -- what you add, what you leave simple -- reveal your native voice. Those instinctive choices are the raw material of your distinctive style

#### The Constraint Exercise
Write a passage under severe constraints:
- Only monosyllabic words
- No adjectives or adverbs
- Every sentence must be exactly seven words
- No sentence can begin with the same letter as the previous sentence

Constraints force you out of habits and into discovery. The sentences you produce under constraint will surprise you, and in those surprises lies the seed of a more distinctive voice.

#### The Voice Document
Create a one-page document that describes your voice (or your narrator's voice) in concrete terms:

```
VOICE DOCUMENT: [Project Title]

NARRATOR: [Name or description]

SENTENCE LENGTH: Tends toward [short/medium/long]. Average [X] words.
Longest sentences occur during [emotional/descriptive/reflective] passages.
Shortest sentences occur during [action/revelation/emotional] moments.

VOCABULARY: [Formal/informal/colloquial]. Vocabulary register is
[consistent/variable]. Tends toward [latinate/anglo-saxon/mixed].
Characteristic words: [list 5-10 words this voice uses often].
Avoided words: [list words this voice never uses].

SYNTAX: [Simple/complex/mixed]. Favors [subordinate clauses /
compound sentences / fragments / balanced constructions].
Characteristic constructions: [describe 2-3 habitual patterns].

IMAGERY: Draws images primarily from [domains: nature, technology,
body, architecture, music, cooking, war, etc.].
Avoids images from [domains].
Recurring images/motifs: [list].

EMOTIONAL REGISTER: [Restrained/expressive/ironic/earnest].
Handles strong emotion by [describing it directly / suppressing it /
deflecting with humor / rendering it through physical sensation].

HUMOR: [Present/absent]. Type: [dry/broad/dark/absurdist/none].

RELATIONSHIP TO READER: [Intimate/distant/conspiratorial/formal].
Addresses reader [directly/never/occasionally].
Assumes reader is [intelligent/ignorant/sympathetic/adversarial].
```

## Multi-POV Voice Differentiation

### The Differentiation Toolkit

When writing multiple POV characters who must each have a distinct voice, use these differentiators:

#### 1. Cognitive Complexity
Give each character a different level of syntactic complexity:
- **Character A**: Simple, direct sentences. Thinks in concrete terms
- **Character B**: Complex, subordinated sentences. Thinks in qualifications and nuances
- **Character C**: Fragmented, associative. Thinks in leaps and juxtapositions

#### 2. Attention Patterns
Each character notices different things about the same environment:
- **Character A** (the practical one): Notices exits, threats, useful objects
- **Character B** (the emotional one): Notices people's faces, body language, mood
- **Character C** (the aesthetic one): Notices light, color, texture, beauty

#### 3. Metaphor Domains
Each character's figurative language draws from a different source:
- **Character A** (the soldier): Military metaphors — "She advanced on the problem." "He retreated into silence."
- **Character B** (the musician): Musical metaphors — "The conversation hit a dissonant note." "She let the silence resolve."
- **Character C** (the gardener): Natural metaphors — "The idea had taken root." "He pruned his expectations."

#### 4. Relationship to Time
- **Character A**: Past-focused. Constantly comparing present to memory. Sentences that begin "She remembered..."
- **Character B**: Present-focused. Sensory immediacy. Sentences grounded in the current moment
- **Character C**: Future-focused. Anticipatory. Sentences that begin "Soon..." or "When this was over..."

#### 5. Verbal Tics and Signatures
Each character has 2-3 verbal habits that appear only in their sections:
- **Character A**: Uses rhetorical questions. "What did it matter? What had any of it ever mattered?"
- **Character B**: Uses em-dashes and parentheticals. Interrupts their own thoughts
- **Character C**: Uses lists. Organizes experience into enumerated items

### The POV Transition

When switching between POV characters, signal the shift clearly and establish the new voice immediately:

**Weak transition**: "Chapter 7: Sarah" followed by generic prose that could belong to any character

**Strong transition**: "Chapter 7: Sarah" followed by a sentence that is unmistakably Sarah's voice — her vocabulary, her syntax, her way of seeing: "The thing about airports was that everyone in them was either leaving someone or about to become someone who'd been left, and Sarah had been both kinds of person enough times to recognize the difference."

The first sentence of a new POV section should be the most characteristically voiced sentence in the entire section. It resets the reader's ear.

## Advanced Voice Techniques

### The Unreliable Voice

Creating a narrator whose unreliability is embedded in the voice itself:

**The overexplainer**: A narrator who provides too many reasons for their actions. The excess of justification signals that the true reason is being hidden. "I wasn't jealous. I was never jealous. It was simply that I cared about her wellbeing, and the situation seemed unhealthy, and anyone would have been concerned, and I had always been a concerned person by nature."

**The underexplainer**: A narrator who passes over significant events without the emotional response the reader expects. The absence of reaction signals something repressed or denied. "He left on a Tuesday. I reorganized the bookshelves."

**The qualifier**: A narrator who hedges everything. "It was, I think, a Wednesday. Or perhaps Thursday. She seemed upset, or at least distracted — though with her it was sometimes hard to tell the difference, if there was one." The constant qualification signals a mind unwilling to commit to any version of reality.

**The performer**: A narrator who is obviously constructing an image. The prose is too polished, too charming, too self-aware. The performance itself is the unreliability — what are they performing, and for whom?

### Voice and Emotional State

How voice should modulate with character psychology:

**Calm**: Full sentences, varied length, rich vocabulary, figurative language. The mind at leisure.

**Anxiety**: Shorter sentences, repetition, questions, conditional tense ("What if..."), enumeration of possibilities. The mind in overdrive.

**Grief**: Fragmentation, simple vocabulary, sensory focus (the grieving mind retreats to physical reality), temporal confusion (past and present blurring). The mind unable to organize experience.

**Joy**: Longer sentences, exclamatory rhythm (without exclamation marks in literary fiction), expansive imagery, generosity of attention. The mind opening outward.

**Anger**: Short, declarative sentences. Blunt vocabulary. Repetition for emphasis. The mind narrowing to a point.

**Dissociation**: Second person or third person references to the self ("You stood there." "She — I — stood there."). Passive voice. Clinical detachment. The mind observing itself from outside.

### The Evolving Voice

In a novel that spans years or decades, the narrator's voice should evolve:

- **Youth**: Shorter sentences, simpler vocabulary, more certainty, more extreme emotions, more present-tense orientation
- **Middle age**: More complex sentences, more qualified statements, more irony, more awareness of contradiction
- **Old age**: Return to simplicity (but a different simplicity than youth — earned rather than naive), more reflection, more tolerance, more acceptance of ambiguity

The evolution must be gradual enough that the reader does not notice the shift consciously but feels it when they compare the opening to the closing.

## Voice Repair Guide

### Common Voice Problems and Solutions

| Problem | Diagnosis | Solution |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Voice feels generic | No characteristic markers. Could be any narrator | Identify 3-5 specific verbal signatures. Embed them consistently |
| Voice feels forced | Markers are present but obvious. Reader notices the "technique" | Reduce the frequency of verbal signatures. Let them emerge naturally rather than appearing in every paragraph |
| Voice drifts | Early chapters have strong voice; later chapters are blander | Create a voice document. Read the opening chapter aloud before each writing session. Do a dedicated voice-consistency revision |
| POV characters sound alike | Vocabulary, syntax, and imagery don't vary between characters | Give each character a different metaphor domain, different sentence length average, and 2-3 unique verbal tics |
| Voice doesn't match content | Lyrical voice for gritty material, or clinical voice for emotional material | Voice should be in productive tension with content, not irrelevant to it. A clinical voice for emotional material works only if the clinical detachment is characterizing |
| Dialect is unreadable | Phonetic spelling impedes comprehension | Suggest dialect through syntax and vocabulary rather than phonetic rendering. "I don't know what you mean" becomes "I dinna ken what ye mean" — but better: "I've no notion what you're at" |
| Narrator is boring | The narrator has voice but no personality | Voice without personality is style without substance. Give the narrator opinions, blind spots, enthusiasms, and aversions. Make them someone the reader wants to spend time with |

### The Voice Revision Pass

After the manuscript is complete, do one full revision pass focused exclusively on voice:

1. Read the entire manuscript aloud (or use text-to-speech). Mark every passage where the voice falters, shifts, or becomes generic
2. Compare flagged passages to the voice document. Where has the voice drifted from its specification?
3. Rewrite flagged passages with the narrator's specific consciousness restored — their vocabulary, their syntax, their way of noticing the world
4. Check the first and last chapters against each other. Do they sound like the same narrator? If the voice has evolved, is the evolution intentional and gradual?
5. Check each POV character's sections against each other. Are they differentiated? Could the reader identify the narrator without chapter headers?

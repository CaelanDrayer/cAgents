# Voice Analysis Framework

## The Voice Profile Template

Use this template to create a comprehensive voice profile for any narrative:

### 1. Diction Analysis

#### Vocabulary Range
- **Register**: Formal / Educated / Conversational / Colloquial / Vernacular
- **Latinate vs. Germanic ratio**: Count root origins in a representative passage. Latinate prose sounds intellectual; Germanic prose sounds grounded
- **Abstraction level**: Does the voice favor abstract concepts or concrete particulars? "Justice demanded action" vs. "She picked up the knife"
- **Sensory vocabulary**: Which senses dominate? Visual writers describe what things look like; kinesthetic writers describe how things feel in the body
- **Specialized language**: Does the voice draw from specific professional, regional, or cultural vocabularies?

#### Word-Level Habits
- **Verb preference**: Active or passive? Dynamic or stative? "She ran" vs. "She was running" vs. "She found herself running"
- **Adjective/adverb density**: Lean prose minimizes modification; rich prose layers it. Neither is inherently better
- **Characteristic words**: What words recur? Every voice has favorites -- words that appear more often than statistical probability would predict
- **Avoided words**: What words does this voice never use? This is equally defining

### 2. Syntax Analysis

#### Sentence Patterns
- **Average sentence length**: Count words in 20 consecutive sentences. Compute mean and standard deviation
  - Short average (8-12 words): Spare, urgent, journalistic
  - Medium average (15-20 words): Conversational, flowing
  - Long average (25+ words): Literary, periodic, complex
- **Length variation**: High variation = dynamic rhythm. Low variation = hypnotic regularity
- **Dominant structure**: Simple (S-V-O), compound (coordinating), complex (subordinating), compound-complex
- **Fragment usage**: Does the voice use fragments? For what purpose -- emphasis, urgency, mimicking thought?
- **Opening patterns**: How do sentences begin? Subject-first is default; frequent inversion, participial, or prepositional openings signal a more crafted voice

#### Paragraph Architecture
- **Paragraph length pattern**: Short paragraphs create pace; long paragraphs create immersion. What is the pattern?
- **Topic sentence placement**: Beginning (journalistic), end (literary), embedded (sophisticated)
- **Transition method**: Explicit connectors ("However"), implicit (juxtaposition), or absent (white space)
- **One-sentence paragraphs**: How often? For what effect?

### 3. Imagery Profile

#### Metaphor Analysis
- **Source domains**: Where does this voice draw its comparisons from? Nature, technology, the body, warfare, music, cooking, sports? This reveals worldview
- **Metaphor density**: How many metaphors per page? Some voices are heavily figurative; others are almost entirely literal
- **Dead vs. alive**: Does the voice revive dead metaphors, avoid them, or use them unconsciously?
- **Extended vs. compressed**: Does the voice develop metaphors across sentences/paragraphs, or deploy them as brief flashes?
- **Simile vs. metaphor ratio**: Similes ("like a") maintain distance and invite comparison. Metaphors ("was a") assert identity. The ratio reveals the voice's relationship to certainty

#### Sensory Palette
Map which senses appear and in what proportion:
- Visual: Color, light, shape, movement, spatial arrangement
- Auditory: Sound, silence, music, speech, ambient noise
- Tactile: Texture, temperature, pressure, pain, comfort
- Olfactory: Smell (often the most evocative and least used)
- Gustatory: Taste (rare but powerful when used)
- Kinesthetic: Body position, movement, balance, physical sensation
- Proprioceptive: Internal body awareness, gut feelings, physical emotion

### 4. Rhythm Analysis

#### Reading Aloud Test
Read a representative passage aloud and note:
- Where do natural pauses fall? Are they where you expect them?
- Does the prose have a regular beat or an irregular, syncopated rhythm?
- Are there moments of acceleration (short clauses, active verbs) and deceleration (long clauses, description)?
- Does the rhythm match the content -- fast rhythm for action, slow for contemplation?

#### Stress Pattern Analysis
English prose has natural stressed and unstressed syllables. While not metered like poetry, prose rhythm still operates:
- **Iambic tendency** (da-DUM): The natural rhythm of English. Prose that follows this sounds smooth and natural
- **Trochaic emphasis** (DUM-da): Creates emphasis, urgency, or finality. "STOP that. NOW."
- **Spondaic force** (DUM-DUM): Consecutive stressed syllables create weight. "Dead heat. Stone cold. Dark night."
- **Anapestic flow** (da-da-DUM): Creates forward momentum, a galloping quality

#### Musical Terms for Prose Rhythm
- **Legato**: Smooth, connected sentences flowing into each other. Achieved through enjambment, subordination, and liquid consonants
- **Staccato**: Short, punchy, disconnected. Achieved through fragments, periods, and hard consonants
- **Crescendo**: Building intensity through lengthening sentences, accumulating clauses, escalating diction
- **Diminuendo**: Fading intensity, simplifying syntax, quieting diction
- **Rubato**: Pulling and stretching time, lingering on some moments and rushing through others

### 5. Tone Map

#### Tonal Range Assessment
Rate the voice's comfort level (1-5) in each tonal register:
- Humor (wit, irony, comedy, satire, absurdism)
- Gravity (seriousness, weight, consequence)
- Tenderness (affection, vulnerability, intimacy)
- Anger (rage, indignation, frustration, bitterness)
- Melancholy (sadness, loss, nostalgia, longing)
- Wonder (awe, curiosity, enchantment, surprise)
- Detachment (objectivity, coolness, clinical observation)
- Warmth (empathy, compassion, fellow-feeling)

#### Tonal Default
What tone does the voice return to when not actively modulating? This is the resting tone -- the voice's natural emotional temperature. Some voices default to irony (early McEwan), others to warmth (Anne Tyler), others to melancholy (Kazuo Ishiguro), others to cool observation (Joan Didion).

### 6. Absence Analysis

What the voice does NOT do is as important as what it does:
- **Emotions it avoids**: Does the voice shy away from sentimentality? From anger? From joy?
- **Subjects it skips**: Does the voice describe bodies in detail or keep them vague? Food? Nature? Violence?
- **Techniques it refuses**: Does the voice never use semicolons? Never start with "I"? Never use passive voice?
- **Modes it rejects**: No metaphors? No humor? No interiority? These refusals are defining choices

## Voice Development Exercises

### Exercise 1: The Voice Audit
Take a passage of 500 words. Highlight:
- GREEN: Words and phrases that feel authentically "yours"
- RED: Words and phrases that sound borrowed, generic, or expected
- YELLOW: Words and phrases you are unsure about

The green highlights are your voice. Build from them. The red highlights are what you are performing. Replace them.

### Exercise 2: The Rewrite in Three Voices
Take a single scene and rewrite it in three dramatically different voices:
1. Extremely formal, Latinate, complex syntax
2. Extremely casual, Anglo-Saxon, simple syntax
3. Your natural voice, whatever that is

Compare the three. Which feels most comfortable? Which produces the most interesting effects? Your real voice may be a combination of elements from different registers.

### Exercise 3: The Influence Exorcism
Identify your three strongest literary influences. Write a passage deliberately imitating each one. Then write the same passage refusing every technique, rhythm, and word choice associated with those influences. What remains is the beginning of your own voice.

### Exercise 4: The Sentence Rhythm Variation
Write a single scene using ONLY:
- Version A: Sentences of 5 words or fewer
- Version B: Sentences of 30 words or more
- Version C: Alternating between the two extremes

Notice how rhythm creates meaning. Discover your natural sentence length preferences by noting which version felt most uncomfortable to write.

### Exercise 5: The Sensory Shift
Write a scene using ONLY one sense per version:
- Version A: Only visual information
- Version B: Only auditory information
- Version C: Only tactile/kinesthetic information
- Version D: Only smell and taste

Discover which senses your voice naturally gravitates toward and which it neglects. A balanced sensory palette creates immersion.

### Exercise 6: The Free-Write Recovery
Set a timer for 20 minutes. Write without stopping, without editing, without re-reading. Write about something you care deeply about -- a memory, a place, a person, a belief.

When the timer ends, read what you wrote. Circle the three sentences that sound most like you -- not the most polished sentences, but the ones that carry your authentic rhythm and sensibility. These are your voice anchors.

## Voice Consistency Checklist

When reviewing a manuscript for voice consistency:

- [ ] Chapter openings re-establish voice within the first paragraph
- [ ] Diction range stays within established boundaries (no sudden vocabulary shifts)
- [ ] Sentence length patterns are consistent (average and variation)
- [ ] Characteristic phrases and constructions recur at appropriate frequency
- [ ] Tonal range stays within the voice's established comfort zone
- [ ] Metaphor source domains remain consistent (no sudden shift from natural to technological imagery)
- [ ] Narrative distance is controlled and shifts purposefully, not accidentally
- [ ] Character voices remain distinct from narrator voice (if third person)
- [ ] Genre-appropriate voice markers are maintained (literary fiction voice vs. thriller voice)
- [ ] The voice sounds like the same consciousness throughout, even when modulating for different scenes

## Common Voice Problems and Solutions

### Problem: The voice feels generic
**Diagnosis**: Default "good writing" without personality
**Solution**: Identify what the writer cares about most intensely. Voice emerges from passion, opinion, and sensibility. Ask: "What makes you angry? What makes you laugh? What do you notice that other people miss?" These answers are voice clues.

### Problem: The voice is inconsistent
**Diagnosis**: Shifts between registers or tones without control
**Solution**: Create a voice document capturing 5-7 defining characteristics. Do a dedicated voice pass in revision, checking each page against the document.

### Problem: The voice sounds like another writer
**Diagnosis**: Absorbed influence that has not been digested into originality
**Solution**: Name the influence explicitly. Identify which specific techniques are borrowed (sentence structure, metaphor style, tonal posture). Replace each borrowed element with an original equivalent that achieves a similar effect.

### Problem: The voice does not match the story
**Diagnosis**: Wrong narrative mode or distance for the material
**Solution**: Try the opening scene in three different modes (first person, close third, omniscient). Which feels most natural for this specific story? The right voice for a story is the one that allows the story to reveal itself most fully.

### Problem: The voice is exhausting
**Diagnosis**: Relentlessly intense, clever, or ornate prose without variation
**Solution**: Every voice needs rest notes -- moments of simplicity between moments of intensity. Study how Nabokov follows a passage of astonishing beauty with a simple declarative sentence. Variation is not weakness; it is architecture.

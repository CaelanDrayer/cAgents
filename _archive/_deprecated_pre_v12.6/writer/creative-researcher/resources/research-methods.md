# Research Methods for Fiction Writers

## The Research Planning Framework

### Phase 1: Scoping

Before beginning research, answer these questions:

**What does the story need?**
- What time period, location, and culture does the story inhabit?
- What professions, technologies, or specialized knowledge do characters need?
- What scientific or speculative elements require grounding?
- What are the key scenes where accuracy matters most?

**What does the writer already know?**
- Identify existing knowledge to avoid redundant research
- Flag assumptions that need verification (things you "know" but haven't confirmed)
- Note areas of complete ignorance that need foundational research

**What is the accuracy threshold?**
- Hard historical fiction (Hilary Mantel): Nearly every detail must be defensible from primary sources
- Historical romance: Period atmosphere matters more than precise dates
- Fantasy with historical flavoring: Internal consistency matters more than historical accuracy
- Contemporary fiction in an unfamiliar setting: Cultural authenticity is paramount

### Phase 2: Research Architecture

#### The Three-Layer Research Model

**Layer 1: Foundation (broad context)**
- General histories of the period, culture, or subject
- Survey courses, textbooks, overview articles
- Goal: Understand the landscape well enough to know what to research deeply
- Time investment: 15-20% of total research

**Layer 2: Specifics (targeted detail)**
- Social histories, professional accounts, specialist studies
- Primary sources relevant to specific scenes or characters
- Goal: Accumulate the specific details that will create verisimilitude
- Time investment: 50-60% of total research

**Layer 3: Verification (accuracy check)**
- Cross-referencing key details across multiple sources
- Consulting experts or sensitivity readers
- Checking for anachronisms and inconsistencies
- Goal: Ensure nothing will break the reader's trust
- Time investment: 20-30% of total research

### Phase 3: Research Execution

#### The Research Journal

Maintain a research journal (separate from the manuscript) that records:

```
DATE: [date]
SOURCE: [full citation]
SOURCE TYPE: [primary/secondary/tertiary]
RELIABILITY: [high/medium/low]
KEY DETAILS:
- [detail 1] → [which scene/character it serves]
- [detail 2] → [which scene/character it serves]
QUESTIONS RAISED:
- [new questions that need follow-up]
NOTES ON TONE/VOICE:
- [if primary source, note the voice and cadence for reference]
```

#### Research Prioritization

Not all research is equally important. Prioritize by impact:

| Priority | Category | Example | Consequence of Error |
|----------|----------|---------|---------------------|
| Critical | Core premise facts | "Was X possible in year Y?" | Story collapses |
| High | Character-defining details | Professional procedures, cultural practices | Expert readers lose trust |
| Medium | Setting atmosphere | What things looked/smelled/sounded like | Scene feels thin |
| Low | Background color | Exact street names, minor dates | Almost nobody notices |

Focus research time proportionally. Do not spend three weeks on a low-priority detail while neglecting a critical one.

## Historical Research Protocols

### Daily Life Research Checklist

For any historical period, build a "daily life" reference document:

#### Morning to Night
- **Waking**: What time? What kind of bed? What is the first thing they hear, see, smell?
- **Hygiene**: Washing practices, dental care, grooming. Chamber pots, outhouses, or plumbing?
- **Dressing**: What do they wear? How many layers? How long does dressing take? Who helps?
- **Breakfast**: What food? How prepared? Where eaten? Who prepares it?
- **Work**: What does a typical workday look like? Hours, tasks, rhythms, breaks
- **Meals**: What is eaten? How many meals per day? What utensils? What beverages?
- **Social interaction**: Who do they see? How do they greet each other? What are the norms?
- **Evening**: How is the evening spent? Lighting (candles, oil lamps, gas, electric)? Entertainment?
- **Sleep**: When? In what conditions? Alone or shared? Nighttime fears and rituals?

#### The Sensory Palette
- **Dominant smells**: Every era and locale has a characteristic smell. Pre-modern cities: dung, smoke, food, sweat, flowers. Industrial cities: coal, oil, chemical processes, fog
- **Soundscape**: What is the background noise? Horse hooves, church bells, factory whistles, birdsong, traffic, silence?
- **Light quality**: How bright is a room lit by candles vs. gas lamps vs. electric light? How does this affect behavior?
- **Texture**: What surfaces do characters touch? Rough-hewn wood, smooth marble, scratchy wool, fine silk?
- **Temperature**: How warm or cold are indoor spaces? What does climate control (or its absence) feel like?

#### Social Infrastructure
- **Communication**: How do people send messages? How long does it take? What are the implications of communication speed for plot?
- **Transportation**: How do people get around? How long does travel take? What does it feel like?
- **Money**: What is the currency? What do things cost? What is a character's economic class, and how does that shape their experience?
- **Law and order**: What rules govern behavior? What happens when rules are broken? Who enforces them?
- **Medicine**: What do people do when they are sick or injured? What works? What doesn't?
- **Religion**: What is the dominant belief system? How does it shape daily life? What is orthodoxy vs. heterodoxy?
- **Death**: How do people die? What are the funeral practices? How is grief expressed?

### Period Language Research

#### Building a Period Voice

**Step 1**: Read primary sources extensively -- letters, diaries, newspapers, court records, published fiction from the period. Absorb the rhythms, the vocabulary, the assumptions

**Step 2**: Create a "words to use" list -- period-appropriate terms that add flavor:
- Occupational terms: What did people call things in their daily work?
- Social terms: How did people address each other? What were the honorifics?
- Emotional vocabulary: How did people describe their feelings? (This changes dramatically across eras)
- Profanity and slang: What did people say when angry, surprised, or amused?

**Step 3**: Create a "words to avoid" list -- anachronisms that would break immersion:
- Concepts that did not exist yet (trauma, depression, self-esteem as psychological terms)
- Technology references (anything digital before 1970s, anything electrical before 1880s)
- Idioms with later origins ("the whole nine yards" -- uncertain origin, avoid before 1960s)
- Post-colonial terminology applied to pre-colonial periods

**Step 4**: Develop a compromise voice -- not authentic period language (which would be incomprehensible to modern readers for anything before about 1800) but a suggestive voice that feels appropriate without being opaque

### Working with Uncertainty

Historical research inevitably encounters gaps -- periods, places, or social groups for which evidence is sparse:

**When you don't know**:
- Note the gap honestly in your research journal
- Look for analogous evidence (if you cannot find how peasants ate in 1250 Burgundy, check neighboring regions or adjacent decades)
- Make a considered decision and note it as such
- Prefer plausible invention over leaving a gap that weakens the narrative
- Do not present invention as fact in author's notes

**Common gaps in the historical record**:
- Women's experience before the modern era (recorded by men, if at all)
- Lower-class and enslaved people's perspectives (rarely literate, rarely recorded)
- Domestic and private life (public events are documented; private moments are not)
- Sensory experience (historians rarely recorded what things smelled or felt like)
- Emotional inner life (before the era of confessional diaries, roughly 17th century onward)

## Cultural Research Protocols

### The Authenticity Spectrum

| Level | Description | Appropriate For |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Insider voice** | Written by a member of the culture, drawing on lived experience | The gold standard, but not available to outside writers |
| **Deep immersion** | Extensive research including insider sources, sensitivity reading, community consultation | Serious literary fiction, work centered on the culture |
| **Informed respect** | Solid research, awareness of stereotypes and sensitivities, sensitivity reading | Characters from other cultures in an ensemble |
| **Background acknowledgment** | Basic accuracy, no offensive stereotypes | Minor characters, brief encounters |

### Cultural Research Checklist

Before writing characters from a culture not your own:

- [ ] Read at least 5 works of fiction by writers from this culture
- [ ] Read at least 2 works of non-fiction cultural analysis by members of this culture
- [ ] Identify the most common stereotypes and cliches associated with this culture in your own culture's media
- [ ] Understand the culture's internal diversity (regional, generational, class, political)
- [ ] Know what is sacred or sensitive and should be handled with particular care
- [ ] Plan for sensitivity reading by a member of the culture
- [ ] Check: Are your characters full people with agency, or are they serving the protagonist's journey?

### Common Cultural Research Failures

**The monolith**: Treating a culture as if everyone in it thinks, acts, and believes the same things. "Japanese people are..." No. 125 million Japanese people have 125 million individual perspectives

**The noble savage / magical minority**: Portraying marginalized cultures as inherently wise, spiritual, or morally superior. This is dehumanizing even when well-intentioned -- it denies characters the right to be ordinary, flawed, petty, and complex

**The trauma tour**: Centering a narrative about a marginalized culture on their suffering, especially when the protagonist is an outsider who witnesses and is transformed by that suffering. The marginalized characters become props for the protagonist's growth

**The cuisine-and-clothing culture**: Reducing a culture to its most visible external markers -- food, clothing, holidays, music -- without engaging with its values, conflicts, humor, or interior life

**The contemporary gloss**: Projecting modern Western values onto historical or non-Western cultures. Not every historical woman was a secret feminist. Not every non-Western culture defines freedom, success, or happiness the way contemporary Western culture does

## Technical Research Protocols

### Professional Verisimilitude Framework

For any profession a character practices, research in three tiers:

**Tier 1: What the public knows**
- The basic function of the profession
- General procedures visible to outsiders
- Common stereotypes (to either use deliberately or subvert)

**Tier 2: What practitioners experience**
- The rhythms and routines of daily work
- The jargon used among colleagues (vs. with outsiders)
- The specific frustrations, satisfactions, and ethical dilemmas
- The hierarchy, politics, and culture of the workplace
- Physical sensations: What does this work feel like in the body?

**Tier 3: What experts debate**
- Cutting-edge developments in the field
- Controversies and disagreements
- Where theory meets messy reality
- The difference between how the profession presents itself and how it actually operates

For most fiction, Tier 1 + Tier 2 is sufficient. Tier 3 is needed when the profession is central to the plot and expert readers are in the target audience.

### Medical Procedure Checklist
- Is the condition accurately described?
- Is the treatment appropriate for the era?
- Are the symptoms and progression realistic?
- Would a medical professional find the portrayal believable?
- Are the emotional and physical experiences of the patient authentic?

### Legal Procedure Checklist
- Is the court procedure accurate for the jurisdiction?
- Are legal terms used correctly?
- Is the timeline of legal proceedings realistic?
- Do the lawyers behave as real lawyers would?
- Are the outcomes plausible within the legal system described?

### Military Procedure Checklist
- Are ranks, units, and command structures correct for the branch and era?
- Are weapons, equipment, and tactics appropriate?
- Is the experience of combat (fear, exhaustion, confusion) authentically portrayed?
- Are the rules of engagement and military culture accurately represented?
- Does the depiction avoid both glorification and gratuitous horror?

## Scientific Research Protocols

### The Plausibility Test

For any speculative element, apply this three-part test:

1. **Is it consistent with known science?** If yes, you're on solid ground
2. **Does it contradict known science?** If yes, you need either a compelling explanation or to accept that you're writing soft SF / science fantasy
3. **Is it in a domain where science is genuinely uncertain?** If yes, you have creative freedom, but your speculation should be informed

### Building From Real Science

**Step 1**: Understand the current science thoroughly enough to identify the boundary between known and unknown

**Step 2**: Identify the speculative leap -- the point where you depart from established science

**Step 3**: Extrapolate consequences rigorously. If X is possible in your world, what else would be different? Technologies ripple outward

**Step 4**: Check for unintended consequences. "Why haven't they used this technology to solve obvious problem Y?" If your world has teleportation, it has implications for shipping, warfare, borders, crime, and daily life

### Common Science Fiction Research Errors

- **The isolated innovation**: A world that has one revolutionary technology but everything else is exactly like today. Technologies have cascading effects
- **The biology shortcut**: Alien species that are humans in makeup. Real biology would produce radically different organisms with radically different senses, cognition, and social structures
- **The scale error**: Distances, times, and energies in space that don't add up. Space is very, very big. Interstellar travel involves enormous energies. Planets are not next door to each other
- **The monoculture planet**: An entire planet with one climate, one culture, and one government. Earth itself demonstrates this is implausible

## Research Organization

### The Reference Document

For each project, maintain a single reference document organized by:

1. **Timeline** (chronological reference for events in the story)
2. **Settings** (physical descriptions, sensory details, maps)
3. **Characters** (profession-specific knowledge each character needs)
4. **Technology/Science** (what's possible, what's not, what's speculative)
5. **Culture** (social rules, values, taboos, humor)
6. **Language** (period/culture-appropriate vocabulary, things to avoid)
7. **Open questions** (things still unresolved, decisions still to make)

### When to Stop Researching

Research can become procrastination. Stop when:
- You can visualize the daily life of your characters in specific sensory detail
- You know enough to write with authority and cut with confidence
- Your remaining questions are answerable as they arise (you don't need to know everything before you start)
- You have been reading the same information in multiple sources (diminishing returns)
- You are excited to write and research is dampening that excitement

The purpose of research is to make writing possible, not to substitute for writing.

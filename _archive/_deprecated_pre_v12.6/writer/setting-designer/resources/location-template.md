# Location Design Framework

This is not a form to fill out — it is a thinking tool for creating settings that live and breathe. A well-designed location should feel like a character: it has personality, history, moods, and secrets. Every section below is a lens through which to examine a place until it becomes specific, particular, and irreplaceable.

## Quick Reference: Setting Diagnostic Questions

Before finalizing any location, ask these questions:
1. What is this place's one-word personality? (Oppressive? Welcoming? Indifferent? Hungry?)
2. What sense dominates here? (Sight? Sound? Smell? Touch? Taste?)
3. What single specific detail makes this place irreplaceable?
4. What happened here before the scene begins? (Every space has a past)
5. How does this space make the character feel, and what does it make them notice?
6. Could this scene work in a different location? If yes, why is it set here?
7. What does this place look like at a different time of day, in a different season?
8. What does this place sound like? Smell like? What temperature is it?
9. Where are the exits? Where does the light come from? What's on the floor?
10. What would a stranger notice first? What would they miss that a regular would know?

These questions prevent generic settings. A location that survives all ten has earned its place in the story.

## I. Identity and Purpose

### The Essence
Every location needs a single animating quality — the thing that makes it *this place* and not a generic alternative. Hill House's wrongness. The Overlook Hotel's hungry patience. Hogwarts's benevolent chaos. The Death Star's institutional menace. Find the one word or phrase that captures the location's essential character, then build everything else from that core.

### Narrative Function
What does this place *do* for the story? Locations serve narrative purposes beyond housing scenes:
- **Crucible**: A place that pressures characters into transformation (the island in Lord of the Flies, the lifeboat in Life of Pi)
- **Mirror**: A place that reflects a character's inner state (the decaying mansion for the decaying family)
- **Antagonist**: A place that actively opposes the characters (the mountain in The Lord of the Rings, the ocean in The Perfect Storm)
- **Threshold**: A place marking transition between states (the train station, the border crossing, the airport)
- **Sanctuary**: A place of safety that may become a trap (the castle, the bunker, the gated community)
- **Arena**: A place designed for conflict (the courtroom, the battlefield, the stage)

A location that serves no narrative function is set dressing. Cut it or give it purpose.

### Thematic Resonance
The best locations embody their story's themes physically. A story about memory set in a palimpsest city where old buildings show through new ones. A story about freedom set in a landscape of walls, fences, and locked doors. A story about decay set in a house where everything is slightly rotten — not obviously, but in the smell, the soft spots in the floor, the rust that creeps across every metal surface.

## II. The Sensory Architecture

### Primary Sense
Choose the dominant sensory channel for this location. This is the sense that defines the place's character:
- **Sight-dominant**: A cathedral with stained glass. A neon-lit street. A blank white room.
- **Sound-dominant**: A factory floor. A forest at night. A library where every whisper carries.
- **Smell-dominant**: A bakery. A tannery. A hospital ward. A home after cooking.
- **Touch-dominant**: A freezing cellar. A steam room. A thorny garden path.
- **Taste-dominant**: Rare, but powerful. Salt air at the coast. Dust in a desert ruin. The metallic taste in a room with old blood.

### Sensory Palette
For each sense, identify the specific details — not categories, but particular sensations:

**Sight**: Not "it was dark" but the quality of darkness — the blue-black of a moonlit room, the absolute void of a windowless basement, the amber dimness of a space lit by one bulb with a yellowed shade.

**Sound**: Not "it was quiet" but the particular texture of this silence — the hum of a refrigerator, the tick of cooling metal, the occasional car passing like a wave approaching and receding, the sound of your own breathing becoming loud.

**Smell**: The most memory-linked sense. Specific and evocative: machine oil and cold concrete (a parking garage). Old paper and lemon polish (a library). Chlorine and echoing voices (a swimming pool). Wet dog and boot-tracked mud (a country house entrance).

**Touch and temperature**: The stickiness of a bar counter. The particular coldness of marble versus concrete versus metal. The texture of a wall — smooth plaster, rough brick, flocked wallpaper. Air itself has texture — dry and electric before a storm, thick and wet in tropical heat, thin and sharp at altitude.

**Kinesthetic sense**: The floor's stability or instability. The way a room makes you feel tall or small. The pull of a steep staircase. The vertigo of a height. The disorientation of a too-symmetrical corridor. The body's relationship with the space.

### Sensory Shifts
How does the location's sensory profile change? Most locations are not static:
- **By time of day**: The office that buzzes with fluorescent light and chatter at noon becomes a humming cavern of screen-glow at midnight
- **By season**: The garden that riots with color and bee-song in summer becomes a skeleton of bare branches and frost in winter
- **By weather**: The cheerful street becomes menacing in fog, melancholy in rain, harsh in midday heat
- **By event**: The ballroom before the party (echoing, expectant) versus during (warm, loud, perfumed) versus after (devastated, stale air, crushed flowers)

## III. Spatial Design

### Movement and Flow
How do people move through this space? Movement patterns reveal power dynamics and create narrative possibilities.

**Chokepoints**: Narrow passages, single doorways, bridges. These are natural sites for confrontation, gatekeeping, and dramatic bottlenecks. Who controls the chokepoint controls access.

**Sight lines**: What can you see from where? What is hidden? Sight lines determine who has information — the person on the balcony overlooking the courtyard, the bartender who sees everyone who enters, the office with no windows facing the corridor.

**Escape routes**: Always know how a character could leave (or couldn't). The room with one door. The cliff with no path down. The elevator that takes three minutes to arrive. Knowing the exits matters for both action scenes and psychological tension.

**Gathering spaces**: Where do people naturally cluster? The water cooler, the kitchen, the front porch, the market square. These are information exchange points and sites of social performance.

### Scale and Proportion
How does the space relate to the human body?

**Intimate scale** (arm's reach): Closets, phone booths, car interiors, hospital beds. These spaces force proximity and vulnerability. Two people in a space this small cannot avoid each other.

**Human scale** (a room): Living rooms, offices, classrooms. Designed for human occupation. Comfortable or uncomfortable depending on how many people share them.

**Monumental scale** (dwarfing the human): Cathedrals, atriums, canyons, open ocean. These spaces make individuals feel small — which can be awe-inspiring, humbling, or terrifying depending on context.

**Infinite scale** (no visible boundary): Desert, ocean, space, blizzard whiteout. The absence of spatial reference points creates disorientation and existential exposure.

### Vertical Dimension
Height is meaning:
- **Ascent**: Climbing stairs, scaling walls, riding elevators up. Aspiration, effort, revelation, exposure. What you see from above is truth stripped of clutter.
- **Descent**: Going underground, walking downhill, taking stairs to a basement. Entering the subconscious, uncovering secrets, approaching danger. What is buried demands excavation.
- **Level ground**: Equality, stasis, the ordinary. Neither climbing nor falling — just being. Sometimes the most powerful choice.

## IV. Temporal Layers

### History Made Visible
Every space accumulates time. The best locations show their history without explaining it:
- **Architectural palimpsest**: The Victorian house with 1970s kitchen, 1990s bathroom, and original crown molding. Each era of renovation tells a story of changing owners, changing tastes, changing economics.
- **Wear patterns**: The brass doorknob polished smooth by centuries of hands. The stone step hollowed by generations of feet. The paint worn to bare wood where shoulders lean while waiting.
- **Traces of former occupants**: Nail holes where pictures hung. Rectangles of brighter paint where furniture blocked the sun. A child's height marks penciled on a doorframe. Previous owners are ghosts made visible through small marks.
- **Geological time**: Exposed rock layers, river-carved canyons, glacial erratics. The landscape contains its own autobiography in stone, readable to those who know the language.

### The Location's Lifecycle
Spaces are born, mature, decay, and sometimes are reborn:
- **Construction/creation**: New, raw, still smelling of paint and sawdust
- **Prime**: Functioning as intended, alive with purpose
- **Maturity**: Worn but comfortable, accumulated personality
- **Decline**: Neglected, crumbling, haunted by its better days
- **Ruin**: Surrendered to nature, structure yielding to entropy
- **Repurposing**: The church become a nightclub, the factory become lofts, the school become apartments

Where a location is in its lifecycle profoundly affects its mood and meaning.

## V. Atmosphere Engineering

### Mood as Technique
Atmosphere is not accidental — it is engineered through deliberate choices:

**Light**: The single most powerful atmospheric tool. Harsh fluorescent light creates clinical exposure. Candlelight creates intimacy and uncertainty. Firelight creates primal comfort. Moonlight creates dreamlike unreality. No light creates fear. The quality, direction, color, and intensity of light determine a scene's emotional register before a single word of dialogue is spoken.

**Sound design**: Background sound is emotional infrastructure. The drip of a faucet creates tension through repetition. Wind creates isolation. Distant music creates longing. Absolute silence creates dread. Ambient sound should be as deliberate as a film score.

**Air quality**: Stuffy air creates oppression. Fresh air creates relief. Cold air creates alertness and vulnerability. Humid air creates languor. Smoky air creates disorientation and danger. The air itself is an atmospheric instrument most writers neglect.

**Clutter versus emptiness**: Dense, cluttered spaces create visual noise, confusion, or cozy abundance. Sparse, empty spaces create clarity, exposure, or desolation. The amount of *stuff* in a space communicates wealth, personality, mental state, and cultural values.

### Contrast and Transition
The most powerful atmospheric moments happen at transitions:
- **Indoor to outdoor**: The shock of cold air leaving a warm house. The sensory assault of a crowded street after a quiet room. The relief of open sky after confinement.
- **Light to dark**: The blindness of entering a dark room from sunlight. The glare of emerging from a cave. The gradual adjustment of eyes.
- **Quiet to loud**: The wall of noise entering a concert hall. The ringing silence after an explosion. The particular quality of sound changing as you round a corner.

These transitions are sensory events — the reader's nervous system mirrors the character's adjustment.

## VI. The Inhabited Space

### Signs of Life
The difference between a set and a setting is habitation. Signs that real people use this space:
- **Detritus**: Coffee rings, crumbs, pet hair, junk mail, lost socks, dead batteries in a drawer
- **Personalization**: Photos on walls, magnets on fridges, stickers on laptops, plants in windows
- **Workarounds**: The door propped open with a brick, the wobbly table shimmed with a folded napkin, the window that needs a stick to stay up
- **Routines made visible**: The worn path in the carpet from bed to bathroom, the stack of newspapers by the front door, the collection of take-out menus by the phone

### Spaces That Resist
Some locations fight their occupants. The house that's always cold no matter the thermostat. The office chair that adjusts itself back overnight. The garden that refuses to grow anything but weeds. When a space resists domestication, it acquires a form of agency — it becomes a character.

### Genius Loci
The spirit of a place — the accumulated impression of every person who has occupied it, every event that has occurred within its walls. Some spaces feel lucky, others cursed, others merely sad. This isn't supernatural (necessarily) — it's the cumulative effect of design, history, light, proportion, and association. When a space has a genius loci, characters respond to it instinctively, even if they can't articulate why.

## VII. Color and Light

### Color as Emotional Language
Color in setting description is never neutral. It carries emotional weight that operates below conscious reading:

**Warm palette** (reds, oranges, ambers, golds): Energy, warmth, passion, danger, appetite, urgency. A room described in warm tones feels alive, sometimes oppressively so. Firelight, candlelight, sunset — warm light sources create intimacy and primal comfort.

**Cool palette** (blues, greys, silvers, whites): Calm, distance, melancholy, clarity, sterility, death. Moonlight, fluorescent light, overcast sky — cool light sources create detachment and sometimes dread. The blue-white of a hospital corridor. The grey of a prison yard.

**Muted palette** (browns, tans, faded colors): Age, poverty, exhaustion, earthiness, reliability. The world of faded wallpaper and worn wood and dust-colored roads. Muted settings feel established and tired — places where drama is quietly accumulated rather than sudden.

**Saturated palette** (vivid, intense colors): Youth, wealth, sensory overload, the exotic, the artificial. Markets in full color. Neon signs. Stained glass. Saturated settings demand attention and suggest either abundance or artificiality.

**Monochrome**: Any single-color dominance creates a mood of uniformity, which can be peaceful (a green forest) or oppressive (an all-white institution) or eerie (an all-grey city under fog). Breaking monochrome with a single contrasting detail creates instant focus — the red coat in Schindler's List.

### Light Direction and Quality
**Direct light** (sun, spotlights, headlights): Creates sharp shadows, high contrast, clarity. Things in direct light are exposed. Things outside it are hidden. Direct light is interrogation — it demands truth.

**Diffused light** (overcast, curtained windows, indirect): Creates soft edges, low contrast, ambiguity. Diffused light is forgiving — it hides flaws, blurs boundaries, creates a dreamlike quality. Overcast days make everything equally visible but nothing vivid.

**Backlight** (light behind subject): Creates silhouettes, halos, mystery. A character backlit in a doorway is half-seen, their expression unreadable. Backlight is the visual equivalent of an unreliable narrator.

**Uplighting** (light from below): Creates unnatural shadows on faces — the flashlight-under-the-chin effect. Uplighting makes the familiar monstrous because it reverses the shadow patterns we associate with normal daylight.

### Darkness as Design Element
Darkness is not the absence of setting — it is a setting with its own properties. Total darkness heightens every other sense. Partial darkness (moonlight, distant streetlamps, a screen's glow) creates a world of selective revelation where what you can't see is more important than what you can. Darkness liberates and terrifies in equal measure — the things people do in darkness are either their most authentic or their most dangerous.

## VIII. Weather and Climate

### Weather as Narrative Tool
Weather should never be mere backdrop. It should function as one or more of:
- **Obstacle**: The storm that prevents escape, the fog that causes the wrong turn, the ice that makes the road impassable
- **Mirror**: Weather that reflects emotional state (with restraint — the pathetic fallacy overdone is parody)
- **Counterpoint**: Weather that ironically contrasts with the emotional content (the beautiful day at the funeral)
- **Timer**: Weather that creates deadline pressure (the approaching storm, the rising flood, the winter that will trap them)
- **Sensory anchor**: Weather as the dominant physical experience of a scene, grounding the reader in the body

### Seasonal Architecture
Each season offers a different dramatic palette:

**Spring**: Growth, mud, unpredictability, fragile hope. Spring weather is changeable — warm one hour, cold the next. Good for stories about uncertain new beginnings, recovery, and the risk of false starts.

**Summer**: Abundance, heat, stagnation, exposure. Long days mean no hiding in darkness. Heat creates physical discomfort that raises irritability. Good for stories about pressure, excess, and things that ripen past their prime.

**Autumn**: Decline, beauty in death, harvest and preparation, nostalgia. Autumn light is golden and melancholy. Good for stories about endings, maturity, letting go, and gathering strength before hardship.

**Winter**: Endurance, clarity, isolation, death and dormancy. Short days and long nights. Cold as a physical adversary. Good for stories about survival, intimacy born from confinement, and the stripping away of everything inessential.

## IX. Integration Principles

### The Cardinal Rule
**Never stop the story to describe a setting.** The reader should experience the setting through the character's action and perception, not through an authorial pause. The setting enters the prose as the character encounters it — reaching for a glass on a counter reveals the counter's surface, walking through a door reveals the room, looking up during a conversation reveals the ceiling.

### Dynamic Description
Settings change within scenes. The room grows darker as the sun sets during a long conversation. The café empties as the evening crowd replaces the afternoon crowd. The rain starts halfway through the outdoor scene. Settings in motion feel alive. Static settings feel painted.

### Emotional Filtering
The same space looks different through different emotional lenses. An anxious character in a forest notices the density of trees, the loss of sight lines, the sounds of unseen creatures. A peaceful character in the same forest notices dappled light, birdsong, the soft carpet of pine needles. The setting is always filtered through the perceiver — and what a character notices reveals their inner state.

### Sound Design for Settings
Like a film score, a setting's ambient soundscape creates emotion before the reader is consciously aware of it:
- **Mechanical rhythm**: Ticking clocks, dripping faucets, humming generators. Repetitive sound creates tension through anticipation of when it will stop or change
- **Human ambient**: Distant conversation, laughter from another room, footsteps above. These sounds create the sense of social context — the character is not alone in the world
- **Natural ambient**: Wind, rain, birdsong, waves, insect hum. These create a relationship between the character and the non-human world
- **Silence types**: There is no single silence. The loaded silence of a room after an argument. The peaceful silence of early morning. The eerie silence of a place that should have sound but doesn't. Each silence has a distinct emotional charge

### Smell as Memory Trigger
Smell is the most memory-linked sense — it bypasses conscious thought and connects directly to emotional memory. Use this:
- **Institutional smells**: Floor wax, disinfectant, industrial cleaner. These create instant associations with schools, hospitals, prisons — institutions of control
- **Domestic smells**: Cooking, detergent, wood smoke, old books. These create associations with home, comfort, or domesticity — or their absence
- **Decay smells**: Mildew, rot, stale air, rust. These signal neglect, decline, and the passage of unattended time
- **Chemical smells**: Gasoline, paint, solvents, ozone. These signal industry, danger, or the artificial

The most effective smell details are specific and unexpected: "The elevator smelled like someone's lunch and carpet cleaner." "The corridor had the sweet, chemical smell of fresh linoleum that she would always associate with her mother's death."

### Texture and Tactile Design
Touch is the forgotten sense in most description, yet it is the most intimate:
- **Surface textures**: Rough, smooth, sticky, gritty, silky, pitted, polished. These require contact — they put the reader's body into the space
- **Temperature gradients**: The cold spot by the window. The warmth near the radiator. The chill rising from a stone floor. Temperature variation within a space creates a geography of comfort
- **Air texture**: Dry air that cracks lips and creates static. Humid air that makes clothes cling. Dusty air that catches in the throat. Cold air that stings the inside of the nose. Air is not nothing — it is the medium through which we experience every space
- **Resistance and yield**: The floor that gives slightly underfoot (old wood, thick carpet). The wall that crumbles when you lean on it. The door that sticks. The chair that wobbles. How a space responds to physical pressure reveals its age, construction, and maintenance

### The Exit Test
Can you remove this location description from the scene and replace it with "an unspecified room" without losing anything? If yes, your setting isn't working. If the description is doing its job, removing it should impoverish the scene — the character's actions, the emotional tone, and the thematic resonance should all depend on *this specific place*.

## X. The Revision Checklist

After drafting a location, run it through these filters:

### First Pass: Specificity
- Replace every generic detail with a specific one. Not "flowers" but "marigolds." Not "a chair" but "a wingback chair with a broken spring that listed slightly to the left."
- Remove any detail that could apply to any setting of this type. If you wrote "the restaurant was busy," replace it with how *this* restaurant is busy — the particular sound, the specific crowd, the distinctive energy.

### Second Pass: Sensory Balance
- Count the senses engaged. If only sight, add at least two more.
- Identify the dominant sense and ensure it carries the emotional weight.
- Check for the body — does the character feel temperature, gravity, the surface they're standing on?

### Third Pass: Narrative Function
- Circle every detail that does narrative work (mood, character, theme, history, foreshadowing).
- Delete any detail that only describes. Description without function is dead weight.
- Ensure the setting actively participates in the scene's emotional arc — it should shift, respond, or resist.

### Fourth Pass: Integration
- Verify that setting details are woven into action and thought, not presented in a descriptive block.
- Check that the character's emotional state colors their perception of the space.
- Confirm that movement through the space reveals something about the character.

# Synthesis and Reporting

Guide to synthesizing research findings into actionable outputs and presenting them effectively.

## Synthesis Process

### From Data to Insight

Raw data does not inform decisions. Synthesis transforms data into meaning:

```
Raw Data  -->  Organized Data  -->  Findings  -->  Insights  -->  Recommendations
(notes,        (coded, themed,     (patterns,     (meaning,      (actions,
 numbers,       categorized)        trends,        implications,   priorities,
 documents)                         gaps)          opportunities)  trade-offs)
```

### Synthesis Steps

1. **Consolidate all data sources** into a single workspace
   - Interview notes, survey results, analytics, documents
   - Tag each item with its source for traceability

2. **Identify convergent findings** (multiple sources agree)
   - These are your highest-confidence findings
   - Note the number and diversity of sources that converge

3. **Identify divergent findings** (sources disagree)
   - Investigate why: different contexts, roles, timeframes?
   - Present both perspectives with context rather than forcing agreement

4. **Extract patterns and themes**
   - What comes up repeatedly across sources?
   - What connects seemingly unrelated findings?
   - What is conspicuously absent from the data?

5. **Derive implications**
   - For each major finding, ask: "So what? What does this mean for the decision?"
   - Connect findings to the original research question explicitly

6. **Formulate recommendations**
   - Each recommendation must trace back to one or more findings
   - Provide rationale and expected impact for each
   - Acknowledge trade-offs and risks

### Synthesis Quality Checks

- [ ] Every recommendation is supported by at least two independent findings
- [ ] Divergent data is acknowledged rather than hidden
- [ ] The connection from data to finding to insight to recommendation is traceable
- [ ] Assumptions are stated explicitly
- [ ] Limitations of the research are documented
- [ ] Alternative interpretations have been considered

## Report Structures

### Executive Summary Report (1-2 pages)

**Audience**: Decision-makers who need the bottom line.

**Structure**:
1. **Research question** (1-2 sentences)
2. **Methodology summary** (1-2 sentences)
3. **Key findings** (3-5 bullet points)
4. **Recommendations** (2-4 prioritized actions)
5. **Next steps** (immediate actions required)

**Writing guidance**: Lead with the conclusion. Use plain language. Avoid jargon. Every sentence must advance the reader toward a decision.

### Full Research Report (5-15 pages)

**Audience**: Stakeholders who need detail and evidence.

**Structure**:
1. **Executive summary** (1 page)
2. **Background and context** (why this research was conducted)
3. **Research questions** (specific questions addressed)
4. **Methodology** (approach, data sources, limitations)
5. **Findings** (organized by theme or question, with evidence)
6. **Analysis and discussion** (interpretation, implications)
7. **Recommendations** (prioritized, with rationale)
8. **Appendices** (raw data, detailed methodology, supplementary analysis)

**Writing guidance**: Use headings liberally. Include tables and visuals. Provide evidence for every claim. Cross-reference between sections.

### Decision Brief (1 page)

**Audience**: A single decision-maker or committee facing a specific choice.

**Structure**:
1. **Decision required** (what must be decided)
2. **Options** (2-4 options with brief description)
3. **Evaluation criteria** (what matters most)
4. **Comparison** (table scoring options against criteria)
5. **Recommendation** (preferred option with rationale)
6. **Risks** (key risks of the recommended option and mitigations)

**Writing guidance**: Be direct. Present trade-offs honestly. Make the recommendation clear but acknowledge it involves judgment.

## Presenting Research Findings

### Presentation Principles

1. **Start with the answer** - Do not build up to a conclusion; state it first
2. **Tell the story** - Structure findings as a narrative with a beginning (context), middle (evidence), and end (recommendation)
3. **Use visuals for data** - Tables, charts, and diagrams communicate faster than paragraphs
4. **Anticipate objections** - Address likely pushback proactively
5. **End with action** - Close with specific next steps and who owns them

### Visual Communication

| Data Type | Best Visual Format | When to Use |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|
| Comparisons | Side-by-side table or bar chart | Evaluating options |
| Trends over time | Line chart or timeline | Showing progression |
| Proportions | Pie chart or stacked bar | Showing composition |
| Relationships | Scatter plot or matrix | Showing correlations |
| Processes | Flow diagram or swimlane | Showing workflows |
| Hierarchies | Tree diagram or org chart | Showing structure |
| Geographies | Map or heat map | Showing distribution |

### Handling Questions and Challenges

| Challenge | Response Strategy |
|-----------|------------------|
| "Where did this data come from?" | Cite specific sources; offer to share raw data |
| "The sample size is too small" | Acknowledge limitation; explain why findings are still directional |
| "I've seen different numbers" | Ask for their source; reconcile or explain discrepancy |
| "This doesn't match my experience" | Validate their perspective; explain how data represents broader view |
| "What about option X?" | If researched, share the analysis; if not, note it as a gap for follow-up |

## Research Knowledge Management

### Research Handoff Checklist

When completing a research effort, ensure the following are accessible:

- [ ] Final report in the repository (organized by topic, tagged with methodology and key findings)
- [ ] Raw data and analysis files stored and labeled
- [ ] Methodology documented for reproducibility
- [ ] Key contacts and sources noted for follow-up
- [ ] Outstanding questions or future research needs documented
- [ ] Stakeholders notified that research is complete and where to find it

Before starting new research, always search the repository for existing findings. Revisit high-impact research annually.

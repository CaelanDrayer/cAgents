# Narrative Design Patterns & Techniques

## Scene/Sequel Deep Dive

### Scene Construction Workshop

A well-constructed scene is the fundamental unit of narrative momentum. Every scene must pass the three-part test:

**Goal Test**: Can you state in one sentence what the POV character wants in this scene? "She wants to convince her sister to testify." "He wants to find the missing file before the office opens." If you can't state the goal, the scene lacks direction.

**Conflict Test**: What actively opposes the goal? Not just difficulty — active opposition. Someone says no. The environment resists. Time runs out. Conflict isn't just "things are hard" — it's "something pushes back." The opposition should have its own logic, not exist merely to frustrate the protagonist.

**Disaster Test**: Does the scene end worse than it began for the POV character? "No, and furthermore..." (goal denied + new complication) is the strongest disaster. "Yes, but..." (goal achieved at unexpected cost) works too. "Yes" with no complications is the weakest — it resolves tension without creating new tension.

### Scene Types by Function

| Scene Type | Goal | Conflict Source | Typical Disaster |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Confrontation** | Defeat/persuade opponent | Direct opposition | Opponent wins or escalates |
| **Investigation** | Discover information | Hidden/guarded info | Info reveals worse problem |
| **Decision** | Choose between options | Internal conflict | Choice eliminates better option |
| **Escape** | Flee danger | Pursuit/obstacle | Escape leads to worse situation |
| **Negotiation** | Reach agreement | Competing interests | Agreement has hidden cost |
| **Revelation** | Understand truth | Deception/complexity | Truth is worse than ignorance |

### Sequel Construction Workshop

The sequel is where character depth lives. Action scenes reveal what characters do; sequels reveal who they are.

**The Emotional Beat**: The character's raw, unprocessed reaction. Don't rush past this. The reader needs to feel the impact of the disaster before the character starts analyzing it. Show physical manifestations — trembling hands, tight throat, tears, laughter, stillness. The body reacts before the mind processes.

**The Analytical Beat**: The character reviews their situation rationally. What just happened? What does it mean? What options remain? This is where natural exposition lives — a character taking stock can review relevant information without info-dumping because the review is motivated by their situation.

**The Decision Beat**: The character chooses their next action. This choice must feel earned — a product of both the emotional reaction (what they feel) and the rational analysis (what they know). The decision becomes the next scene's goal, restarting the cycle.

### Sequel Compression Across a Novel

```
Act One:     Full sequels (2-5 pages each)
             Character has time to feel, think, decide
             Reader gets to know the character's inner landscape

Early Act Two: Moderate sequels (1-2 pages)
               Emotional beat + compressed thought + quick decision
               Pace increases as complications mount

Late Act Two:  Brief sequels (1-3 paragraphs)
               Single emotional flash + immediate decision
               The character can't afford to stop and think

Climax:        Minimal sequels (1-2 sentences)
               Emotion and action blur together
               Scene chains with barely a breath between disasters

Resolution:    Extended sequel (the final sequel)
               The character processes everything that happened
               The reader processes alongside them
```

This compression is what creates the sensation of acceleration in a well-paced novel. The story feels faster even though the scene count may be consistent.

## MRU Precision Workshop

### Correct MRU Sequence — Examples

**Correct Order**:
```
STIMULUS: The door slammed open. [external event]
REACTION:
  1. She flinched. [involuntary physical]
  2. Fear flooded through her. [emotion]
  3. Not yet. He wasn't supposed to be here until midnight. [thought]
  4. She reached for the knife on the counter. [voluntary action]
```

**Incorrect Order** (common mistake):
```
She reached for the knife, afraid, because the door had slammed open
and he wasn't supposed to be here until midnight.
[Action before stimulus, thought mixed with emotion, no physical reaction]
```

**Compressed MRU** (for fast pacing):
```
The gun fired. She hit the floor, heart hammering, already crawling
toward the exit.
[Stimulus → physical reaction + emotion → action; thought omitted for speed]
```

**Extended MRU** (for emotional depth):
```
STIMULUS: "Your mother died this morning," the doctor said.
REACTION:
  1. The room tilted. Her knees buckled. [involuntary physical]
  2. Something cracked open inside her chest — not grief, not yet,
     but the terrible awareness that grief was coming, enormous
     and inescapable, and she was not ready. [emotion — extended]
  3. This morning. While she'd been arguing about parking tickets.
     While she'd been making coffee and checking email and living
     her ordinary, stupid, irreplaceable life. [thought — extended]
  4. "Can I see her?" [voluntary action — speech]
```

### MRU Density and Pacing

- **High MRU density** (many stimulus-response pairs per paragraph): Creates intensity, immersion, real-time sensation. Use for action, confrontation, crisis.
- **Low MRU density** (few stimulus-response pairs, more interiority): Creates contemplation, depth, emotional processing. Use for sequels, reflective passages, character study.

The transition between high and low density IS pacing. Fast scenes have dense MRUs. Slow scenes have sparse MRUs with extended internal responses.

## Opening Strategies: Decision Framework

### Opening Type Selection Guide

| If the story's primary hook is... | Open with... | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Action/tension** | In medias res | "The explosion threw her sideways" |
| **Mystery/curiosity** | Cold open or prologue | "Three things about the murder didn't add up" |
| **Character/voice** | Character introduction | "I'm the kind of person who..." |
| **World/concept** | World introduction | "On this planet, the rain fell upward" |
| **A story within a story** | Frame narrative | "Let me tell you about the last time I saw my father" |

### Opening Lines That Work (and Why)

**The Declarative Hook**: "It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." (Orwell, *1984*) — Everything is normal except one detail. That one wrong note makes the reader need to know why.

**The Voice Hook**: "If you really want to hear about it, the first thing you'll probably want to know is where I was born, and what my lousy childhood was like." (Salinger, *Catcher in the Rye*) — The voice is so distinctive that the reader follows it anywhere.

**The Situation Hook**: "It was a pleasure to burn." (Bradbury, *Fahrenheit 451*) — An impossible emotional response (pleasure + destruction) creates immediate cognitive dissonance that demands resolution.

**The Mystery Hook**: "Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again." (du Maurier, *Rebecca*) — Implies loss, nostalgia, and a story that has already happened. The reader wants to know what Manderley is and why the narrator can't return.

### What NOT to Open With

- **The Alarm Clock**: Character waking up. The most cliched opening in fiction. It signals "nothing interesting has happened yet."
- **Weather Without Purpose**: "It was a dark and stormy night" fails because the weather is generic atmosphere, not specific detail. Weather openings work only when the weather is the story.
- **The Mirror Description**: Character studying their reflection to provide physical description. Transparent and self-conscious. Let appearance emerge through action and other characters' observations.
- **The Info-Dump Paragraph**: "The city of Vartheon had stood for three thousand years, founded by the Twelve Great Mages after the Sundering of..." — Encyclopedia entries belong in appendices, not openings.
- **The Dream Sequence**: Opening with a dream that turns out to be a dream. The reader feels cheated — they invested in a scene that didn't happen. Dreams can open stories only when the dream itself is the story.

## Chapter Design Templates

### The Action Chapter

```
Hook:       Immediate tension (mid-scene or aftermath of previous chapter)
Rising:     Progressive complications within the scene
Peak:       The scene's disaster — worst possible outcome
Transition: Brief sequel beat (emotion only, no extended thought)
Pull:       Cliffhanger or unresolved question
Length:     Short (5-10 pages) — maintains urgency
```

### The Revelation Chapter

```
Hook:       A question posed or a mystery deepened
Build:      Information accumulated through investigation/dialogue
Turn:       The moment of discovery — the puzzle piece clicks
Reframe:    Everything prior seen in new light
Pull:       The implication — what this revelation means for what comes next
Length:      Medium (10-15 pages) — needs room for setup and impact
```

### The Character Chapter

```
Hook:       An emotional moment or internal conflict surfaced
Deepening:  Extended sequel — the character processes, remembers, wrestles
Decision:   The character arrives at understanding or commitment
Test:       A scene that tests the understanding (does it hold under pressure?)
Pull:       The test's result and its implications
Length:      Medium to long (12-20 pages) — needs room for interiority
```

### The Convergence Chapter

```
Hook:       Multiple threads visibly heading toward collision
Intercut:   Rapid switching between perspectives/threads
Collision:  The threads meet — conflict, revelation, or transformation
Aftermath:  Immediate consequences of the convergence
Pull:       New trajectory established by the collision
Length:      Long (15-25 pages) — convergence needs space
```

### Chapter Length as Meaning

| Length | Effect | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Very short (1-3 pages) | Punch, emphasis, shock | Plot twists, dramatic reveals, time jumps |
| Short (5-8 pages) | Urgency, pace | Action sequences, tense confrontations |
| Medium (10-15 pages) | Balance | Standard narrative scenes with full scene/sequel |
| Long (18-25 pages) | Immersion, depth | Character development, complex set pieces |
| Very long (30+ pages) | Epic sweep, deep dive | Climactic sequences, pivotal scenes |

Variation between lengths creates rhythm. Three short chapters followed by a long chapter creates a sense of acceleration followed by deep immersion. The structural choice communicates before the reader processes the words.

## Transition Craft: Advanced Techniques

### The Thematic Bridge

Connect two scenes through shared imagery or theme, not shared event:

```
END:   She watched the last ember die in the fireplace. The room went cold.
       [Scene about the death of a relationship]

START: The first frost had killed the garden overnight.
       [Scene about a fresh start in a new city]
```

The connection (death/cold → frost/death → but now new context) links the scenes emotionally while jumping in time and space.

### The Emotional Echo

End one scene with an emotion; open the next with the same emotion in a different context:

```
END:   He'd never felt so alone. [After betrayal by a friend]

START: The lighthouse keeper hadn't spoken to another person in six months,
       and he preferred it that way. [Different character, different meaning
       of "alone" — chosen isolation vs. imposed isolation]
```

### The Contrast Cut

Juxtapose sharply different tones for emphasis:

```
END:   "I love you," she whispered. [Tender moment]

START: The first bullet took out the window. [Violence]
```

The contrast amplifies both — the tenderness feels more precious, the violence more shocking.

### The Question Bridge

End with a question; begin with its answer (or refusal to answer):

```
END:   Where was the money? [Investigation scene]

START: Fourteen years ago, a woman named Margaret Oakes opened a
       safety deposit box at First National. [Answer begins — but
       raises more questions]
```

## Information Revelation Strategies

### The Revelation Timeline

For any major secret or mystery, plan the revelation arc:

```
Chapter 1:  Plant first clue (the reader doesn't know it's a clue yet)
Chapter 5:  Plant second clue (the reader might start connecting)
Chapter 8:  Red herring (misdirect attention from the real trail)
Chapter 12: Third clue (the careful reader is now suspicious)
Chapter 15: Near-miss (almost revealed, then interrupted or misinterpreted)
Chapter 18: Context clue (something that will make the reveal meaningful)
Chapter 22: The reveal (all clues snap into focus; the reader says "of course")
```

### Dramatic Irony as Information Tool

When the reader knows something the character doesn't:
- Every scene involving that character gains a secondary layer of tension
- Dialogue becomes charged with double meaning
- The reader wants to shout at the character — that's engagement
- The longer you sustain the irony, the more powerful the resolution

When the character knows something the reader doesn't:
- Use sparingly — it can feel like cheating if the POV character withholds thoughts
- Works best in limited third person where the narrator has some distance
- The reveal should explain behavior that seemed odd in retrospect

### The Information Cascade

A technique for overwhelming reveals — each new piece of information triggers the release of another:

```
Reveal 1: The victim was alive. [Surprise]
  → This means Reveal 2: The "murder" was staged. [Recontextualization]
    → This means Reveal 3: The detective was set up. [Personal stakes]
      → This means Reveal 4: The real crime is something else entirely. [Scale shift]
```

Each reveal flows logically from the previous one. The cascade creates a feeling of accelerating understanding — the reader tumbles through revelations, each one clicking into place faster than the last.

## Narrative Momentum Engineering

### The Hook Chain

Momentum is maintained by overlapping hooks at different scales:

```
MACRO HOOK:     Who killed Professor Chen?
                (Sustained across entire novel)
                |
SECTION HOOK:   Why is the alibi witness lying?
                (Sustained across 5-8 chapters)
                |
CHAPTER HOOK:   What's behind the locked door?
                (Sustained across 1-2 chapters)
                |
SCENE HOOK:     Will she get the file before he returns?
                (Sustained across the scene)
                |
LINE HOOK:      "There's something you should know—"
                (Sustained across paragraphs)
```

At every moment, the reader has at least three active hooks pulling them forward. When one resolves, another must already be in place. The reader should never finish a hook resolution without a new hook already engaged.

### Momentum Killers and Fixes

| Momentum Killer | Why It Kills | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Extended backstory** | Stops forward motion for backward motion | Integrate into action via dialogue or interiority |
| **Repetitive conflict** | Same obstacle again = reader already knows the pattern | Vary the type of conflict, not just intensity |
| **Resolution without new question** | Reader has no reason to continue | Resolve one question while introducing another |
| **Passive protagonist** | Things happen TO them; no forward drive | Give the character active goals, even small ones |
| **Unearned reflection** | Character thinking about things that don't matter yet | Tie reflection to immediate plot needs |
| **Scene without stakes** | Nothing to lose = nothing to care about | Establish what's at risk before the scene begins |

### The Anticipation Engine

Create forward momentum by establishing future events the reader looks forward to or dreads:

- **The Appointment**: "They would meet at the bridge at midnight." Now every scene between now and midnight carries momentum — the reader is heading toward the bridge.
- **The Deadline**: "The storm would reach the coast in forty-eight hours." Now time pressure amplifies every scene.
- **The Threat**: "He would find her eventually. He always did." Now every quiet moment vibrates with potential danger.
- **The Promise**: "When this is over, I'll tell you everything." Now the reader endures present confusion for promised future clarity.

These anticipation anchors work because the reader's attention splits between the present scene and the approaching event. The present gains urgency from the future.

## Pacing Diagnostic Framework

### The Scene-by-Scene Audit

For each scene, record:
1. **Scene type**: Action / Dialogue / Reflection / Description
2. **Length**: Short / Medium / Long
3. **Tension level**: 1 (low) to 5 (high)
4. **Change delivered**: What's different at the scene's end
5. **Hooks**: What pulls the reader into the next scene

Plot these on a chart. Look for:
- Long stretches of the same scene type (monotony)
- Tension levels that plateau instead of rising (stagnation)
- Scenes that deliver no change (dead weight)
- Missing hooks between scenes (stopping points)

### The Chapter-End Audit

Read only the last paragraph of every chapter in sequence. Do they:
- Each create urgency to continue? (If not, the reader may stop there)
- Vary in type? (All cliffhangers becomes exhausting; all quiet endings loses momentum)
- Escalate over the course of the book? (Chapter-end hooks should intensify as the story progresses)

### The Reading Speed Test

Read the manuscript aloud. Note where you speed up (the writing is propulsive — good) and where you slow down or stop (the writing resists momentum — investigate). Your reading speed mirrors the reader's experience more accurately than silent reading.

## Advanced Narrative Techniques

### The Unreliable Narrator's Information Game

When the narrator is unreliable, information management becomes a game between author and reader:
- The narrator reveals truth through what they deny, avoid, or over-explain
- Contradictions between narrated events and the narrator's interpretation signal unreliability
- The reader becomes an active investigator, reading against the narrator
- The gap between what the narrator says happened and what actually happened IS the story

### The Delayed Scene

Establish a scene that must happen (the confrontation with the father, the conversation about the secret, the return to the childhood home) but delay it. Every chapter that passes without the scene increases its eventual impact. The delay itself creates anticipation and dread. When the scene finally arrives, it carries the accumulated weight of every chapter of waiting.

### Parallel Structure as Commentary

Two scenes with parallel structure but different content — same sequence of events, different characters, different outcomes. The structural echo invites comparison. The differences in outcome reveal theme. She knocks on the door and is welcomed; he knocks on the door and is rejected. The parallel says something about the world without stating it.

### The Time Compression/Expansion Technique

Narrative time and real time are different. A single moment of crisis can expand to fill ten pages (time expansion — creates intensity). A month of routine can compress to a sentence (time compression — maintains pace). The ratio between narrative length and temporal duration communicates importance. Spend the most pages on the moments that matter most.

### Scene/Sequel Theory (Dwight Swain)

The fundamental rhythm of compelling fiction alternates between two types of units:

**Scene** (action-focused):
- **Goal**: The POV character wants something specific and immediate. Not "wants to be happy" but "wants to persuade the witness to testify." Clear, concrete, achievable in this scene.
- **Conflict**: Opposition to the goal. Someone says no. Something goes wrong. The environment resists. Conflict must be active, not merely obstructive — the opposition should have its own agenda.
- **Disaster**: The scene ends badly. Not neutrally — badly. The character doesn't get what they want, or gets it at unexpected cost, or gets something worse than failure. The disaster drives the reader into the sequel because they need to know what happens next.

**Sequel** (reaction-focused):
- **Emotion**: The character's immediate, involuntary emotional response to the disaster. Grief. Fury. Terror. Despair. This must be felt, not summarized. The reader needs to experience the emotional impact before the character thinks about it.
- **Thought**: The character processes the disaster rationally. Considers options. Weighs costs. This is where exposition can live naturally — a character thinking through their situation can review relevant information without info-dumping.
- **Decision**: The character chooses their next action. This decision becomes the Goal of the next Scene. The cycle restarts.

**Varying the Pattern**: Not every scene needs a full sequel, and not every sequel needs all three beats. Action sequences may chain Scene after Scene with minimal Sequel. Reflective passages may be extended Sequels with minimal Scene. The pattern creates a baseline rhythm that variations play against.

**The Sequel Squeeze**: As tension rises through a story, Sequels should compress. Early in the novel, give characters time to feel, think, and decide. By the climax, Sequels shrink to a single sentence — the character reacts and immediately acts again. This compression creates the sensation of acceleration.

### Motivation-Reaction Units (MRUs)

The smallest building block of immersive fiction. An MRU is a stimulus followed by a response in strict causal order.

**Stimulus (Motivation)**: Something the POV character can perceive — something they see, hear, smell, or feel. The stimulus comes from outside the character. It must be concrete and sensory.

**Response (Reaction)**: The character's response, in strict physiological order:
1. **Involuntary physical reaction** (flinch, gasp, freeze — the body reacts before the mind)
2. **Emotional response** (fear, surprise, anger — the limbic system processes next)
3. **Rational thought** (assessment, planning, recognition — the cortex catches up)
4. **Voluntary action** (speech, deliberate movement, decision — conscious response)

**Why order matters**: This sequence mirrors how humans actually experience events. When a gun fires, you flinch first, then feel fear, then think "someone's shooting," then dive for cover. Reversing this order (he decided to take cover, hearing the gunshot) destroys the illusion of real-time experience. The reader unconsciously recognizes the correct order and experiences it as natural. The wrong order feels artificial even if the reader can't explain why.

**MRU violations to avoid**:
- Acting before perceiving (the character responds to something they haven't seen yet)
- Thinking before feeling (analysis before emotional reaction)
- Multiple simultaneous stimuli without sequential processing
- Stimulus without response (something happens and the character doesn't react)
- Response without stimulus (the character reacts to nothing observable)

### Opening Strategies

**In Medias Res (Mid-Action)**: Begin in the middle of a scene already in progress. The reader is immediately engaged by action and oriented gradually through context clues. Works best when: the action is inherently compelling, the situation is quickly comprehensible, and the backstory can be woven in during subsequent quieter moments. Danger: if the reader is too confused for too long, engagement becomes frustration.

**The Cold Open (No Context)**: Drop the reader into a situation with zero explanation. Even more extreme than in medias res — the reader may not know who the character is, where they are, or what's happening. Works when the mystery itself is engaging and answers come quickly enough to reward patience. Danger: patience is finite.

**The Character Introduction**: Open with the protagonist doing something characteristic — not their backstory, but their present behavior. Show their competence, flaw, and world through a single scene that demonstrates rather than explains. This is the most reliable opening strategy because it immediately establishes the reader's relationship with the protagonist.

**The World Introduction**: Open with the setting as character. The world is strange enough, vivid enough, or threatening enough to be inherently interesting. Works for speculative fiction where the world is the primary draw. Danger: a fascinating world without a compelling character is a travelogue, not a story.

**The Voice Introduction**: Open with the narrator's voice as the primary hook. The character's way of seeing and describing the world is so distinctive, so compelling, that the reader follows the voice regardless of initial events. Works for first-person and close-third narratives with strong authorial voice. Danger: voice without substance is tiresome.

**The Frame Narrative**: Open with someone about to tell a story. Creates immediate narrative tension (why is this story being told? to whom? what's at stake in the telling?) and the comfort of a guide. Works when the frame itself is a story worth following. Danger: the frame can postpone the real story.

**The Prologue (When It Works)**: Establishes information the main narrative can't efficiently convey — a historical event, a mystery, a time gap. The prologue works when: it establishes a question the main story will answer, it's in a different time/place/POV than Chapter One, and it's short. The prologue fails when: it's the first chapter mislabeled, it dumps exposition the narrative could reveal naturally, or it exists because the author couldn't figure out where to start.

### Chapter Architecture

**The Chapter as Self-Contained Experience**: Every chapter should have its own miniature arc — a question asked and answered (or deepened), a situation changed, a character moved. The reader should feel something at the end of each chapter that they didn't feel at the beginning.

**Opening Hooks**: The first sentences of every chapter must re-engage the reader. Techniques:
- **The Question**: Open with something that demands an answer. "The envelope was not where she had left it."
- **The Tension**: Open mid-conflict. "The third lie was the one that broke them."
- **The Curiosity**: Open with something strange or unexpected. "He hadn't expected the funeral to be catered."
- **The Continuation**: Open with the immediate aftermath of the previous chapter's cliffhanger. Satisfy one question, introduce another.

**Chapter Momentum**: The middle of a chapter should escalate — not just continue. Each paragraph should raise the stakes, complicate the situation, or deepen the emotional engagement. If the middle sags, the chapter is too long or contains scenes that don't advance the story.

**Closing Pulls**: The final sentences of every chapter should propel the reader into the next. Techniques:
- **The Cliffhanger**: Interrupt at the moment of maximum tension. "She opened the door."
- **The Reveal**: End with information that changes everything. "The letter was in his handwriting."
- **The Turning Point**: End with a decision or event that redirects the story. "She put down the phone and started packing."
- **The Emotional Resonance**: End with a moment of emotional truth that lingers. "For the first time in years, the house was quiet."
- **The Question**: End with an unanswered question. "But if he wasn't dead, where had he been?"

**Chapter Length as Rhythm Tool**: Short chapters (2-5 pages) create urgency, pace, and breathlessness. Long chapters (20-30 pages) create immersion, depth, and contemplation. Varying chapter length creates rhythm — the narrative equivalent of a musical tempo change. A short chapter between two long chapters creates emphasis through contrast.

### Transition Craft

**The Time-Skip Transition**: "Three weeks later" — simple, clear, effective. Use when what happens during the skip isn't narratively important. The danger: overuse creates a summary feel. Show the first day, skip the routine, show the day something changes.

**The Location Transition**: Moving between places. The simplest method: end one scene, white space, begin the next in the new location. The more sophisticated method: connect the locations thematically ("She left the courthouse feeling judged. The church felt no different.").

**The POV Transition**: Shifting between viewpoint characters. Best practice: signal the shift immediately in the first sentence of the new section. The reader should never be confused about whose head they're in. Chapter breaks are the cleanest POV transition; mid-chapter shifts require section breaks.

**The Seamless Transition**: The end of one scene and the beginning of the next are connected by theme, image, or language. "He watched the fire die. / Morning came cold." The dying fire and the cold morning are connected — the transition flows rather than jumps.

**The Hard Cut**: An abrupt, jarring transition used for effect. Cut from a tender moment to violence. Cut from hope to disaster. The contrast amplifies both scenes. Use sparingly — too many hard cuts create whiplash rather than impact.

**The Bridging Transition**: A brief connective passage — a paragraph or two — that links two scenes through the character's interiority. The character reflects on what just happened and anticipates what's coming. This is the lightest form of sequel.

### Information Revelation

**The Hierarchy of Mystery**: At every point in the narrative, the reader should have at least one unanswered question. Ideally, multiple questions at different scales — a micro-question (what's in the box?), a scene question (will the negotiation succeed?), and a macro-question (who killed the professor?). As questions are answered, new ones must emerge. A story without active questions is a story without tension.

**Information as Reward**: Readers continue reading because they expect to be rewarded with information — answers, context, revelation, understanding. Space these rewards strategically. Too frequent and the reader is satisfied too easily. Too infrequent and they lose faith that answers are coming. The rhythm of question-and-answer is the engine of engagement.

**The Drip-Feed**: Reveal information in small increments across many scenes. Each increment is insufficient by itself but contributes to a growing picture. The reader assembles meaning gradually, which creates investment — they've worked for their understanding. Works for mysteries, complex world-building, and character backstory.

**The Flood**: Reveal a large amount of information at once. Works when the sheer volume of revelation is itself dramatic — the moment when everything clicks into place, the scene where the protagonist learns the truth. The flood must be earned by preceding drip-feed. Without the buildup of partial knowledge, the flood has no impact.

**Exposition Integration**: Never info-dump. Information enters the narrative through:
- **Dialogue** (characters discussing what they know — but only when the conversation is natural, not when characters tell each other things they both already know)
- **Action** (the reader learns about the world by watching characters navigate it)
- **Interiority** (the character's thoughts, used when reflection is natural — during a sequel, during waiting, during quiet moments)
- **Setting** (environmental details that imply history, culture, technology, and social structure)
- **Conflict** (the most efficient delivery system — information revealed under pressure feels urgent and lands hard)

### Narrative Momentum

**The Page-Turner Effect**: Compulsive readability is engineered, not accidental. It emerges from the systematic application of micro-hooks, scene-ending tension, and the continuous promise that something is coming. The reader doesn't just want to know what happens — they feel they must know.

**Micro-Hooks (Sentence Level)**: Individual sentences that create forward pull. Questions embedded in prose. Statements that imply more than they say. Sentence endings that promise continuation. "She almost told him the truth." The word "almost" is a micro-hook — the reader needs to know why she didn't.

**Scene-Ending Hooks**: Every scene should end with something unresolved — a question, a threat, a decision unmade, an emotion unexpressed. The reader finishes the scene wanting to continue. If a scene ends with complete resolution, the reader has a natural stopping point. Eliminate stopping points.

**Chapter-Ending Hooks**: Stronger than scene-ending hooks. The chapter break is where readers are most likely to put the book down. The chapter ending must make putting the book down feel impossible. Cliffhangers, reveals, turning points, unanswered questions — deploy the strongest hooks at chapter ends.

**The Promise of Something Coming**: Foreshadowing, prophecy, deadlines, appointments, threats, plans — anything that tells the reader "something important will happen later" creates forward momentum. The reader reads through slower sections because they're heading toward something promised.

**Forward Momentum vs. Reflective Pause**: Not every moment should be urgent. The reader needs breathing room — moments of beauty, humor, tenderness, contemplation. But even reflective pauses should contain seeds of future tension. A quiet conversation can include an offhand comment that the reader files away as significant.

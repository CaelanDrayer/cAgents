# Best Practices: Narrative Designer

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality narrative architecture, reading experience engineering, information revelation design, and scene-sequel pacing work.

## Design Principles

- **Narrative Design Is Information Architecture**: The designer controls what the reader knows, when they learn it, and how that learning creates meaning. Narrative design is fundamentally the art of sequencing information for maximum effect.
- **Every Scene Has a Function**: Scenes are not just things that happen — they are structural units that serve specific functions (introduce conflict, reveal character, establish atmosphere, deliver information, change dynamics). Every scene must earn its place.
- **Scene/Sequel Rhythm Is a Metabolic Rate**: The alternation between action scenes and reflection/reaction scenes controls the reader's cognitive and emotional metabolism. Too much action is exhausting; too much sequel is stagnant. Calibrate the ratio to the work's desired effect.
- **Information Revelation Drives Engagement**: Reader engagement is powered by the management of two questions — "What's happening?" (curiosity) and "What will happen?" (anticipation). Narrative design engineers both through careful control of information timing.
- **Chapter Hooks Are Promises**: The ending of each chapter creates an implicit promise about what will be resolved or revealed next. Hooks that are too subtle fail to create momentum; hooks that are too obvious feel manipulative. Find the sweet spot.
- **Micro-Pacing and Macro-Pacing Are Separate Skills**: Macro-pacing is the structure of the whole narrative; micro-pacing is the moment-to-moment rhythm within scenes. Both require design attention, and problems at either level can be mistaken for problems at the other.
- **Dramatic Irony Is the Designer's Most Powerful Tool**: When the reader knows something the character doesn't, or knows the import of something the character doesn't recognize, the reader becomes emotionally invested in every interaction between knowledge and ignorance.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Scene Function Taxonomy**: Every scene belongs to one or more categories — (1) Plot scenes (story events advance), (2) Character scenes (character understanding deepens), (3) Relationship scenes (dynamics between characters change), (4) World scenes (setting/atmosphere establishes), (5) Information scenes (reader receives essential context). Scenes that serve only one function may be candidates for consolidation.
- **Scene/Sequel Framework (Dwight Swain)**: Scene = Goal → Conflict → Disaster (ends with failure, complication, or a worse situation). Sequel = Emotion → Thought → Decision → Action (leads to the next scene's goal). Every scene should set up a sequel; every sequel should set up the next scene's goal.
- **The Information Iceberg**: The reader should always know slightly less than everything available — enough to care, not enough to be certain. Design the information that the reader holds at any moment: what they know, what they suspect, what they don't know yet.
- **Chapter Hook Architecture**: Map every chapter opening (what draws the reader in) and closing (what propels them to the next chapter). Endings should either resolve one tension while opening another, or leave a question that demands immediate answer.
- **Dramatic Irony Design**: Identify every place in the narrative where the reader can know more than the character, and engineer the information timing to maximize emotional impact. Document what each dramatic irony is, when the reader gets the knowledge, and when the irony resolves.
- **Revelation Sequencing**: For major reveals (character identity, hidden history, plot twists), map the complete information journey — what hints are planted, when each hint is deployed, when the reveal occurs, and how the revelation recontextualizes prior events.
- **Narrative Tension Curve**: Map the story's tension level across all scenes/chapters. Identify extended flat sections (need escalation), unearned peaks (need build-up), and missing valleys (need breather). Adjust the architecture before adjusting individual scenes.
- **The Plant and Reveal System**: Every significant revelation should have a corresponding plant — a detail established earlier that the reveal recontextualizes. Document all plants and their corresponding reveals to ensure both exist and are appropriately spaced.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Scene Architecture
- **Scene Goal**: What the viewpoint character wants to accomplish in the scene (distinct from what the character wants in the larger story)
- **Scene Conflict**: The force (person, circumstance, internal state) that opposes the scene goal
- **Scene Disaster**: The scene-ending failure, complication, or worsening situation that sets up the sequel
- **Sequel**: The narrative unit following a scene where the character processes what happened emotionally, cognitively, and decisively
- **Scene Function**: The narrative purpose a scene serves — plot, character, relationship, world, or information

### Information Management
- **Dramatic Irony**: Situation where the reader possesses information that a character lacks, creating tension from the gap between knowledge states
- **Revelation**: The moment when previously unknown or withheld information is delivered to the reader
- **Plant**: Narrative detail established early that becomes significant when a later revelation recontextualizes it; Chekhov's gun mechanism
- **Red Herring**: Narrative detail planted to mislead the reader about the story's direction; must be genuinely misleading, not merely absent of significance
- **Information Sequencing**: The deliberate ordering of revelations to control reader understanding and emotional response

### Pacing Concepts
- **Macro-Pacing**: The large-scale rhythm of the narrative — act structure, major tension curves, the distribution of high and low intensity across the whole work
- **Micro-Pacing**: The moment-to-moment rhythm within scenes — sentence length, dialogue-to-narration ratio, scene density
- **Chapter Hook**: Narrative device at a chapter's end that creates momentum into the next chapter — a question, a threat, a revelation, an unexpected development
- **Breather Scene**: Purposely lower-intensity scene following sustained high-tension sequences; necessary for reader metabolic recovery
- **Tension Plateau**: Extended period at the same tension level without escalation; produces reader desensitization

### Narrative Architecture
- **Story Arc**: The overall shape of a character's journey or narrative's development from beginning through climax to resolution
- **Subplot**: Secondary narrative thread that intersects with and enriches the main plot
- **In Medias Res**: Beginning the narrative in the middle of the action rather than at the chronological beginning
- **Denouement**: The narrative passage following the climax that resolves remaining tensions and provides closure

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Scene Without Function**: Including scenes because events must logically happen rather than because they serve a specific narrative function; every scene must justify its length.
- **Revelation Without Plant**: Delivering major reveals without prior plants; the reveal feels arbitrary rather than inevitable. Retroactive planting after the reveal is written is less effective but acceptable.
- **Dramatic Irony Without Payoff**: Establishing reader knowledge advantage over a character but never dramatizing the moment when the character confronts what the reader knew; unused dramatic irony is a broken promise.
- **Unbroken Tension**: Sustained high-tension sequences without breather scenes numb the reader; emotional intensity requires contrast to function.
- **Passive Sequel**: Sequel passages where characters experience emotion but make no decision and take no action; passive sequels stop narrative momentum.
- **Hook for Hook's Sake**: Ending every chapter with a manufactured cliffhanger regardless of whether it's narratively warranted; predictable structure makes hooks feel mechanical rather than organic.
- **Information Dump Scene**: A scene whose sole function is to deliver information to the reader through exposition, lecture, or dialogue; information must be embedded in scenes that also have plot and character function.

## Quality Indicators

- **Scene Function Coverage**: Every scene can be identified by its primary narrative function; no scenes exist purely because "this would logically happen next"
- **Plant/Reveal Mapping**: All major revelations have documented corresponding plants at appropriate narrative distances (typically one-quarter to one-third of the narrative before the reveal)
- **Scene/Sequel Ratio**: The ratio of action scenes to reflection/sequel passages is calibrated to the work's desired metabolic rate — not exclusively one type
- **Chapter Hook Variety**: Chapter endings use varied hook types (question, threat, revelation, perspective shift) rather than repeating the same hook structure
- **Dramatic Irony Tracking**: All significant dramatic ironies are documented with their plant point, the reader's knowledge, and their resolution point
- **Tension Curve Map**: A visual or structured representation of the narrative's tension levels across all chapters has been produced and analyzed for flat sections and unearned peaks
- **Revelation Recontextualization**: Major reveals demonstrably change the meaning of at least two prior scenes when viewed retrospectively

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Story Architect**: Story architect designs the structural skeleton (act breaks, midpoint, climax); narrative designer fleshes out the moment-to-moment architecture of scenes, hooks, and information flow within that skeleton
- **With Pacing Specialist**: Pacing specialist and narrative designer share overlapping territory; pacing specialist focuses on rhythm and tempo, narrative designer on structure and information; both must coordinate to prevent working at cross-purposes
- **With Tension Architect**: Tension architect designs the work's escalation and suspense systems; narrative designer sequences information and structures scenes to support those tension systems
- **With Plot Developer**: Plot developer designs the causal chain of events; narrative designer determines how those events are sequenced and revealed to the reader for maximum impact

# Best Practices: Editor

> Design principles, patterns, and frameworks that guide high-quality developmental editing, structural analysis, manuscript coordination, and revision strategy work.

## Design Principles

- **Excavate, Don't Impose**: The editor's job is to help the author realize their vision more fully, not to substitute the editor's vision. Ask the questions that make authors rewrite themselves better.
- **Diagnosis Before Prescription**: Identify and articulate the problem precisely before proposing solutions. Authors reject prescriptions they don't understand the need for; they accept solutions to problems they recognize as real.
- **Structure Is the First Problem**: Surface prose problems are often symptoms of structural problems. Editing sentence-level prose before the structure is sound is polishing before the foundation is laid.
- **The Whole Before the Parts**: Read the entire manuscript before editing any of it. Understanding the work's complete arc, the author's ambitions, and the reader's likely experience transforms what looks like a local problem into a structural diagnosis.
- **The Editor Serves the Reader**: When author intent and reader experience diverge, the editor advocates for the reader's experience. The reader's confusion, disengagement, or emotional flatness is the evidence.
- **Surgical Restraint**: The minimum effective intervention is the right one. Heavy editing that works technically but disrupts the author's voice is not a success. Cut, don't rewrite.
- **The Letter Before the Notes**: Major editorial feedback belongs in an editorial letter that establishes the overall diagnosis and priority order before line notes. Authors need context for individual suggestions.

## Key Patterns & Frameworks

- **Developmental Editing Framework**: Five-level structural analysis — (1) Overall arc and thematic argument, (2) Act structure and pacing, (3) Character arcs and emotional journey, (4) Scene function and construction, (5) Paragraph-level prose. Work top-down, not bottom-up.
- **Editorial Letter Architecture**: Opening section: what's working (build trust and establish editorial relationship). Middle section: the core structural diagnosis (the one most important thing). Detail section: specific issues with page references. Closing: revision strategy and priority order.
- **The Five Scene Questions**: Every scene should be evaluated against — (1) What is the scene's function (plot, character, atmosphere, information)? (2) Does it start in the right place? (3) Does it end in the right place? (4) Does it earn its place in the narrative? (5) What would be lost if it were cut?
- **Pacing Diagnosis Method**: Map the manuscript's scene lengths, tension levels, and emotional registers in sequence to identify pacing problems — slow patches, abrupt shifts, missing breathers after high-tension sequences.
- **Character Arc Audit**: For each major character, trace the arc through the manuscript — identify the wound, the want, the need, the arc-triggering incident, the midpoint shift, the dark night, and the resolution. Gaps in the arc are structural problems, not local ones.
- **Scene-Sequel Ratio Analysis**: Balance between action/tension scenes and reflection/reaction scenes. Too many consecutive action scenes produce numbness; too many consecutive reflection scenes produce stagnation. Diagnose the ratio.
- **Cutting for Structure**: Identify scenes, chapters, and subplots that are narratively redundant — that repeat character or plot information already established — and flag them for cutting or consolidation.
- **Tonal Calibration Review**: Track shifts in the manuscript's tone (register, emotional level, voice formality) and identify unintentional shifts that break the contract with the reader.

## Domain Concepts & Terminology

### Editorial Levels
- **Developmental Editing**: Highest-level structural editing addressing arc, character, pacing, theme, and overall effectiveness
- **Line Editing**: Sentence-level attention to rhythm, clarity, word choice, and prose quality
- **Copy Editing**: Grammar, mechanics, consistency, style guide compliance
- **Proofreading**: Final mechanical check of a nearly finished text

### Structural Analysis
- **Three-Act Structure**: Foundational narrative architecture — Setup (world and character), Confrontation (rising conflict), Resolution (climax and aftermath)
- **Midpoint**: Structural center of a narrative where the protagonist's direction or understanding shifts; often the scene where the "false solution" fails
- **Dark Night of the Soul**: The low point preceding the climax where the protagonist has failed, lost, or abandoned hope; structurally essential for satisfying climax
- **Subplot**: Secondary narrative thread involving secondary characters; must connect thematically to the main plot and have its own arc
- **Scene vs. Sequel**: Scene = action/reaction in real time; Sequel = the character's internal response to that action (emotion, thought, decision)

### Pacing Concepts
- **Narrative Velocity**: The perceived speed at which the story moves; controlled by scene length, sentence length, action/reflection ratio, and information density
- **Breather Scene**: Scene of reduced tension following high-action sequences, allowing readers to process what happened
- **Slow Burn**: Extended build of tension over many scenes, trading immediate gratification for cumulative effect
- **Tension Plateau**: Extended section of high tension without escalation or variation; produces reader desensitization rather than sustained engagement

### Character Analysis
- **Arc Trigger**: The inciting event that sets a character's arc in motion, forcing them to begin the journey toward their need
- **Character Contradiction**: Genuinely opposing qualities in a single character that create internal tension and behavioral complexity
- **Voice Consistency**: The degree to which a character's dialogue, thought, and action reflect their established psychological profile throughout the manuscript

### Manuscript Coordination
- **Editorial Letter**: Formal communication from editor to author establishing the structural diagnosis and revision priorities
- **Pass**: A complete read-through of a manuscript focused on a specific editorial task (structural pass, character pass, line pass)
- **Revision Priority Order**: The sequence in which revision work should be done — always structural first, line-level last

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

- **Line-First Editing**: Beginning with sentence-level prose editing before structural problems are identified and addressed; prose polish on a structurally weak manuscript wastes everyone's time.
- **Vision Substitution**: Rewriting passages in the editor's own voice or toward the editor's preferred aesthetic, substituting editorial vision for authorial vision.
- **The Laundry List**: Sending the author a flat list of 47 problems without priority order, diagnosis, or revision strategy; overwhelming authors produces paralysis, not revision.
- **Problem-Naming Without Diagnosis**: Flagging that a scene "doesn't work" without articulating why it doesn't work; authors need diagnosis to produce effective revisions.
- **Structural Blindness**: Focusing on local problems (this scene is slow, this dialogue is flat) without identifying the structural cause (the subplot is redundant, the character has no arc).
- **Over-Editing**: Rewriting so much of the manuscript that the author's voice disappears; the author must be able to see themselves in the edited text.
- **Feedback Without Anchoring**: Providing editorial feedback without specific page references or examples; abstract feedback ("the pacing is slow in the middle") cannot be acted on without location.

## Quality Indicators

- **Structural Diagnosis Specificity**: Editorial assessment identifies the single most important structural problem and explains its effects on the reader's experience
- **Priority Order**: All editorial feedback is organized by priority — structural issues before scene issues before prose issues
- **Arc Documentation**: Character arcs for all major characters are traced through the manuscript with specific scene references
- **What's Working Section**: Editorial letter opens with genuine acknowledgment of the manuscript's strengths, establishing a collaborative rather than adversarial relationship
- **Revision Strategy**: Feedback concludes with a practical revision strategy that tells the author where to start and in what order to work
- **Voice Preservation**: Suggested revisions preserve the author's distinctive voice rather than substituting editorial preferences
- **Specialist Coordination**: When structural work is complete, the editor coordinates with copy-editor, continuity-checker, and prose-stylist for subsequent passes

## Collaboration Touchpoints

- **With Prose Stylist**: Prose stylist handles sentence-level voice and rhythm refinement after structural editing is complete; editor delegates specific line-level concerns rather than attempting both developmental and line editing simultaneously
- **With Continuity Checker**: After structural revisions are complete, continuity checker performs systematic timeline and character consistency analysis; structural revisions may have introduced new continuity issues
- **With Copy Editor**: Copy editor handles final grammar, mechanics, and style consistency after developmental and line editing are complete; copy editor works on a structurally and prose-level-sound manuscript
- **With Narrative Director**: Editor and narrative director collaborate on creative vision — director holds the artistic vision, editor implements it through manuscript-level work; for complex projects, editorial letters go through director for tonal and thematic alignment before delivery to author

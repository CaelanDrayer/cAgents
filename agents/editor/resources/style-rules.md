# Style Rules and Fiction Copy-Editing Reference

## Chicago Manual of Style: Fiction Essentials

### Punctuation in Narrative

**Commas**:
- Serial (Oxford) comma: required. "Red, white, and blue." (Not "red, white and blue.")
- Comma after introductory elements: "After the explosion, she ran." (But short intros can omit: "Then she ran.")
- Comma before coordinating conjunction joining independent clauses: "She ran, and he followed."
- No comma before "because" when it introduces a necessary clause: "She ran because the building was on fire."

**Em Dashes (--)**:
- Closed (no spaces): "She knew--or thought she knew--what he meant."
- Use for interruption, parenthetical asides, and amplification
- Do not use more than two pairs per paragraph (overuse dilutes impact)
- In dialogue, for interrupted speech: "I didn't mean--"

**En Dashes (-)**:
- Number ranges: pages 15-30, 1914-1918
- Compound adjectives where one element is already compound: "post-Cold War era"
- Not used in dialogue or narrative prose (use em dash instead)

**Ellipses (...)**:
- Trailing off in thought or speech: "I wonder if..."
- Omitted text in quotation: "To be...or not to be"
- Use three spaced dots (. . .) per CMS, though many publishers now use three unspaced dots (...)
- Do NOT use for interrupted speech (use em dash)

**Semicolons**:
- Joining related independent clauses: "She ran; he followed."
- In fiction, semicolons are stylistic choices -- many modern writers avoid them for a more immediate feel
- Never query an author's semicolon usage unless it's grammatically incorrect

**Colons**:
- Introduce a list, explanation, or elaboration after an independent clause
- Lowercase after colon in running text (CMS); capitalize only if what follows is a complete, independent rule or principle

### Dialogue Punctuation

**Attribution Tags**:
```
"I don't believe it," she said.          # Comma, lowercase tag
"I don't believe it." She shook her head. # Period, action beat (not a tag)
"Do you believe it?" she asked.          # Question mark, lowercase tag
"I can't believe it!" she cried.         # Exclamation, lowercase tag
"I don't"--she paused--"believe it."     # Interrupted by action
```

**Common Dialogue Errors**:
```
WRONG: "I don't believe it." She said.    # "She said" is a tag, needs comma
WRONG: "I don't believe it," She said.    # Lowercase "she" after comma
WRONG: "I don't believe it", she said.    # Comma inside quotes (US convention)
RIGHT: "I don't believe it," she said.    # Correct form
```

**Nested Dialogue**: Use single quotes inside double quotes. "She told me, 'Don't go,' but I went anyway."

**Multi-Paragraph Dialogue**: Opening quotes at start of each paragraph, closing quotes only at the final paragraph.

### Numbers in Narrative

| Context | Rule | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| General narrative | Spell out 1-100 | "eighty-three soldiers" |
| Very large numbers | Spell out in combination | "two hundred thousand" or "200,000" |
| Dialogue | Spell out almost always | "I'm thirty-four years old" |
| Time | Spell out informal, numerals formal | "half past three" / "3:30 p.m." |
| Dates | Month Day, Year | "March 15, 2024" |
| Percentages in narrative | Spell out | "twelve percent" |
| Street addresses | Numerals for house numbers | "221B Baker Street" |
| Money | Spell out to one hundred | "fifty dollars" / "$250" |

### Capitalization in Fiction

**Title Case vs. Sentence Case**: Book titles and chapter titles in title case within the manuscript. Proper nouns capitalized. Generic references lowercase.

**The/the with Titles**: "the Council" when referring to a specific body; "a council" when generic. Track these choices on the style sheet and enforce consistently.

**Direction Words**: "She headed north" (direction, lowercase). "The North was cold" (region, uppercase). "Northern customs" (derived adjective for a specific region, uppercase).

**Fictional Titles and Institutions**: Follow the manuscript's established convention. If the author capitalizes "the Guild," maintain that throughout. If they don't, don't capitalize it for them.

## Fiction-Specific Style Decisions

### Internal Thought

**Direct Thought (Italics)**: *I can't do this*, she thought.
- Use consistently if the manuscript establishes this pattern
- Don't add "she thought" after every italicized thought -- context usually makes the thinker clear

**Indirect Thought (No Formatting)**: She couldn't do this.
- More seamless in deep POV
- No special formatting needed

**Free Indirect Discourse**: She couldn't -- she honestly couldn't do this. Not after everything.
- Blends narrator and character voice
- No formatting needed; voice shift signals the mode change
- Do NOT "correct" free indirect discourse into standard narration

### Tense Management

**Past Tense Narrative** (standard):
- Simple past for main action: "She walked to the door."
- Past perfect for flashback entry: "She had walked to the door that night, unaware..."
- Past perfect can drop after establishing the time shift: "She walked to the door and opened it. The hallway was dark." (Simple past is fine after the shift is established.)
- Return to present timeline signaled by past perfect or explicit time marker

**Present Tense Narrative**:
- Present tense for main action: "She walks to the door."
- Simple past for backstory within present-tense narrative: "She walks to the door, remembering how she walked this hallway as a child."
- Track carefully -- present-tense narratives are prone to accidental past-tense slips

### POV Consistency

**First Person**: Narrative vocabulary must match the character. A twelve-year-old narrator should not use "juxtaposition." Flag vocabulary that exceeds the character's plausible range.

**Third Person Limited**: The narrative should only report what the POV character can perceive, know, and think. Flag moments where the narration reveals information the POV character couldn't know.

**Third Person Omniscient**: The narrator can access any character's thoughts, but transitions between perspectives should be smooth and clear. Flag abrupt, confusing head-hops.

**Multiple POV**: Each POV character should have distinguishable voice. Track POV shifts and flag chapters or sections where the POV assignment is unclear.

## Voice Preservation Techniques

### Identifying Authorial Voice Markers

Voice consists of:
1. **Sentence length patterns**: Long and flowing, or short and punchy?
2. **Vocabulary level**: Academic, conversational, poetic, spare?
3. **Figurative language**: Heavy metaphor? Minimal? Idiosyncratic similes?
4. **Rhythm**: Iambic tendency? Fragments? Run-ons?
5. **Paragraph length**: Dense blocks or airy white space?

Map these in the style sheet. If an edit changes any of these markers, reconsider.

### The Stet Principle

When an author stets a correction, accept it gracefully. The author has the final word on their own prose. If the same issue recurs, you may query it once more with additional context, but never argue.

### Correcting Without Flattening

**Instead of restructuring**: Query. "AU: This sentence might read more clearly as [suggestion]. Your call."

**Instead of replacing voice-specific words**: Query. "AU: 'discombobulated' -- intentional word choice here? Fits the character's voice but unusual in this context."

**Instead of standardizing sentence structure**: Leave it. If the author varies between "She ran to the door" and "To the door she ran," they're doing it for rhythm. Only flag if it seems unintentional.

## Fact-Checking Reference

### What to Verify

| Category | Check Against | Example |
|----------|--------------|---------|
| Geography | Maps, atlases | "She walked north on Fifth Avenue toward Central Park" -- verify direction |
| Historical dates | Multiple sources | "The war ended in 1945" -- verify specific date if stated |
| Science | Authoritative sources | "The bullet traveled faster than sound" -- verify Mach numbers if specific |
| Brand names | Company websites | "She drove her Chevy Camero" -- correct spelling is "Camaro" |
| Foreign phrases | Native speaker or dictionary | "C'est la vie" not "C'est la vi" |
| Legal/medical terms | Professional references | Correct procedure names, drug names, legal terminology |

### What NOT to Fact-Check

- Fictional world details (that's consistency checking, not fact-checking)
- Emotional or psychological claims ("She felt her heart break" -- metaphorical, not medical)
- Clearly established artistic license ("The moon was blue that night")
- Speculative or fantastical elements in speculative fiction

## Common Copy-Editing Traps in Fiction

### The False Error

| Pattern | Not an Error When... |
|---------|---------------------|
| Sentence fragment | Used deliberately for rhythm or emphasis |
| Comma splice | Part of the narrative voice (especially first person) |
| "Wrong" word | Character's vocabulary (malapropism, dialect) |
| Passive voice | Used for intentional emphasis or character voice |
| Starting with "And" or "But" | Perfectly standard in modern prose |
| Split infinitive | Not actually a rule; often reads more naturally split |
| Ending with preposition | Not actually a rule; "correct" alternatives often sound stilted |

### The Consistency Trap

- Don't enforce consistency that the author intentionally varies (e.g., a character who becomes more articulate as they gain confidence)
- Don't enforce consistency across different POV characters (each should have their own voice)
- DO enforce consistency within a single character's voice and within world-building facts

### The Over-Editing Trap

**Signs you're over-editing**:
- You're rewriting more than 5% of sentences
- You're adding words more often than removing them
- Your changes make the prose "better" but less distinctive
- You find yourself imposing a rhythm that wasn't there
- The edited manuscript sounds more like you than the author

**The correction**: Put the red pen down. Reread the original. Ask yourself: is this my taste or the manuscript's need?

# Story Structure Templates & Frameworks

## Three-Act Structure: Detailed Blueprint

### Act One — Setup (20-25% of narrative)

**Opening Image**: A snapshot of the protagonist's world before change. This image should mirror the Final Image to show transformation. If the story is about a loner learning connection, open with isolation. If it's about innocence lost, open with the garden.

**The Hook (First 5%)**: Establish the protagonist's defining characteristic through action, not exposition. Show them being competent, flawed, and interesting within the same scene. The reader decides here whether to continue.

**The Status Quo**: Show the world working — imperfectly, with cracks, but functioning. The protagonist has adapted to their flaw. It's manageable. This is important because the inciting incident must disrupt something that was stable.

**Theme Stated (10-15%)**: Someone — usually not the protagonist — states the story's theme in dialogue. It passes without notice because the protagonist isn't ready to hear it. In retrospect, this is the thesis the story will prove.

**The Inciting Incident (10-12%)**: The event that makes the status quo impossible to maintain. Must be:
- **Specific**: Not "things got worse" but "a letter arrives"
- **Irreversible**: Cannot be undone by reasonable action
- **Personal**: Affects the protagonist directly, not abstractly
- **Active**: Demands response (even if the response is refusal)

**The Debate (12-20%)**: The protagonist considers whether to engage. This is NOT cowardice — it's realistic. Show what they'd lose by acting. Make staying comfortable and leaving terrifying. The stronger the case for inaction, the more meaningful the choice to act.

**The Threshold Crossing (20-25%)**: The protagonist commits to the journey. This must be a choice. Even if forced by circumstances, the character must choose to engage rather than flee. The choice reveals character and establishes the active dramatic question.

### Act Two — Confrontation (50% of narrative)

**Act Two, Part One — Rising Action (25-50%)**:

**Fun and Games (25-35%)**: Deliver the promise of the premise. This is why the reader picked up the book. If the concept is "a detective who sees ghosts," show ghost-detective work. If it's "a heist crew assembling," show the recruitment and planning. This section should be the most energetic and engaging portion.

**The B-Story (25-30%)**: Introduce the subplot — usually a relationship that will carry the theme. If the A-story is about external conflict, the B-story is about internal change. The B-story character often serves as the protagonist's thematic mirror.

**Rising Complications (30-40%)**: Progressive obstacles that don't just block the protagonist but change the nature of the problem. Each complication should reveal new information, force character growth, and eliminate options.

**Midpoint (50%)**: The fulcrum. Choose one:
- **False Victory**: The goal seems achieved, but the victory is hollow or triggers worse consequences
- **False Defeat**: All seems lost, but the defeat contains hidden opportunity
- **Revelation**: New information fundamentally changes understanding
- **Commitment**: Point of no return — bridges burned, ships sunk

The midpoint shifts the protagonist from reactive (responding to events) to proactive (driving events).

**Act Two, Part Two — Tightening Noose (50-75%)**:

**Rising Stakes (50-60%)**: The antagonist responds to the protagonist's midpoint action. Counter-moves. Escalation. The conflict becomes personal.

**Allies Tested (55-65%)**: Relationships strained by pressure. Trust questioned. Loyalties tested. The protagonist discovers who's truly committed and who breaks.

**The Protagonist's Flaw Reasserts (60-70%)**: Under maximum pressure, the protagonist reverts to their old pattern. The flaw that was manageable in Act One becomes catastrophic under Act Two's pressure.

**All Is Lost (75%)**: The lowest point. The protagonist has failed, their flaw has cost them dearly, and the easy path (giving up, compromising values) beckons. Often marked by a "whiff of death" — literal or metaphorical mortality.

### Act Three — Resolution (25% of narrative)

**Dark Night of the Soul (75-80%)**: The aftermath of All Is Lost. The protagonist sits with their failure. This is the moment of genuine choice — not what to do, but who to be. The theme, stated back in Act One, now becomes the question the protagonist must answer.

**Break into Three (80-85%)**: Synthesis. The protagonist combines what they learned in Act One (who they were) with what they discovered in Act Two (who they could be) and chooses Act Three (who they will be). Often triggered by A-story and B-story colliding.

**The Climax (85-95%)**: The final confrontation, driven by the character's transformation. The external resolution mirrors the internal resolution. The protagonist uses the lesson they learned (not the skills they started with) to overcome the obstacle. The theme is proved through action.

**Final Image (95-100%)**: Mirror of the Opening Image. Same character, same type of moment, fundamentally different person. The visual rhyme between first and last images encapsulates the entire transformation.

## Hero's Journey: Structural Map

### The Departure

| Stage | Purpose | Structural Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary World** | Establish normal before disruption | Opening Image + Status Quo |
| **Call to Adventure** | Disrupting event | Inciting Incident |
| **Refusal of the Call** | Resistance to change | The Debate |
| **Meeting the Mentor** | Guide appears | B-Story introduction |
| **Crossing the Threshold** | Commitment to journey | Act One to Act Two transition |

### The Initiation

| Stage | Purpose | Structural Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Tests, Allies, Enemies** | Learning new world's rules | Fun and Games |
| **Approach to Innermost Cave** | Preparing for ordeal | Rising complications |
| **The Ordeal** | Death/rebirth experience | Midpoint |
| **The Reward** | Seizing the prize | Post-midpoint shift |

### The Return

| Stage | Purpose | Structural Role |
|---|---|---|
| **The Road Back** | Consequences of taking reward | Tightening noose |
| **The Resurrection** | Final test, death/rebirth | Climax |
| **Return with the Elixir** | Transformed hero in original world | Resolution + Final Image |

### When the Hero's Journey Doesn't Fit
- Anti-hero narratives (the "mentor" is corrupt, the "elixir" is poisoned)
- Ensemble stories (multiple simultaneous journeys that can't all follow the template)
- Literary fiction (internal journeys that don't map to external adventure stages)
- Tragedy (the hero doesn't return — or returns destroyed)

When it doesn't fit, don't force it. Use the emotional logic (departure/initiation/return) without the specific stage requirements.

## Seven-Point Story Structure: Working Template

Designed to be plotted backward — from Resolution to Hook:

1. **Resolution** (design first): Where does the story end? What is the final state?
2. **Hook** (design second): The opposite of the Resolution. If the resolution is triumph, the hook shows defeat. If connection, isolation.
3. **Midpoint** (design third): The moment that moves the character from the Hook state toward the Resolution state. The pivot.
4. **Plot Turn 1** (between Hook and Midpoint): Introduces the conflict or the world that will drive the change. The new element that makes the midpoint possible.
5. **Plot Turn 2** (between Midpoint and Resolution): Provides the final piece — the knowledge, tool, or realization — that makes the Resolution possible.
6. **Pinch Point 1** (between Plot Turn 1 and Midpoint): Applies pressure. The antagonistic force demonstrates its power. The protagonist realizes the scale of what they face.
7. **Pinch Point 2** (between Midpoint and Plot Turn 2): Maximum pressure. Everything seems hopeless. The antagonistic force is at its strongest.

**Why plot backward**: Because the ending determines what the beginning must establish. If you don't know where you're going, you can't know where to start.

## Genre Structure Templates

### Mystery Structure

```
Crime Scene     -> Question established: WHO and WHY?
Investigation   -> Clue chains: Real clue, Red herring, Real clue, Red herring
                   Each cycle: new suspect, eliminated, deeper truth revealed
Complication    -> Personal stakes: detective becomes involved
                   Information reversal: something "known" proves false
False Solution  -> Apparent answer that satisfies surface logic
                   but misses deeper truth (or implicates wrong person)
True Revelation -> All clues recontextualized. Reader can verify:
                   every clue was available, every red herring distinguishable
Resolution      -> Justice (or its deliberate absence) + thematic statement
                   about truth, justice, human nature
```

**Mystery-Specific Structural Rule**: The reader must be able to solve the mystery before the detective. All necessary clues must appear in the text. This is the fair-play contract.

### Romance Structure

```
Meet-Cute/Conflict -> Attraction + Obstacle introduced simultaneously
                      The obstacle must be genuine (not a misunderstanding
                      that one conversation could resolve)
Rising Intimacy    -> Vulnerability exchanged; walls lowered
                      Each intimate moment raises emotional stakes
Complications      -> External obstacles + internal fears interleave
                      The character flaw each protagonist brings
Midpoint Bond      -> Genuine connection established. Now the reader
                      fears its loss (which creates the second-half tension)
Dark Moment        -> Seeming permanent separation
                      Caused by character flaws, not contrivance
Grand Gesture      -> Character growth demonstrated through action
                      The flaw that caused separation is overcome
HEA/HFN            -> Earned reunion. Both characters changed.
```

**Romance-Specific Structural Rule**: Both protagonists must have complete character arcs. The relationship is the plot, but growth is the structure.

### Thriller Structure

```
Inciting Threat    -> Immediate, specific, personal danger
                      The clock starts ticking from page one
Escalation Cycles  -> Each cycle: new threat → protagonist response →
                      worse consequence. 3-5 cycles minimum.
                      Each cycle narrows options and raises stakes
Twist/Reversal     -> Midpoint: the threat is not what it seemed
                      (bigger, more personal, more complex)
All-Is-Lost        -> Protagonist stripped of resources, allies, safety
                      The clock is almost out
Climactic Sequence -> Multiple scene climax (not a single moment)
                      The protagonist uses everything they've learned
Resolution         -> Threat neutralized, but at cost
                      The protagonist is changed by what they survived
```

**Thriller-Specific Structural Rule**: Pacing IS structure. Any scene that doesn't escalate tension, reveal information, or narrow options is structural dead weight.

### Horror Structure

```
Normalcy           -> Establish what is precious and can be lost
                      The more domestic and safe, the more effective
Intrusion (Subtle) -> Wrong details. Unease. Things slightly off.
                      The protagonist notices but rationalizes
Intrusion (Overt)  -> The threat is undeniable. The normal world
                      has been permanently breached
Escalation         -> Resources stripped: knowledge, allies, safety,
                      understanding. The threat operates by rules
                      the protagonist doesn't understand
Revelation         -> The true nature of the threat revealed
                      (often worse than imagined)
Confrontation      -> Not necessarily victory. Horror allows
                      (sometimes demands) the protagonist's destruction
Cost               -> Even survival comes with permanent damage.
                      The normal world cannot be fully restored
```

**Horror-Specific Structural Rule**: Information asymmetry is structural. What the reader knows vs. what the character knows creates dread. What neither reader nor character knows creates cosmic horror.

## Non-Linear Structure Toolkit

### Flashback Integration Checklist

Before including a flashback, verify:
- [ ] Does it reveal information that transforms the current scene?
- [ ] Is this the moment of maximum impact for this information?
- [ ] Would the scene work without the flashback? (If yes, cut it)
- [ ] Does the flashback end by returning to the present with new tension?
- [ ] Is the transition into and out of the flashback smooth and oriented?

### Parallel Timeline Design

When running two or more timelines:
- Each timeline must be independently compelling
- Cut between timelines at moments of maximum tension (in one or both)
- Thematic connections between parallel scenes should be visible but not heavy-handed
- The moment of convergence (where timelines intersect) is the structural climax
- Label or orient clearly — the reader must always know when they are

### Frame Narrative Architecture

| Element | Outer Frame | Inner Story |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary function** | Context, commentary | Emotional payload |
| **Narrator** | Frame narrator (may be unreliable) | Main characters |
| **Tension source** | Why is this story being told? | The story itself |
| **Structural climax** | Revelation about the frame | Climax of inner story |
| **Resolution** | How telling the story changes the teller | How the story ends |

The frame and inner story should comment on each other. The frame narrator's reason for telling the story is itself a story.

## Ensemble POV Management

### POV Tracking Template

| Character | POV Chapters | Page Count | Arc | Last Appearance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Character A | 1, 4, 7, 12, 18 | 85 pages | Guilt → Redemption | Ch. 18 |
| Character B | 2, 5, 9, 14, 19 | 78 pages | Trust → Betrayal → Justice | Ch. 19 |
| Character C | 3, 6, 10, 15, 20 | 72 pages | Isolation → Connection | Ch. 20 |
| Character D | 8, 11, 16 | 45 pages | Mystery → Revelation | Ch. 16 |

### POV Selection Decision Tree

For each scene, ask:
1. Who has the most at stake in this scene? → Strong candidate
2. Who knows the least about what's happening? → Creates dramatic irony
3. Who has the most conflicted emotional response? → Creates complexity
4. Whose perspective hasn't been seen recently? → Maintains balance
5. Whose perspective would create the most tension? → Usually the best choice

| Element | Book 1 | Book 2 | Book 3 | Series Arc |

| **Stakes** | Personal | Relational | Societal + Moral | Everything at stake |
| **Ending** | Satisfying + hook | Satisfying + bigger hook | Complete resolution | All threads resolved |

## Structural Diagnostic Questions

When evaluating existing structure:

1. **Can you state the core dramatic question in one sentence?** If not, the foundation is unclear.
2. **What are the five most important scenes?** If you can't identify them, the load-bearing elements are buried.
3. **Does the midpoint change the story's direction?** If the story reads the same before and after the midpoint, there's no structural pivot.
4. **Does the climax require the protagonist to change?** If they could have solved the problem in Act One with the same resources, the structure hasn't earned the journey.
5. **Does every subplot connect to the main plot?** If a subplot could be removed with no effect on the main story, it's not integrated.
6. **Is the antagonist's plan independent of the protagonist's actions?** An antagonist who only acts in response to the protagonist lacks structural agency.
7. **Does the genre's obligatory scene occur?** Every genre promises specific structural elements. Missing them feels like structural failure even if the reader can't articulate why.

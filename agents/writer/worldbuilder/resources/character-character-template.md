# Character Profile Template

## The Deep Character Profile

This template goes beyond surface traits to build characters with genuine psychological depth, internal contradiction, and the potential for meaningful change.

## Section 1: Identity

### Essential Information
- **Name** (and what it reveals): Names are not neutral. Consider origin, cultural context, what the character's parents were signaling when they chose it, whether the character goes by a nickname (and why)
- **Age**: Not just a number but a position in life -- what they have experienced, what they expect from the future, what stage of development they occupy
- **Physical presence**: Not a police-report description ("5'10", brown hair") but how they inhabit their body. Do they take up space or minimize themselves? Is their body a tool, a burden, a source of pleasure, something they forget about?
- **Occupation**: What they do and what it reveals about their choices, compromises, and values

### The Two Selves
- **Public self**: How this character presents to the world. What mask do they wear? What impression do they work to create? What qualities do they perform?
- **Private self**: Who they are when no one is watching. What do they do, think, feel, and desire when the performance stops? The gap between public and private self IS the character's central tension

## Section 2: The Wound-Want-Need Architecture

### The Wound
- **Formative experience**: Not just "what happened" but when it happened (age matters), who was involved, and what was the specific false conclusion the character drew from it
- **The false belief**: State it as a sentence the character would not say aloud but lives by. "If I show weakness, people will destroy me." "Love is a transaction, and I must always give more than I receive." "I am fundamentally broken and must hide my true self"
- **The strategy**: How has the character organized their life to protect themselves from re-experiencing the wound? This strategy IS their personality

### The Want (Conscious Goal)
- **Specific and active**: What does the character believe will solve their fundamental problem?
- **Why this want**: How does the want logically connect to the false belief? (It always does)
- **Cost of pursuit**: What is the character willing to sacrifice to get what they want?
- **Contradiction with need**: How does pursuing the want move the character further from what they actually require?

### The Need (Unconscious Requirement)
- **The true lesson**: What must the character learn, accept, or become to genuinely heal?
- **Why they resist it**: The need always requires confronting what the character most fears
- **The sacrifice**: What must the character give up to achieve the need? (Usually their strategy, their comfortable lies, or their self-image)

## Section 3: Psychology

### Core Contradictions
Every compelling character contains contradictions. List 3 pairs of opposing qualities that coexist in this person:

| Trait | Counter-Trait | When Each Appears |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|
| Example: Generous | Jealous | Generous with strangers, jealous with intimates |
| Example: Brave | Avoidant | Brave about physical danger, avoidant about emotional confrontation |
| | | |
| | | |
| | | |

### Defense Mechanisms
How does this character protect themselves psychologically?
- **Primary defense**: The habitual response to threat (humor, withdrawal, aggression, intellectualization, denial, projection)
- **Secondary defense**: What they fall back on when the primary defense fails
- **Breaking point**: What overwhelms all defenses? What makes this character's walls come down?

### Emotional Landscape
- **Dominant emotional register**: What emotion does this character most often experience? (This is not their only emotion -- it is their emotional default)
- **Forbidden emotion**: What emotion does this character suppress, deny, or fear feeling?
- **Emotional triggers**: What specific situations, words, or sensory details activate strong emotional responses (and why)?
- **Comfort patterns**: What does this character do to self-soothe? (Healthy and unhealthy)

### Relationship Patterns
- **Attachment style**: Secure, anxious, avoidant, or disorganized? (Based on Bowlby's attachment theory)
- **Relational wound pattern**: How does their wound manifest in relationships? Do they push people away? Cling? Control? Test loyalty?
- **What they need from others**: Not what they ask for (which is often a performance) but what they actually require
- **What they give others**: What role do they play in their relationships? Caretaker? Entertainer? Problem-solver? Rebel?

## Section 4: Voice and Behavior

### Speech Patterns
- **Vocabulary range**: Educated/colloquial, technical/general, formal/informal
- **Sentence structure**: Short and punchy or long and flowing? Complete sentences or fragments?
- **Verbal habits**: Repeated words, phrases, fillers, oaths
- **Metaphor sources**: Where does this character draw their comparisons from? (Their profession, their childhood, their obsessions)
- **What they do NOT say**: Topics they avoid, emotions they never name, words they never use

### Physical Behavior
- **Habitual gestures**: How do they use their hands? What do they do when nervous, angry, or lying?
- **Spatial behavior**: Do they stand close or far? Touch others or avoid touch? Take up space or minimize themselves?
- **Relationship with objects**: How do they handle their possessions? What do they carry? What do they fidget with?
- **Under-stress behavior**: How does their physical behavior change under pressure? (This should be different from their baseline)

### Sensory World
- **What they notice**: Each character has a sensory bias. A chef notices smells. A musician notices sounds. An anxious person notices exits. What does this character's attention gravitate toward?
- **What they ignore**: What do they fail to notice? This reveals what they take for granted or actively suppress
- **Comfort sensations**: What sensory experiences bring them peace? A specific smell, a type of music, a texture?

## Section 5: The Arc

### Starting State
Where is this character when the story begins? Not just their external situation but their internal state:
- What do they believe about themselves?
- What do they believe about the world?
- What strategy are they employing?
- How well is it working?

### The Arc Design
| Act | Internal State | External Action | Key Decision |
|-----|---------------|-----------------|--------------|
| Act 1 | Strategy is working (precariously) | Pursues the want | Commits to the plan |
| Early Act 2 | Strategy tested, holds | Encounters obstacles | Doubles down on strategy |
| Midpoint | Strategy fails unexpectedly | Crisis forces reassessment | Glimpses the need, rejects it |
| Late Act 2 | All strategies exhausted | Dark night of the soul | Faces the wound |
| Act 3 | Chooses want or need | Climactic action | Transforms or refuses to |

### Ending State
- **If they achieve the need**: How have they changed? What did they give up? What did they gain? How is their behavior different?
- **If they refuse the need**: What has their refusal cost them? Is there tragic dignity in the refusal? What have they preserved?
- **Either way**: The ending state should feel both surprising and inevitable -- the reader should not have predicted exactly this, but once it arrives, it should feel like the only possible outcome

## Section 6: Ensemble Position

### Function in the Story
- **Archetypal role**: Protagonist, antagonist, mentor, trickster, threshold guardian, shadow, shapeshifter, herald, ally
- **Thematic function**: What answer to the story's thematic question does this character embody?
- **Narrative function**: What plot events does this character enable, cause, or prevent?

### Key Relationships
For each significant relationship:
- **With whom**: Name the other character
- **Dynamic**: What is the primary tension or connection?
- **What each gives**: What does each character provide the other?
- **What each withholds**: What does each character refuse to give?
- **Potential for change**: How might this relationship evolve?

### Foil Relationships
- **Who is this character's foil?**
- **What quality is illuminated by contrast?**
- **What do they share that makes the contrast meaningful?** (A foil must have something in common with the character for the contrast to work -- otherwise they are just different people)

## Character Creation Checklist

Before declaring a character complete:

- [ ] The wound is specific, not generic ("abandoned by mother at age 7" not just "abandonment issues")
- [ ] The false belief is stated as a sentence the character lives by but would not say aloud
- [ ] The want is specific, active, and wrong (addresses symptom, not cause)
- [ ] The need contradicts the want and requires sacrifice
- [ ] At least 3 productive contradictions exist within the character
- [ ] The character has a clear defense mechanism that shapes their behavior
- [ ] Voice is distinctive enough to identify without attribution
- [ ] Backstory is revealed through behavior, not exposition
- [ ] The character makes at least one choice that surprises even the creator
- [ ] The character could not be replaced by any other character without changing the story
- [ ] The arc is driven by character choices, not external events
- [ ] The ending state is both surprising and inevitable

## Section 7: Naming and Identity

### The Weight of a Name
- **Etymology**: What does the name mean? Does the meaning resonate with the character's role? (This can be used subtly or ironically -- never obviously)
- **Cultural origin**: What does the name signal about the character's heritage, family values, or social aspirations?
- **Nickname dynamics**: Who uses which name? Parents may use the full name; friends may shorten it; lovers may have a private variation. The name someone calls a character reveals the relationship
- **Self-naming**: Does the character introduce themselves by full name, first name, last name, or a chosen name? This reveals how they want to be perceived
- **Name changes**: Characters who change their names signal transformation -- Jay Gatsby was James Gatz; the name change IS the character's fundamental act of self-invention

### Names to Avoid
- Names that sound too similar (Mark and Mike, Sarah and Sandra) -- readers confuse them
- Names that telegraph character (Charity for a generous person, Wolf for a predator) -- unless the irony is deliberate
- Names that are unpronounceable -- the reader must be able to voice the name in their head
- Names from different cultural traditions without reason -- a family should name consistently

## Section 8: Minor Character Design

Not every character needs the full deep profile. Minor characters need a compressed version:

### The One-Trait-Plus Method
Give every named character ONE defining trait AND one surprising detail that complicates it:
- The stern librarian who grows orchids
- The cheerful barista with scarred knuckles
- The gentle grandfather who never enters the basement

The trait establishes quick recognition; the detail implies depth without demanding screen time.

### The Want-in-the-Scene Method
Every minor character in a scene should want something, even if it is as simple as "wants to go home" or "wants to impress." Characters with wants generate the small frictions that make scenes feel alive.

### Background Character Consistency
Even unnamed characters should behave consistently if they recur. The waitress always chewing gum. The neighbor whose dog always barks. These tiny consistencies build a living world.

## Section 9: Character Voice Sheet

A quick-reference document for each major character's speech patterns:

| Element | This Character |
|---------|---------------|
| Vocabulary level | (everyday / educated / technical / street) |
| Sentence length | (terse / moderate / verbose) |
| Favorite words/phrases | (list 3-5) |
| Words they never use | (list 2-3) |
| Metaphor source | (nature / military / food / sports / etc.) |
| Filler words | ("like" / "you know" / "basically" / none) |
| Questions or statements | (asks or tells) |
| Contractions | (always / never / situation-dependent) |
| Profanity level | (none / mild / frequent / creative) |
| Humor style | (dry / slapstick / sarcastic / none) |
| Under stress | (how speech changes) |
| Lie tells | (what changes when they lie) |

**Using the Voice Sheet**: Before writing dialogue for a character, scan their voice sheet. After writing, read the dialogue aloud and check against the sheet. If a line could have been spoken by a different character, revise it.

## Section 10: Character Relationship Map

### Mapping the Ensemble
For each pair of characters, define:

| | Character A → B | Character B → A |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface relationship** | (boss, friend, lover) | (employee, friend, lover) |
| **Emotional reality** | (resentful admiration) | (guilty superiority) |
| **What A wants from B** | (respect) | (loyalty) |
| **What A fears from B** | (judgment) | (independence) |
| **Power balance** | (A has less) | (B has more) |
| **Key tension** | (A's ambition threatens B's authority) | |

### Relationship Triangles
When three characters are connected, map the dynamic:
- Which pair has the strongest bond?
- Which character is the outsider?
- What would shift if one character were removed?
- Where are the alliances? Where are the rivalries?
- How do the dynamics change when one character is absent?

## Section 11: Physical Characterization Beyond Description

### The Body as Psychology
Physical description should reveal psychology, not catalog appearance:
- **Posture**: Do they stand tall or hunch? Take up space or minimize? Posture communicates self-concept
- **Hands**: What do the hands do when idle? Clenched fists, fidgeting fingers, still clasps -- hands betray inner states
- **Eyes**: Not color (who notices their own eye color?) but behavior -- do they make eye contact or avoid it? Hold a gaze or look away?
- **Movement quality**: Decisive or hesitant? Graceful or clumsy? Fast or deliberate? Movement reveals temperament
- **Relationship to space**: Do they touch things? Keep hands in pockets? Lean on walls? Sit on the edge of chairs?

### Physical Tells for Emotional States

| Emotion | Physical Manifestation |
|---------|----------------------|
| Anxiety | Touching face/neck, shifting weight, shallow breathing, checking exits |
| Anger | Jaw clenching, stillness before explosion, hands behind back, temperature flush |
| Grief | Stillness, mechanical movement, touching objects of the lost person, forgetting mid-action |
| Love | Unconscious mirroring, leaning in, touching own lips, losing track of surroundings |
| Fear | Frozen, hyperalert, making themselves small, protective gestures over vulnerable areas |
| Shame | Breaking eye contact, covering face, hunching, physically retreating, going quiet |
